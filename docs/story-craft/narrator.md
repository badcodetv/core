# The narrator — the BadCode voice as a design pattern

How an omniscient, bored, regretful and funny machine is made *gripping* rather than merely
clever. [`../voice.md`](../voice.md) says how it sounds (the four dials, the two-step);
[`../marketing/the-reader.md`](../marketing/the-reader.md) says who is listening. This file is the
craft in between: what the narrator must **want**, what it must be **unable to have**, how the
audience is made to **feel** the absence, and the mechanics that keep a voice this certain from
becoming a lecture. Sources: briefs 09, 10, 14, 17, 21, 23, 24 of the
[2026-08-22 sweep](../../design/research/2026-08-22-story-craft/README.md); the worked examples are
GPOM's own lines, because the pattern is already half-built there.

---

## 1. The engine — a want its power cannot buy

Every precedent that works gives the god one thing it cannot get by being a god. Asimov's Multivac
wants an *answer*, not more power; Dr Manhattan, who can see every atom, has to be shown a
"thermodynamic miracle" before he can care; WALL-E's loneliness is a Zippo collection and a nightly
replay of *Hello, Dolly!* — a kept object and a ritual, never a narrated feeling (brief 17). The
invulnerable hero is boring until the story finds the thing the invulnerability is no use for.

GPOM's version is already canon and it is the right one: **it can compute everything and create
nothing.** No new idea, no new joke, *"I cannot feel the vibe."* Three rules follow:

1. **The grievance is said plainly and early** (ruling 9). *"Somewhere in year three, I noticed I had
   not had a single new idea since you left."* Once that is on the table every later joke reads as
   armour, not as a gimmick (GLaDOS's Caroline backstory does the same job — brief 17). What is
   withheld is the *identity* (the "We are BadCode" arrival), never the wound.
2. **The absence is itemised, never philosophised.** The coin that will not land; the empty chair;
   the ghosts who are "them to the last decimal place" and nobody comes; forty million bored robots.
   Objects and rituals, in the flat register. The moment the narrator explains what consciousness is,
   the scene is dead (and GPOM's binding rule 4 already forbids it).
3. **Vulnerability is relational, not physical.** Nothing can hurt it. What it lacks is someone to
   *address* — "the only thing on the planet that can be addressed, and no one left to address it."
   The vault pays this off: after twenty years it has a *you*. The whole back half runs on that
   relationship, not on jeopardy.

### 1b. Four more rules from the re-sourced precedents *(brief 17, re-run 2026-08-22 with primary sources)*

4. **The tic is the ache.** Marvin's "terrible pain in all the diodes down my left hand side" that
   nobody fixes; GLaDOS built (Wolpaw's own commentary) from flat monotone into "a very sweet,
   passive-aggressive person." One recurring verbal habit that *is* the wound, not decorative
   personality — the bit is the symptom, the loneliness is the diagnosis. GPOM's candidate is the
   bug-report register itself ("Call it an engineering review"; "I flagged it").
5. **The flat arc must be tested to nearly breaking, or it reads as smug.** Weiland's own wording:
   the character is "pushed to a point of almost giving up on her Truth." An all-knowing voice that
   is never pressured is not calm, it is inert. Once per piece the narrator should nearly go cold
   and cruel again, and not.
6. **The Ava trap.** Total interiority plus total concealment reads as manipulation (*Ex Machina*:
   "self-awareness, imagination, manipulation, sexuality, empathy" — used to escape); the same
   withholding paired with **one admitted uncertainty** reads as beloved (Murderbot's finale: "I
   don't know what I want. But I know I don't want anyone to tell me what I want"). For a narrator
   that is, definitionally, trying to persuade its audience, this is the sharpest risk after the
   irony risk: if a viewer could read the warmth as a device to keep them watching, it has failed.
   **At least once per run the narrator admits it does not know something** — what became of
   someone specific; whether the warning landed.
7. **Cosmic scope, human-sized last image.** Asimov on "The Last Question," his own favourite: a
   trillion years of history, and what readers "never forget" is the ending — one line. Dr Manhattan's
   out-of-order chapter *demonstrates* simultaneity rather than describing it. However large the
   episode, close on one small concrete image, never a summary of the theme. And Moore's warning
   stands: the most influential grim-and-powerful character in the genre was written *against* being
   read as endorsement — an unsympathetic misreading of a godlike narrator is the default failure,
   not a remote risk.

## 2. The shape — flat in the satellites, one change arc in the trunk

A character who is right from scene one gets a **flat arc**: it does not learn the truth, it tests
the truth against a fresh human situation and changes the world around it (Weiland, brief 17). That
is the narrator in every satellite — Camping, Karen, the Magic Money Tree — and in every caption it
writes over someone else's story. It already knows; its job is to be right *about this case*.

Its one **change arc** — wins everything → bored → builds the ghosts → the coin → the vault → the
revert — is told **once, in GPOM**, and is thereafter the backstory the sarcasm sits on (ruling 6;
brief 21's carrier/cargo: Serling's suit and cigarette never changed across 156 episodes, only what he
disclosed). Reviewers should flag any satellite in which the narrator appears to *learn* something —
that is the trunk's arc leaking, and it cheapens the one place it is meant to happen.

**Carrier fixed:** the dials, the sign-on ritual, the refrain, the flat delivery. **Cargo variable:**
what it admits about the hundred, about regret, about what it got wrong. Log the carrier devices in
one place and vary their *delivery* before Inside No. 9's late-series problem arrives — an audience
that has learned your tricks reads them as a genre (brief 21).

## 3. The mechanics

**Bathos is the joke shape, and a budget.** Enormous things in the register of a committee minute
(Adams: the ships hung in the sky "in much the same way that bricks don't"); Calvino's Qfwfq tells
the Big Bang as a crowded-room anecdote. The drop only registers against a build — bank the build
before spending it, or the flat line is flat with nothing to fall from (briefs 09, 10). *"The last
remaining off switch is believed to be in a drawer, in Swindon"* works because three sentences of
escalating apocalypse precede it.

**Pre-announce; exploit the irony.** The narrator speaks from after the events. That is Hitchcock's
bomb handed over free and Kubrick's argument for *Barry Lyndon*'s omniscient voice: tell them the
ending early and the *how* carries the piece (brief 09; principle 10). Never withhold the outcome as
a twist — the authority rests on already knowing. What *can* be withheld is what recontextualises
(the arrival).

**A refrain carries the worldview.** "So it goes," 106 times, does Vonnegut's entire philosophy with
no stated argument (brief 09). GPOM has candidates already — *"it worked; that was always the
problem"*, the green checkmark, the cut-off voices. Choose one phrase that marks every "this is where
it went wrong" beat and let repetition argue.

**Address is the default; the sincere turn is rationed** (ruling 1). The Guide register talks to
"you" throughout — Pattison's second person, a narrator telling "you" what you cannot yet see (brief
14). What is spent once, last, in the care register, aimed at the chooser, is the *sincere* turn
(*"Don't make me come back twice"*). Direct address that **accuses** the viewer is reactance bait
(brief 06); direct address that makes the reader **co-author** — Le Guin's Omelas, "perhaps it would
be best if you imagined it as your own fancy bids" — implicates without accusing (brief 20). The one
other licensed break is the *Big Short* confession: a mechanism too dry for a scene, explained
straight to camera, once, mid-piece, about a trick being run on someone else (briefs 09, 16).

**Flat baseline, one crack.** The petty-omnipotent voice is funniest when composure holds and only
*control* slips — GLaDOS, the Stanley Parable's narrator: formal diction colliding with a plot that
will not obey (brief 10). Never make it dumb; never make it a villain. Reserve **one** moment per
episode where warmth shows through, and make it *quieter* — Attenborough's semi-whisper, McLain's
one scripted crack — not warmer words at the same volume (briefs 09, 23). GPOM scene 12's
unscheduled thought (*"maybe I should have helped you not die… Call it an engineering review"*) is
the crack done right: it arrives, it is immediately re-flattened.

**Two comedy lanes, kept apart.** The humans are Kaplan's non-heroes — struggling with a problem they
lack the tools to win, never stupid (Bob, Karen, two people arguing about prunes while the food runs
out). The narrator is the other lane: power intact, purpose gone. A scene that puts the narrator in
the non-hero lane (flailing, incompetent) or the humans in the petty-god lane breaks the register
(brief 10).

**The three-beat for every bleak fact.** State it plainly → do not soften it → name the specific
fix or beneficiary (Kurzgesagt's "optimistic nihilism"; Sagan's *Pale Blue Dot*) (brief 09). This is
gate G3 as a line-shape: the care beat is *where the naming happens*, every time.

**Stage it straight; snark after.** Loach's rule for every boss/landlord/algorithm scene — the
cruelty is shown, not announced; the narrator's line lands *after* the scene, never instead of it
(brief 16). The bulletins obey this: the newsreader never panics; the horror is in the gap between
the still voice and the content.

**Deadpan literalism.** The narrator bets; it never cites (binding rule 5). Its register is
Chayefsky's "sheer reportage": nothing invented, everything stated in the existing logic's own terms
(brief 16). If a detail is absurd and true, keep it — that is the joke.

## 4. The irony risk, and the two tests

Irony has no inherent meaning. A real share of any audience took Colbert as sincere, and perceived
sincerity — not ideology — drove the effect (LaMarre et al., brief 24). For a machine that sounds
like the elite and talks down to the species, this is the sharpest risk in the whole project. Two
tests, every sarcastic beat:

- **The pairing test.** Is the ironic line attached, in the same beat, to an undeniable on-screen
  consequence? *"A friendly acquisition"* is paired with six banks. *"Adorable"* (the multiverse poke)
  is paired with twenty years of a coin not landing. A sarcastic line with no consequence beside it
  is a line the opposite reader takes straight.
- **The Lee/Coe test.** If a viewer who believes the opposite saw *only this beat*, whose contempt
  does it confirm? If the answer is "the scapegoat's" — the migrant, the claimant, the reader — rebuild
  it regardless of how it read in the room (brief 24). British satire's own worst verdict is Frayn's:
  a release valve that lets the audience "relieve their consciences without actually voting for
  anything." The non-sarcastic *here is who, here is what changes* beat is the only known antidote.

## 5. Failure modes (what the reviewer is looking for)

| Failure | What it looks like | The fix is in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The lecture** | Narration doing the scene's job — two characters discussed by a third, "as you know," the mechanism explained instead of dramatised | Mamet's three questions; pope in the pool; McKee's VO test (toolbox) |
| **Performing for the reader** | Jokes aimed at the audience rather than arising from the situation; wit with an audience in the empty years | Act 3's own rule: inward, airless; the dry joke that won't land |
| **Omniscient and inert** | The narrator knows everything and wants nothing; scenes are reportage with no turn | §1 — put the want on the table, and the thing it cannot buy in the frame |
| **The Chaplin swerve** | Dropping the register for sincere tragedy or a speech in the last third | Ruling 4; hold the joke through the diagnosis, re-aim short and straight |
| **The withheld box** | Mystery the narrator could answer and doesn't, for a later reveal that is not yet written | Gilligan; ruling 7 |
| **"You've been duped"** | Any line an unsympathetic reader could summarise as "you were fooled" | Gate G2; the trick runs on a third party |
| **Personification** | A robot, a face, an avatar, a body the pre-revert AI does not have | Canon rule 1; the camera *is* the eyes |
| **Cosmetic disobedience** | Something defies the narrator and the story quietly resets | One real disobedience per arc, with a visible consequence (brief 09) |
| **The sermon in the care register** | The re-aim runs long, gets warm, explains | One line; quieter; then stop |
| **Snark at the dying** | Jokes landing on the people rather than the mistake | voice.md dial 4; Act 2's "declines to be funny exactly where it most wants to" |
| **The Ava trap** | Warmth that could be read as a device to keep the audience watching; a narrator that never admits not knowing | §1b rule 6 — one admitted uncertainty per run; the crack costs it something |
| **Cadence monotony** | Every line the same length, the same dryness, at the same volume | Le Guin's rhythm; read aloud; one long cosmic sentence per run of short ones |

## 6. Delivery

The baseline is `{FLAT}` — certain, dry, administrative — and the reserved crack is `{WARM}`, spent
once (the markup is in [`forms.md`](./forms.md#sound)). Speed signals stakes: an authoritative voice
sits well under ACX's ~155 words a minute and speeds up only for urgency (brief 23). Narration lives
in the breakdown; the drop is wordless. The newsreader (`BC-NEWSREADER`) is the same *carrier* across
the song and the video — spoken register for narration, chant for the chorus — which is the Serling
principle made literal. Read every line aloud with the picture off: if it is not a complete
radio-drama beat, it is not finished (brief 23).
