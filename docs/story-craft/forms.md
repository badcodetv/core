# Forms — the craft that changes with the medium

[`principles.md`](./principles.md) is medium-agnostic; this is what changes when the story has to be
a scroll comic, a 3–5 minute narrated video, a drum & bass track, or a serialised run. It restates no
principle — rules that live there are cited by number — and every claim names its brief
(`design/research/2026-08-22-story-craft/`) with the brief's evidence grade kept. The
[house rulings](./principles.md#house-rulings-on-contested-advice) win any conflict, 7 and 11 above
all: no retention figure, pixel range, drop-off point or "first N seconds" appears here as fact.

---

## 1. The scroll comic

### The unit and the grammar

The unit is the gap: closure — the reader fusing two stills into time — happens in the gutter, and
McCloud calls it comics' grammar (brief 11). His six transition types are the most-taught vocabulary
and the least reliable; Gavaler found readers cannot agree which category a cut belongs to. Edit with
his four **closure types** instead — for any jump, which gap is the reader filling: *spatial, temporal,
causal, associative* — and is that the gap you meant (brief 11)?

On a phone, distance is time — the half of McCloud's infinite canvas that survived; the any-shape page
died of friction, so: vertical only, fixed width (brief 11; principle 28). The next screen does the
page-turn's job: a short scroll reads as a quick reaction, a long one as a held breath, and the panel
before a reveal *leans* toward the scroll with a gaze or a motion line (brief 10). Vertical geometry
is also argument — Bong's crew called *Parasite* "a stair movie" because the class hierarchy is spatial
and never spoken (brief 16); a descending scroll tracks a fall in status with no caption. The
*scroll = collapse* doctrine is ruled in
[`how-we-tell-it.md`](../stories/gitpush-origin-master/how-we-tell-it.md) — apply it, don't restate it.

### Pacing a 20–40 panel narrated comic

Shape, no numbers (ruling 11). The first panel gets splash treatment — one image doing establishing
shot and tonal overture before any caption (brief 05; Eisner's wording unverified beyond a synopsis).
Then hook early, development, a landed final beat — and a **second hook** around a third of the way in,
not only a last-panel turn; the drop-off claim behind it is craft-blog folklore, the shape is kept
(brief 11). Vary gutter distance throughout and reserve one genuinely long blank scroll for the beat
that deserves a held breath — in GPOM, the narrator admitting it won and then got bored (brief 11;
principle 28). The last panel closes the piece's own loop; the open thing is the run's known ending
(ruling 7).

### Word and picture

Principle 25 is the test: delete the caption, and if the reader loses only confirmation of something
visible, cut it. McCloud's taxonomy names the failure — *duo-specific*, both channels saying one thing
— and the target, *interdependent*, neither alone carrying the idea (brief 22, recalled from memory
there and marked unverified).

Caption and balloon are different channels. Eisner: the balloon is "a desperation device" making sound
visible; the caption is the external narrating voice; the reader sorts *who* before *what* by container
and colour, and hand-lettered versus typeset read as different narrators (briefs 11, 22). So the
narrator's captions and any human's balloons are distinguishable before either is read. Inside the
panel is primary; text is a guest, and Eisner's named failure is the writer overwriting in space the
artist allocated (brief 11).

One silent panel per piece (principle 25) — only where the image is unambiguous enough to earn it, or
"trusts you" reads as "forgot" (brief 22).

Generated stills add a rule drawn panels never needed: a drawn panel can be redrawn to add what a
caption needs; a generated image is a locked fact once accepted, with pose and identity drift across a
sequence (brief 22, a house constraint, not a web-sourced claim). **Decide the one fact the image must
nail before generation** — expression, gesture, environment — and write the caption to cover what the
pose range cannot reach, never to paper over drift the sequence should have been regenerated to fix.

### Constraint, colour, blackout

One visible compositional constraint per comic, broken once. Gibbons chose *Watchmen*'s nine-panel grid
for the control it gave; the rigid regularity is what makes a single deviation an event (brief 11). A
rule not ruthless enough for long enough is wallpaper.

Colour is the adjective layer: Ware applies it after the sequence is structurally complete, as an
emotional modifier — and disclaims having a system, so this is one master's intuition, not a portable
rule (brief 11). Our use: grid logic constant, palette temperature saying which timeline the reader is
in — cold future, warm 2026.

For the darkest beat, withhold. *Saga* #42 ends on six blacked-out panels; Vaughan credits Staples's
pacing and handwritten narration, not the surprise (brief 11). A blackout turns the gutter inside out —
the reader infers what happened *inside* a panel that refuses to show it. A story-within-the-story
does the same job for the beneficiary beat (gate G3): *Tales of the Black Freighter* parallels Veidt
without either text naming the other (brief 11).

## 2. The narrated video (3–5 min)

### What it is

Stills or short clips under a voice: *La Jetée*'s photo-roman, the Eameses' *Powers of Ten*, Every
Frame a Painting's single-claim essay, Kurzgesagt's hope-engineered explainer (brief 12). Adam Curtis
is the nearest cousin and the cautionary one: his two tracks — narration with "free-form riffs" over
it — are what Rosenbaum calls Kuleshov-effect persuasion beneath scrutiny, and the critique is
unresolved (brief 12). We run two tracks (picture plus D&B) knowingly; the tripwire is gate G3 — a
riff that lets the beneficiary go ambiguous has become the thing his critics name.

### The shot-and-narration rhythm

Glass's loop: concrete action, step back to a bigger thought, return to action (briefs 12, 23). Action
is where something happens to the human; the bigger thought is where the narrator goes cold and names
the chooser and beneficiary; then back to plot — the repeatable home for the sarcasm/care two-step.
Between shots, *but* or *therefore*, never *and then* (principle 7; Zhou via Welles, brief 12).

The motion budget is one moving shot (principle 26): *La Jetée*'s single dissolve into motion works
because stillness is total elsewhere; a photo-roman that cheats with occasional pans loses the contrast
(brief 12). The signal-degrade — the narrator's own transmission stuttering to static — is the premise
made audible, once per release, never per video (brief 12). Cut density *down* between drafts: the
Eameses went from six simultaneous readouts to "the most essential information" (brief 12).

### The words-to-picture discipline

Write to a words-per-second budget per shot: time each line against its still's real duration before
recording; a line that fits only by speed-reading means the shot is too short or the line is
duo-specific (brief 22). The one sourced ceiling is ACX's roughly 155 words a minute; an authoritative
narrator sits noticeably under it and saves speed to signal stakes (brief 23). McKee's test on every
line — "any idiot can write a voice-over" targets VO that merely captions the picture; keep a line only
if a silent cut would lose real information (brief 09). The caption carries what the picture cannot
(principle 25): foreknowledge, causality across time, the named decision. Counterpoint — narration over
images "that may or may not be related" — reads as intent only against a default of legible sync;
budget it for the beats that land the political point (brief 22).

### Sound

One element leads each moment — voice, music or effects, never all three (Thom's "100% theory", brief
23; principle 27). Sustained intensity is a newspaper printed in capitals; the bed needs slow sections
for the voice to occupy (brief 23). True silence is Murch's budget — three or four per film or it
becomes predictable — and the outgoing line at a scene change is held roughly 30% longer than a
mid-scene cut so it decays rather than clips (brief 23). Sound may lead picture: a sting or line a beat
before its panel does anticipation work hard sync cannot (brief 23). Since the picture is stills, the
script carries what live action spreads across blocking — Crook's "bad lines cannot be obscured with
stagecraft" (brief 23).

The house VO markup is brief 23's deliverable, verbatim:

| Mark | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `/` | Short beat, comma-weight | ~0.2–0.3s, inside a sentence |
| `//` | Full-stop beat | ~0.5–0.8s, between sentences |
| `///` | Held pause — earn it | ~1–2s, reserved for a real turn |
| `[SIL n s]` | True silence, bed and VO both stop | Murch's 3–4-per-film discipline; max 2–3 per episode |
| `[BREAKDOWN]…[/BREAKDOWN]` | VO sits in a half-time/thinned bed section | Default home for plot-carrying narration |
| `[DROP]` | Full-tempo hit, wordless (or one word) | Scene-cut punctuation, not exposition |
| `[DUCK -Xdb]…[/DUCK]` | Music explicitly attenuated under this span | Named level, not "turn it down a bit" |
| `{FLAT}…{/FLAT}` | Baseline register: certain, dry, administrative | Default narrator voice |
| `{WARM}…{/WARM}` | The reserved crack — caring shows through | Spend once per episode |
| `[whispers]` `[sarcastic]` `[laughs]` | Bracketed performance cues (ElevenLabs-style) | Portable straight to TTS |
| CAPS | Hard emphasis, one word only | Never a whole line (Thom's warning) |

How it meets the pipeline: the narrator is a Suno voice, and
[`narration.md`](../stories/gitpush-origin-master/songs/narration.md) §1 rules that Suno cannot hit a
timecode. The take is split to stems and the vocal stem **cut into one clip per line**, each slid onto
its picture beat in Premiere — so every line is a cut point, written short and full-stopped. `/`
directs delivery *inside* a line (v5.5 obeys the bracket architecture; 4.5 shreds it). `//`, `///` and
`[SIL]` are *executed in the edit* — the pause is where the clip is slid, not what the take did.
`[DUCK]` acts on the instrumental stem; `[BREAKDOWN]` and `[DROP]` apply where the bed is a D&B track,
while GPOM's narration bed is ruled "a held note, a room, and two events." Picture is cut to the VO
except for built animations with locked frames, where the words go to the picture (narration.md §2).

### The opening

A promise, not a hook (principle 15): the first 10% tells a stranger what kind of story this is and who
it is about, with no narration dump (brief 05). Open on the protagonist mid-situation (ruling 14) —
in-medias-res fails by starting too *late*, action with nobody to care about (brief 05). Open on a
question, not a thesis, when a mechanism is explained (Kurzgesagt, brief 05). The whole promise — tone,
stakes, who — goes before any table-setting; the MrBeast document is the practitioner evidence, of
unconfirmed provenance, so take the shape and none of its numbers (brief 12). And Welles: *War of the
Worlds*' dread came from the deliberately tedious dance-music stretches *before* the bulletins —
Houseman called the drag "increasingly sinister" (brief 12). A signal-degrade with no boring stretch
first is a gimmick.

### Short-form cutdowns

Script the opening seconds as their own artefact and build backward; never trim the long-form open to
size (brief 12; the platform's own guidance exists, its figure is not cited). Our reader is sound-off
and scroll-first (the-reader.md): the cut lands its promise with no audio, one text overlay per beat,
never stacked (brief 12, a mixed-grade guide citing the platform).

## 3. The drum & bass track

Everything mechanical — style prompts, Voice stacks, syllable matching, rhyme by section, chorus
escalation, punctuation as brake, stems, the session method — lives in
[`docs/suno-gpt/`](../suno-gpt/README.md), chiefly [`lyric-craft.md`](../suno-gpt/files/lyric-craft.md).
This section is narrative-in-lyrics only.

### Narrative in lyrics

Point of view is chosen, not defaulted. Pattison: third person is "basically God"; second person
addresses a "you" the narrator can tell things it does not yet know; direct address is pop's norm with a
named trap — a set of it "becomes static" (brief 14, practitioner-grade). A narrator who knows what
"you" cannot yet know is second-person by structure, so that is the default; the sincere direct turn
stays rationed (ruling 1).

Before bars, the drama in one sentence — Skinner took McKee's course and kept "every song needs a drama
at the centre of it" — checked for a chooser and a beneficiary, not a mood (brief 14; gate G3). Then
*comment, don't describe*: his songs work like noir, through suggestion, so plot cannot be predicted
from lyrics alone — a three-minute story's main defence against sermon (brief 14).

A second voice carries irony the narrator cannot state: "Stan"'s Dido chorus stays warm while the
letters darken, and the last verse flips voice, realising too late (brief 14 — listicle-grade
structural description, no Eminem quote found). Displacement solves the sarcasm/care two-step: Dammers
wrote the Specials' break-up as "Ghost Town" because he "didn't want to write about my state of mind"
— private grief for charge, observed detail for the political image (brief 14). One concrete gesture
over a stated feeling — Springsteen's "twisting of a ring, the twirling of a baton" — and if a wrongdoer
speaks first-person, withhold judgement and let consequence or a second voice anchor the verdict (brief
14; that caveat is the brief's own synthesis, flagged as such).

The flat non-singing interlude is the home for the plainest political line: "Fitter Happier" is a
text-to-speech checklist Yorke could not deliver sung; "Fight the Power" opens on a speech sample before
any music (brief 14). Stripping melody resets the guard against "message" — which is why the
beneficiary-naming beat can live there, and why it is rare. Name institutions and mechanisms in their
checkable acts, never a living private individual (ruling 13; Chuck D, brief 14). The dual ending is a
ready-made signature — *A Grand Don't Come for Free* offers a bitter close and a redemptive one; every
BadCode piece already holds "don't make this mistake" and "you are loved, we can fix it" (brief 14).

### Where the voice sits on the bed

The genre answers it: the drop follows a build and a breakdown where drums thin — so narration lives in
the breakdown and the drop is wordless punctuation (principle 27). Old-school D&B hosted an MC, a
practice since declined (brief 23). Grade honestly: drop/breakdown structure and the MC tradition are
Wikipedia-grade; producer-level mechanics for a half-time vocal over a 170-plus-BPM bed are
(unverified) — brief 23 found no practitioner source.

### The concept-EP spine

One concrete, losable object, never a theme: *A Grand Don't Come for Free* turns on a literal missing
£1,000; *good kid, m.A.A.d city* threads a non-linear timeline through a borrowed van and a voicemail
that acts as a timestamp (brief 13; principle 2). Theme alone makes a mood board with a shared subject.

## 4. The serialised run

### Trunk and satellites

GPOM is the trunk; Camping, Karen and the Magic Money Tree hang off it. Schur's fix for a concept that
"burns off really quickly": fast plot over a slow engine that cannot finish early (brief 13). Each
satellite is fast plot with its own losable object; the narrator's engine never resolves inside any one
story. The run's theme is one clause, stated once, and each satellite supplies a *new facet* — Rogers
built a Leverage season on the word "Consequences" and gave every character a different inflection
rather than proving it weekly (brief 21). A pitch that restates a covered facet is cut.

Cross-story tissue is texture, never a gate: Brooker's recurring ticker, symbol and song are "an extra
bit of texture for fans," against the shared-universe failure where entry starts to *require* prior
viewing (brief 13; the Feige "homework" attribution is under-sourced there). Release the trunk's
*frame* first — premise and authority — and let its ending lag; satellites borrow only the opening
premise (brief 21). Decide closed-versus-serialised *in writing* per release wave: Rogers closed
Leverage's first season "in case we never made another," and the same post calls it safe and
self-inflicted damage (brief 21).

Canon is provisional, Lindelof-style — "it's going to be that unless someone can beat it" — committed
only as far as the next piece needs (brief 21). The honesty cost is ours specifically:
Grillo-Marxuach's warning against later claiming "we knew all along" lands harder on a narrator whose
authority is "I already lived this." Fix the ending before publicising the mystery (principle 3); teaser
copy promises only what canon can answer (brief 13).

### The recurring narrator

Carrier fixed, cargo variable (principle 24). Serling's identical bearing — "board-straight in a fitted
suit, cigarette in hand" — was the only connective tissue across an anthology with no returning
characters; episode ninety was trusted because the ritual matched episode three (brief 21). The shelf
life is Pemberton's: by Inside No. 9's late series audiences were "eagle-eyed" for the writers' own
tricks (brief 21). Log the recurring devices in one document and vary the delivery while the rule stays
fixed. Full pattern: [`narrator.md`](./narrator.md).

### Leaving things open

Anything left open passes Dickens's test — built to survive the real gap between releases, not a binge
(brief 13; ruling 7).
And Gilligan: "mystery is almost always good; confusion is always bad" — withhold only what the reader
can reconstruct (brief 13; principle 12). Run it on every narrator aside: does the dry line skip a step
the reader must be *told*, or one they can *complete*?

### The B-story and the doors

The B-story is the A-story's negative space — Espenson: "often the more emotional, more internal story,"
the theme in a minor key (brief 21). When the A-thread is sarcastic macro-history, a quiet B-thread on
one minor human whose interior is never narrated is where perspective-getting lives.

The ARG lesson at our scale: *Year Zero* seeded one story into a T-shirt, a USB stick and a
thermochromic CD so any single artefact was a complete door in (brief 13). The repository artwork, a
commit message that reads in-universe — each one of several doors, optional, never required to follow
the comic or the video.

## 5. Cross-form scarcity budgets

Spend any of these everywhere and it lands nowhere (principle 26).

| Device | Budget | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moving shot in a stills video | One per video | *La Jetée* — brief 12 |
| True silence (bed and VO stop) | Three or four per film; two or three per episode | Murch — brief 23 |
| Signal-degrade | One per release, not per video | brief 12 |
| Fake-bulletin / interruption trick | One per surprise, after a dull stretch | Morris; Welles/Houseman — briefs 10, 12 |
| Sincere direct-address turn | Once, last, care register, aimed at the chooser | ruling 1; briefs 06, 08 |

| Blackout panel | The darkest beat only | *Saga* — brief 11 |
