## The facts (checked 2026-11-21)

**Switzerland: people vote on single national questions, several times a year.**
- About four federal voting days a year; since 1991 close to 10 individual national questions a year (1848-1900:
  about one a year). ([swissinfo: how direct democracy has grown](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/politics/how-direct-democracy-has-grown-over-the-decades/41481992),
  [swissinfo: how it works](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/swiss-democracy/how-swiss-direct-democracy-works/89073820))
- How it grew: 1848, a referendum is needed to change the constitution; 1874, people can force a referendum on a
  new law; 1891, people can propose their own change to the constitution (the popular initiative).
  ([Swiss government: the way to modern direct democracy](https://www.aboutswitzerland.eda.admin.ch/en/the-way-to-modern-direct-democracy-in-switzerland))
- Most citizens' own proposals fail (about 11% accepted since 1848), so it isn't chaos.
- 🔴 **The honest downside:** the 2009 vote banning new minarets passed (about 58%) after parliament had rejected
  it. Critics say it broke the constitution's guarantee of religious freedom. Anyone attacking the short will raise
  this; Ireland's model (below) is the answer to it.
  ([Migration Policy Institute](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/swiss-vote-ban-minarets-sparking-international-criticism),
  [EJIL: Talk!](https://www.ejiltalk.org/the-swiss-referendum-on-the-prohibition-of-minarets/))

**Ireland: ordinary people, picked at random, settled questions politicians wouldn't touch. Twice.**
- **Abortion:** the Citizens' Assembly, 99 people picked at random to reflect the country, met over five weekends
  (Nov 2016 to Apr 2017) and recommended repealing the Eighth Amendment. A parliamentary committee followed it; the
  referendum on 25 May 2018 passed 66.4% to 33.6% on a 64% turnout.
  ([Electoral Reform Society](https://www.electoral-reform.org.uk/?p=1873),
  [RTÉ results](https://www.rte.ie/news/newslens/2018/0526/966234-referendum-results-at-a-glance),
  [Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/web/bioethics/-/ireland-public-debate-concerning-abortion-and-the-repeal-of-the-eighth-amendment-to-the-constitution))
- **Same-sex marriage:** the Constitutional Convention (100 members; Claude's memory says 66 were random citizens,
  🔴 not confirmed) voted 79% in April 2013 to recommend it; the referendum on 22 May 2015 passed with 62% on a 61%
  turnout, the first country to bring it in by popular vote.
  ([Convention reports](https://citizensassembly.ie/overview-previous-assemblies/2013-2014-convention-on-the-constitution/reports-recommendations/),
  [Washington Blade](https://www.washingtonblade.com/?p=64951))

**Taiwan: thousands of people online found what they agreed on, and the government made it law.**
- On Uber, 2015: a four-week online consultation (vTaiwan, on a tool called Pol.is; 3,000 to 4,000+ took part,
  sources differ) started as two camps and found points both sides agreed on. The government agreed in May 2016 to
  adopt them (e.g. app cars can't pick up on the street; Uber pays tax and is regulated).
  ([CompDemocracy case study](https://compdemocracy.org/Case-studies/2014-vTaiwan/),
  [Shareable](https://www.shareable.net/open-data-hactivists-help-taipei-craft-regulatory-response-to-uber/))
- The twist worth using: the tool shows people what they agree on, not what they fight about.
- 🔴 The arguments didn't stop: Uber drivers were still being fined afterwards.

## Corrections to the first draft

- "Switzerland's been doing it since 1848" is only half right: 1848 was constitution changes only. Votes on
  ordinary laws came in 1874; people's own proposals in 1891. Safe line: "since the 1800s".
- Ireland did it twice, not once: same-sex marriage (2015) as well as abortion (2018).

## Why it isn't moany

The way out is real and already working. Three countries, three methods: vote on each question (Switzerland), let a
random jury of ordinary people work it out (Ireland), find what everyone agrees on (Taiwan). Each answers a different
fear: the Swiss one is "will people vote for anything?", the Irish one is "won't it just be the loudest?", the
Taiwanese one is "won't it just be a fight?"

## More examples (checked 2026-11-21)

**Proof the package doesn't fit: people split their own ticket when they're allowed to**
- **Florida, Nov 2020:** the same voters, on the same ballot, backed Trump and voted 60.8% for a $15 minimum wage
  (it needed 60%), a policy his party opposed. ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Florida_$15_Minimum_Wage_Initiative_(2020)),
  [Marketplace](https://marketplace.org/2020/11/04/florida-voters-approve-15-an-hour-minimum-wage))
- **Republican US states voted for free healthcare for the poor (Medicaid expansion)** over their own leaders'
  objections: Idaho 60%, Nebraska 54%, Utah 53% (2018); Missouri 53%, Oklahoma 50.5% (2020).
  ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/11/07/utah-idaho-and-nebraska-approve-medicaid-expansion-ballot-measures.html),
  [Georgetown CCF](https://ccf.georgetown.edu/2020/08/05/missouri-voters-approve-medicaid-expansion/),
  [Commonwealth Fund](https://commonwealthfund.org/blog/2020/oklahoma-voters-approve-expanding-medicaid-eligibility-ballot-measure))
  🔴 Utah's lawmakers later swapped it for a smaller plan: the vote was watered down.

**Ordinary people decide the money directly**
- **Porto Alegre, Brazil, from 1989:** residents in public meetings decided how part of the city budget was spent,
  growing to about half of it by the late 1990s; tens of thousands took part. 🔴 Suspended in 2017 by a later
  government. ([WRI](https://www.wri.org/insights/what-if-citizens-set-city-budgets-experiment-captivated-world-participatory-budgeting),
  [Lincoln Institute](https://www.lincolninst.edu/node/43426))
- **Paris, from 2014:** 5% of the city's investment budget, about €100 million a year, goes to projects residents
  propose and vote on; anyone living there aged 7+ can vote, whatever their nationality. About 210,000 voted in 2018.
  ([Paris city hall](https://www.paris.fr/pages/budget-participatif-3980),
  [Banque des Territoires](https://www.banquedesterritoires.fr/la-mairie-de-paris-debloque-100-millions-deuros-par-pour-son-budget-participatif),
  [CS Monitor](https://csmonitor.com/World/Europe/2015/0731/How-should-Paris-spend-its-budget-Locals-now-get-to-choose))

**Random citizens, made permanent**
- **East Belgium (Ostbelgien), from 2019:** the first region with a permanent citizens' council by law: 24 people
  picked at random choose the topics, then assemblies of up to 50 random citizens work on them (so far health care,
  education, affordable housing), and parliament must debate and answer. ([newDemocracy](https://www.newdemocracy.com.au/2019/02/25/ostbelgien-model-belgium/),
  [Bertelsmann Stiftung](https://www.bertelsmann-stiftung.de/en/our-projects/democracy-and-participation-in-europe/shortcut-archive/shortcut-7-the-ostbelgien-model))
- **The UK has done it once:** Climate Assembly UK (2020), 108 people picked by lottery, commissioned by six Commons
  committees, six weekends; advice only. ([Climate Assembly UK](https://www.climateassembly.uk/report/read/executive-summary.html),
  [Carbon Brief](https://carbonbrief.org/qa-how-the-climate-assembly-says-the-uk-should-reach-net-zero))

**Where politicians took it back (the honest downside, and maybe the joke)**
- **France's climate convention (2019-20):** 150 random citizens wrote 149 proposals; Macron promised 146; by most
  counts only about 10% went into law unchanged. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Citizens_Convention_for_Climate),
  [Mehr Demokratie](https://www.mehr-demokratie.de/en/mehr-wissen/citizens-assemblies/news/details-view/french-climate-assembly-adopts-recommendations))
- **Iceland's crowdsourced constitution:** 25 elected citizens drafted it in 2011 with public input online; 67% backed
  it in the 2012 referendum (49% turnout); parliament shelved it and it was never adopted.
  ([openDemocracy](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/democracy-on-ice-post-mortem-of-icelandic-constitution/),
  [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/putsch-icelands-crowd-sourced-constitution-killed-by-parliament-2/))

**What the pattern says (Claude's read):** when people get to choose one question at a time, they don't vote along
party lines, and the ideas often hold up. When it fails, it's usually because politicians took the decision back
(France, Iceland, Utah). That could be the short's twist: the problem isn't people choosing, it's people being allowed
to.

## The biggest impact (checked 2026-11-21 unless marked)

When people decided one question themselves, these changed the most:

- **Northern Ireland, 1998: they voted for peace.** The Good Friday Agreement went to the people: 71.1% yes in
  Northern Ireland (81% turnout) and 94.4% in the Republic, the same day. The vote, not just the politicians,
  ended the Troubles' 30 years. ([ARK](https://ark.ac.uk/elections/fref98.htm), [CAIN](https://cain.ulster.ac.uk/issues/politics/election/ref1998.htm))
- **South Africa, 1992: white voters voted to end apartheid.** 68.7% yes on an 85% turnout, after de Klerk said he'd
  resign if he lost. "Today we have closed the book on apartheid." 🔴 Only white people could vote in it, which is
  the point and the irony. ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/africa/south-africa-the-remarkable-referendum-of-1992-1.1994113),
  [SAHistory](https://www.sahistory.org.za/node/13012))
- **New Zealand, 1993: the people changed how government works.** They voted out first-past-the-post for
  proportional representation (53.9%, 85% turnout), after 70.5% picked it in a first vote in 1992. Every election
  since has been proportional. This is the exact BadCode point: you can change how government works, not just who.
  ([Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/graph/35700/results-of-the-1993-referendum-on-the-electoral-system),
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_New_Zealand_electoral_reform_referendum))
- **Australia, 1967: 90.77% yes**, the highest ever, to let the national government make laws for Aboriginal people
  and count them in the census. 🔴 It did not give them the vote (a common myth). ([National Archives of Australia](https://www.naa.gov.au/explore-collection/first-australians/other-resources-about-first-australians/1967-referendum))
- **Uruguay, 2004: water made a human right.** Started by a citizens' coalition, passed with 64.6%: water and
  sanitation must be run by the state, and people come before profit. The first country to put it in its
  constitution. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Uruguayan_constitutional_referendum),
  [TNI](https://tni.org/files/wateruruguayrev.pdf))
- **Brazil: towns where people set the budget lost fewer babies.** Places using participatory budgeting spent more
  on sanitation and health, and infant deaths fell by about 1-2 per 1,000 (5-10%), more the longer it ran.
  (Gonçalves, *World Development* 2014; Touchton and Wampler 2020.) ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/wdevel/v53y2014icp94-110.html),
  [People Powered](https://www.peoplepowered.org/resources-content/effects-pb-municipal-expenditures-infant-mortality))
- **Ireland, 2015 and 2018** (above): random citizens unlocked same-sex marriage and abortion reform.

Big, and the honest other side (from Claude's memory, not re-checked this session):
- **Brexit, 2016:** 51.9% to 48.1% to leave the EU. The biggest single choice the UK public has made. The
  counter-argument everyone will raise: a yes/no on a huge question, with no detail, split the country. Ireland's
  route (a random jury that hears the evidence first, then the vote) is the answer to it.
- **Switzerland, 1971:** women got the national vote only when Swiss men voted for it; one canton held out until a
  court forced it in 1990. Direct democracy is only as good as who's allowed in the room.
- **Chile, 2020-23:** 78% voted to write a new constitution, then rejected both drafts. People can say no to the
  result as well as yes to the idea.
