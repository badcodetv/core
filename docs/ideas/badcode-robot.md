# The BadCode Robot

Build the BadCode robot — the superintelligence from the future, sent back in the cheapest
body we can get away with — out of open-source bits and pieces that a robot could very
easily manufacture. Then make the *building of it* the content.

The instinct: **let's make the BadCode robot in as cheap of parts as we could.** Off-the-shelf
electronics, open-source designs, no proprietary anything — the kind of thing a robot arm
could assemble from a bin of parts. The build doubles as an **electronics lesson**: every
component chosen is a chance to explain what it does and why it costs what it costs. Educational
stuff around it, presented straight, in the BadCode voice. That would make for some
interesting videos.

The joke writes itself: the machine that ended humanity, reduced to a pile of hobbyist parts
and a soldering iron, explaining resistors to you because it regrets everything.

**Parked as a plant for later.** The next step is *research*, not building: work out the
necessary components for a cheap, open-source, educational hardware project — what a
minimum-viable BadCode robot actually needs, what it costs, and what the lesson plan looks
like. No canon, no story yet.

*Story or claim?* Neither yet — it is a **format** (a build series). The stake, if it grows one,
is in the parts list: who makes them, who owns the designs, and what "cheap" reveals about
where the money in hardware actually goes.

## Format thoughts

A **video series** — the build itself, one component or lesson per episode. Hardware and
software both live in the repo (the parts list, the firmware, the lesson notes). Not a
comic, at least not to start.

## Where it goes: the robot dances

*(Added 2026-09-11 — a moonshot thrown into the air as it came. Kept as said.)*

The obvious next step once the thing stands up: **give it a tune and it dances.** Load audio
from our own music, coordinate dance moves to it — *the robot performs a tune.* Then the
product line writes itself: we sell the robot with the **BadCode original loaded as the
tune it ships with**, and the whole point is that **you can give it tunes you just made.**
That is what AI music is *for* — everyone can make a track now, so everyone can have a
robot that dances to theirs.

Two ways to own one: **DIY, build it yourself out of electronics** from the open-source
parts list, or **buy a kit**, maybe. Either way the machine is the same and the content is
the same.

But the *beginning* is not a product. **To begin with it is educational content: how to
build cool robots that just dance to any given music** — and where you can coordinate the
dance moves yourself, not just watch it wobble to a beat.

Then the escalation. If a **Raspberry Pi** can be part of the setup, then **OpenCV** can be
part of the setup, and now maybe we do **limb analysis** — a camera watches a human dance,
and the robot does what the human wants it to do. You teach it the moves by doing them.

Kai's own note on all this: *"a crazy moonshot idea"* — but it *"sounds like a really cool
electronics project to do with a friend"*, and that was enough to flip it from "parked plant"
to *"let's do that."* There is a friend in mind.

Media grows with it: **video** (the build series), **software** (firmware, the
choreography, the OpenCV pipeline — all in the repo), and now **music** (the shipped tune,
and the invitation to replace it).
