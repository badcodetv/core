# Political photomontage

> Photographs sliced and recombined into hard-edged images that make an accusation in a single frame.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A tradition from Dada and the 1930s in which press photographs are cut apart and rejoined to expose what the originals hide. John Heartfield's anti-Nazi covers made montage a weapon; Peter Kennard carried it through British anti-nuclear and anti-austerity protest. Images are monochrome or limited in colour, with razor-edged cuts, scale shifts and one bold idea. Often paired with a blunt caption.

## Signature traits

- Halftone newsprint grain and high-contrast black and white, sometimes a single spot red
- Sharp scissor-cut edges, no feathering, visible seams
- Scale clash: a tiny crowd under a giant hand or coin
- Single central metaphor per image, centred and legible at thumbnail size
- Newspaper-style captions in heavy sans or slab serif, often cropped
- Flat, frontal, poster-like composition with minimal depth
- Documentary photographs as source material, not illustrations

## References

- *Adolf the Superman: Swallows Gold and Spouts Tin* — John Heartfield, 1932. Classic montage poster: an X-ray of Hitler swallowing coins; the idea reads in one glance.
- *Haywain with Cruise Missiles* — Peter Kennard, 1980. British protest montage placing missiles on Constable's The Hay Wain; the defining UK reference.
- *Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada Through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch in Germany* — Hannah Hoch, 1919-1920. Dada montage showing the dense collage tradition that preceded agitprop.
- *Orgasm Addict sleeve (Buzzcocks single)* — Linder Sterling, 1977. British punk montage of advert and body fragments, turning the form on consumption and class.

## What it carries

Angry, accusatory, urgent; the visual equivalent of a shout. It carries righteous fury and clarity. It fights nuance, warmth and ambiguity, and can read as preaching.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Single-image stings: poster frames, title cards, thumbnails, cutaways inside a longer film to nail an economic metaphor (a coin crushing a town). Good for a punchy opening or closing frame. Undermines anything that must not sermonise: sustained use becomes lecturing, which BadCode wants to avoid.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana produces strong montage stills with: 'photomontage, 1930s agitprop poster, halftone newsprint grain, black and white with a single red element, hard scissor-cut edges, flat frontal composition, one central metaphor'. Spell out the two source images being joined and the scale clash. Anonymous crowds are fine; avoid asking for named public figures. Veo should animate minimally: 'static montage, slow push-in, subtle paper flicker, one element slides in'. Negative: 'smooth blend, photorealistic composite, soft edges, gradient, 3D'.

## How generators get it wrong

The image model blends elements seamlessly like a Photoshop composite, removing the cut-and-paste honesty; insist on visible edges and halftone. It over-details and over-lights, giving glossy poster art rather than newsprint. Typography is often garbled, so add captions in the edit. Veo tends to warp and breathe the whole image; ask for a locked frame with only one piece moving.

## Production cost

Stills are easy and consistent because each image stands alone; no character continuity needed. Motion is the cost: long animation ruins it, so use held frames with Premiere moves. Poor for sustained narrative.
