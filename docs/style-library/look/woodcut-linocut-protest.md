# Woodcut / linocut protest print

> Carved-block black-and-white (or one spot colour) imagery with gouged white lines, in the tradition of the wordless woodcut novel and the labour-movement print.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Relief printing from carved wood or lino, dominant in German Expressionist and Belgian social-realist printmaking after WWI and in American Depression-era wordless novels. Every mark is a cut, so shapes are bold, lines taper and there is almost no mid-grey: light and dark are fought over directly. It carries a century of association with strikes, famine and war, which gives it moral weight before any narration arrives.

## Signature traits

- Pure black ink on off-white paper, optionally one flat spot colour (red or ochre) misregistered slightly
- No continuous tone: shading is made of parallel gouge lines, cross-hatch and stippled cut marks
- Thick, slightly wobbly outlines; shapes with visible chisel-edge corners
- Hard, high-contrast silhouettes; figures as masses of black with white eyes and hands
- Heavy crowd scenes in tight stacked compositions, little empty space
- Subtle paper grain and uneven ink coverage (flecks of unprinted paper in black areas)
- Square or near-square frame typical of the woodcut novel plate

## References

- *The Idea (L'Idée)* — Frans Masereel, 1920. Wordless woodcut novel; a pure idea is persecuted by authority and the crowd, the model for sequential protest imagery.
- *Gods' Man* — Lynd Ward, 1929. American wordless woodcut novel; a bargain with money/power told only in images.
- *War (Krieg) woodcut cycle* — Käthe Kollwitz, 1922-23. Seven woodcuts of grief and mass loss; the benchmark for human weight in black and white.
- *Passionate Journey* — Frans Masereel, 1919. Wordless woodcut novel of working-class city life, with hundreds of plates in one consistent hand.

## What it carries

Solemn, urgent, righteous, tragic. It can carry grief and anger very well and a bitter dry joke if the composition does the punchline. It fights against light comedy, glossy optimism and anything that needs facial nuance.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about workers, debt, eviction, hunger, strikes, who owns what. A black-and-white woodcut is a good fit for 'the machine eats people' tragedy and for a narrator who speaks with grave authority. It would undermine slapstick, cute characters, tech-satire that depends on shiny devices, or any joke needing colour to land.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana responds well to naming the medium and the process: 'hand-carved linocut print, black ink on cream paper, visible gouge marks, no gradients, no shading except parallel cut lines, bold outlines, slight ink unevenness'. Specifying the exact ink count ('one colour: black; plus flat brick red spot colour, slightly offset') keeps the palette locked. Put the composition in the frame explicitly (crowd, silhouette, high horizon). Exclude: 'photograph, gradient, soft shadows, 3D render, glossy, painterly, digital airbrush, colour, text'. For Veo image-to-video, ask for 'locked-off camera, only the ink-black shapes shift slightly, like cut paper stop-motion' and expect that you will often get camera drift or shading creeping in.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators produce 'digital woodcut' that looks like a vector filter: clean uniform stroke width, smooth gradients hidden inside hatching, and fake texture overlay. The fix is to demand tapering cut marks and uneven ink, and to reject any plate with gradient skies or airbrushed skin. Faces drift into photographic realism and hatching becomes decorative rather than describing form. Text in the frame is usually garbled, so keep it out of the image and add it in editing. In video, Veo shades the flat black shapes, adds lens depth, and the hatching shimmers and boils frame to frame, which can be used on purpose as 'line boil' but is hard to control.

## Production cost

Stills are cheap and character consistency is relatively good because characters are silhouettes and masses, so identity rides on costume shape. Motion is the hard part: 8s Veo clips of fine hatching shimmer and drift, so plan for held plates with slow Premiere push-ins and cuts rather than animating inside the plate. Panel-sequence storytelling (one plate per beat, cross-dissolved or hard-cut to narration) is cheapest and truest to the form.
