# Georgian hand-coloured satirical etching

> Crowded, caricatured, hand-coloured etchings of politicians, bankers and kings, with speech scrolls and labels, from the 1780s to 1830s London print shop.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A British tradition of single-sheet political cartoons, etched in copper and coloured by hand, sold in London print-shop windows to a public who read them as the newspaper of the day. Figures are grotesquely exaggerated, symbols are labelled, and the image is packed with detail to be read slowly. It is the ancestor of the editorial cartoon and carries a deeply British disrespect for the powerful. Victorian wood-engraved press illustration (Punch, Doré's London) is its close cousin and shares the line-and-hatch finish.

## Signature traits

- Fine pen-and-needle etched line with cross-hatching and stipple, in brown-black ink on cream paper
- Washes of hand-applied watercolour in flat patches: pale yellow, rose, blue-grey, brick red
- Exaggerated caricature: swollen bellies, hooked noses, tiny heads or huge wigs on historical figures
- Labelled objects and speech scrolls/balloons with lettering in period typefaces
- Crowded, horizontal compositions with several vignettes and a clear central figure
- Emblematic symbols: pudding, money bags, scales, crowns, John Bull in a waistcoat
- Title and publisher's imprint and date lettering underneath the image

## References

- *The Plumb-pudding in danger* — James Gillray, 1805. Pitt and Napoleon carve up the globe as a pudding; the template for 'powerful men divide the world' satire.
- *The Bank Restriction Note (Bank Restriction, or Newgate Paper Money)* — George Cruikshank, 1819. A forged banknote parody showing hanged people; a savage British satire of money and the law.
- *Monty Python's Flying Circus animations* — Terry Gilliam, 1969-1974. Moving cut-out Victorian engravings used for modern satire; the proof this look works in motion.
- *London: A Pilgrimage* — Gustave Doré and Blanchard Jerrold, 1872. Victorian engraved views of London's poor and rich, for the engraving side of the family.

## What it carries

Rude, comic, grotesque, knowing, defiantly British. It carries mockery of the rich and powerful in a way that feels like heritage rather than propaganda. It fights against earnestness, sleekness and any sentimental treatment of its targets.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

The strongest fit for this audience in the whole family: a native British tradition of attacking the powerful, with symbols everyone reads (pudding, scales, top hat). It suits the system-as-a-crowded-room story, a stock-market, bank or cabinet scene. It names the decision-maker visually, which matches the reader guidance. It would undermine stories that need sympathy for an individual in realistic detail, and the period costume risks 'costume drama' if the subject is modern; use it as a deliberate anachronism.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators produce a clean digital 'vintage' illustration: smooth line, even colour, plausible but empty. The caricature is timid, faces look like ordinary people in costume, and the crowded packed composition collapses into a single centred figure. Period text is gibberish. Fix by requesting several named vignettes, extreme distortion, and adding the captions in post. Offensive stereotype is a real hazard in the historic caricature tradition (it relies on antisemitic and racist tropes in places), so prompts must target behaviour and office, never ethnicity.

## Production cost

Stills are cheap and rich with detail, but each plate needs checking for tropes and nonsense text. Character consistency across plates is moderate because caricature is exaggerated and the generator tends to soften it. Motion is best done as cut-out animation in edit, with pieces cut from a plate, which is slow but gives a distinctive look and avoids Veo's depth problems.
