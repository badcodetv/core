# French New Wave

> Jump cuts, street shooting and a playful, essayistic camera that talks back at the audience.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A late-1950s to 1960s French movement from critics-turned-directors who shot on location with light equipment, natural light, handheld cameras and small crews. It broke continuity rules (jump cuts, direct address, abrupt tone shifts) and mixed genre, politics and film quotation. Later Godard became openly political and anti-consumerist; Varda brought documentary eye and women's perspectives. Its energy is of young people making cinema from whatever is at hand.

## Signature traits

- Jump cuts within a single scene; breaks of continuity left visible
- Handheld camera on real Paris streets, cafes and apartments, using natural light
- Black-and-white (Breathless, 400 Blows) or flat, bold primary colour (Pierrot le Fou, Contempt) with block reds, blues and yellows
- Characters looking or speaking to camera; narrator or voice-over commentary
- On-screen text, title cards, posters and slogans quoted in frame
- Long tracking shots down streets or in cars alongside quick cuts
- Casual, improvised-looking performances, cigarettes, sunglasses, striped tops
- Film and art references, mix of comedy, crime, love and politics in one piece

## References

- *Breathless (À bout de souffle)* — Jean-Luc Godard, 1960. The jump-cut manifesto, shot on the street in natural light.
- *The 400 Blows (Les Quatre Cents Coups)* — François Truffaut, 1959. A boy and a school system; tender street realism with a final freeze-frame.
- *Cléo from 5 to 7* — Agnès Varda, 1962. A real-time walk through Paris; a model of observational city shooting with a subjective edge.
- *Weekend* — Jean-Luc Godard, 1967. A car-crash satire of consumer society, with a famous long traffic-jam tracking shot.
- *Pierrot le Fou* — Jean-Luc Godard, 1965. Primary-colour pop-art crime film that quotes, interrupts and talks to the audience.

## What it carries

Wit, restlessness, romance, irreverence and cool. It carries comic detachment and political jabs without sounding like a sermon. It fights against earnestness and polish, and against a smooth emotional arc; it can feel like a pose and the 'cool' register is easy to overplay.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire told with a narrator who interrupts, cites and jokes: a fit for a future superintelligence addressing the audience directly, with on-screen text, title cards and facts quoted over images. Good for consumerism, advertising and money jokes, where Weekend's logic of 'the car crash is the economy' is an excellent model. It would undermine a sincere, slow tragedy of one family, and a UK working-class audience may read French cool as arty or distant unless it is grounded in local places.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana: 'French New Wave film still, 1960, black and white 35mm, handheld, natural daylight, sidewalk cafe, young man in a suit with a cigarette, grainy, slightly overexposed highlights, 1.37:1' or for the colour strand 'flat primary colour blocks, red car, blue wall, yellow dress, 1960s pop-art, hard daylight, wide shot, no shadows'. Ask for transposed locations such as a UK high street or seaside to avoid pastiche Paris. For Veo, describe quick pans, a car-side tracking shot, or a locked static two-shot, and plan jump cuts in the edit by taking two clips of the same setup. Text overlays and title cards should be added in post. Exclude: 'polished studio lighting, smooth gimbal, glossy colour grade, symmetrical beauty portrait, lens flare'.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators produce a tourist's Paris (Eiffel Tower, berets, accordions) and a romanticised 'vintage' filter rather than the casual street look; avoid by naming the specific street action and a different city. Jump cuts need consistency of two takes, and models rarely keep a face and costume identical between clips, so a 'jump cut' can read as a different person; use a character reference. Veo smooths and stabilises, which removes handheld looseness. Modern faces and hair slip into period shots. On-screen text is unreliable in the model, so add it later. Primary-colour palettes can drift to muddy or oversaturated.

## Production cost

Moderate. The style is built in the edit, so the generator only needs to produce clean usable takes, but the jump cut demands repeated near-identical takes which is the weak point of image-to-video. Using the same start frame for two clips with different prompts helps. Voice-over and on-screen text carry much of the style, which is cheap.
