# 1970s-80s public information film

> Short, stern, state-voiced warning films with plain filmstock colour, a menacing figure and a blunt instruction at the end.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The UK Central Office of Information produced short films broadcast between programmes, warning about drowning, fireworks, railway lines or nuclear war. They use plain, slightly faded 16mm photography, a stage-trained voice, an ominous synth or orchestral stab, and a simple end card with the instruction. They are a quintessential British memory, remembered as both comforting and traumatising, and the form is built to deliver a single message under a minute.

## Signature traits

- 16mm or 35mm filmstock with warm faded colour, visible grain and slight gate wobble
- Suburban, council-estate or playground settings shot in flat overcast daylight
- Simple framings: locked-off wide shots and slow zooms on a child or object
- A faceless or hooded personification of danger, e.g. a dark figure at a pond
- Authoritative male or maternal voiceover with clipped, dry delivery
- Sparse electronic or orchestral score with sudden stings, and ominous silence
- A final title card with the public instruction in blocky type and a state crest or slogan
- Runs 30-60 seconds, one idea only

## References

- *Lonely Water* — Central Office of Information (voiced by Donald Pleasence), 1973. The archetypal menacing-figure film about drowning; stands for the form's tone.
- *Apaches* — Central Office of Information, directed by John Mackenzie, 1977. Rural farm-safety film in which children die on screen; shows how blunt the form could be.
- *Charley Says* — Central Office of Information, with cartoon by Richard Taylor, 1973. Cartoon cat and children, showing the friendly face of the same warning model.
- *Protect and Survive* — Central Office of Information, 1976. Cold War civil defence material in the same flat official register.
- *Don't Die of Ignorance* — UK Government campaign, including the 'tombstone' television advert, 1987. A late stern state warning in the same tradition.

## What it carries

Chilling, dry, paternal, darkly funny, nostalgic. Carries 'a stern voice from authority tells you what to do' and so is a ready vehicle for the future-narrator warning. Fights against cosiness, long arcs and any need for character depth.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Short sharp warnings, the 'humans, please don't make this obvious mistake' premise in its purest form. A 45-second film about a mortgage, a payday loan or a zero-hours contract delivered in the state's voice is both familiar and funny to UK viewers of a certain age. Strong for shareable shorts. Undermines multi-act narrative and subtle emotion; younger viewers may not recognise the reference, and lecturing the viewer is a risk that must be handled with wit.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Describe the film stock and period concretely: '1970s British public information film, 16mm, faded warm colour, visible grain, flat overcast daylight, council estate in the north of England, locked-off wide shot, slow zoom in, dark hooded figure standing at the edge of frame, 4:3'. Add period details: parka coats, orange-brown palette, brick terraces. Put the end card text in post. Nano Banana can replicate a faded stock feel from material words rather than the brand. Exclude: modern cars, smartphones, sharp digital detail, HDR, teal-orange grade, contemporary signage.

## How generators get it wrong

The engines tend to give a modern digital image with filters laid on top, so period feel is shallow: specify era-appropriate objects and avoid anachronisms like modern cladding, car shapes and clothes. Generated grain looks like a uniform noise layer; add real grain, gate weave and colour fade in post. The menacing figure may become a stock horror character with glowing eyes; ask for a plain, mundane figure. State crests and the end card are mangled by generators; build them as graphics.

## Production cost

Low: a one-minute form, few locations and a minimal cast. Period set dressing needs careful prompting and checking for anachronisms. Voice-over carries the film, so the voice and script matter more than the footage. Excellent as a repeatable series format with a fixed end-card.
