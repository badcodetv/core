# Plasticine clay stop-motion

> Characters and sets modelled in plasticine and photographed one frame at a time, with fingerprints and mouth shapes you can see.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Clay animation was developed commercially from the early 1900s and became a British signature through Aardman Animations in Bristol from the late 1970s. Figures are plasticine over armatures, filmed on miniature sets with practical lights, typically at 12 to 24 distinct poses per second. Lip-sync uses swapped or sculpted mouth shapes, and the surface carries thumbprints and seams. In Britain it carries deep associations with warmth, tea, sheds and small-town decency, which is exactly why it makes a good Trojan horse for sharp satire.

## Signature traits

- Visible fingerprints, tool marks and slightly lumpy surfaces on every figure
- Wide, heavy-browed faces with big replaceable mouth shapes and simple dot or bead eyes
- Real miniature sets at tabletop scale, built from card, foam and fabric, with practical warm key light and soft shadows
- Motion animated on twos (about 12 distinct poses per second), so movement is slightly stepped and quivers
- Shallow macro-lens depth of field with background falling off into soft blur
- Saturated but earthy palette: mustard, brick, olive, cream, with pale skin tones
- Subtle surface boil, where clay shifts slightly between frames
- Broad, slow gags: a deadpan face held for a long beat before the punchline

## References

- *The Wrong Trousers* — Nick Park / Aardman, 1993. The benchmark for clay character acting and set-piece action, including a dry-humoured menace.
- *Creature Comforts* — Nick Park / Aardman, 1989. Real vox-pop interviews with ordinary people voiced onto clay animals: a direct model for ordinary British speech as comedy and politics.
- *Chicken Run* — Peter Lord and Nick Park / Aardman, 2000. A class and labour allegory (an exploitative owner, workers escaping the farm) delivered inside a family film.
- *Morph* — Peter Lord and David Sproxton / Aardman, 1977. The simplest possible clay character, showing how little a figure needs.

## What it carries

Warm, funny, handmade, British and unthreatening, which lets cruelty land harder when it arrives. It can carry gentle comedy, fond mockery and mounting dread played deadpan. It fights against grand epic scale, grit, horror and anything needing crowds or photorealism.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Excellent for small-scale, character-led satire about bosses, landlords, councils and shops, where an ordinary person is quietly crushed by a system, and for talking-head or vox-pop formats. It undermines stories that need scale (mass layoffs, collapse of nations) unless they are staged as one small room, and it risks softening anger into cosiness if the target is not sharply named.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana responds well to physical-material language: 'plasticine clay character, visible thumbprints and tool marks, slightly lumpy surface, miniature handmade set, macro lens, shallow depth of field, warm practical studio lighting, photographed stop-motion frame'. Name the set materials (cardboard, felt, painted foam) so it reads as a real model rather than a render. Put 'smooth CGI, glossy plastic, Pixar-style 3D render, subsurface glow, perfect symmetry, 4K cinematic' in the negative or exclude wording. For Veo, describe the stepping explicitly: 'stop-motion animation shot on twos, 12 frames per second, slightly jerky stepped movement, clay surface boiling between frames, static locked camera'. Keep one small action per 8-second clip. Describe the aesthetic rather than naming a studio or director.

## How generators get it wrong

The image model defaults to glossy 3D animated-feature output with smooth subsurface skin and perfect lighting that looks like a render, not a model. Fix with material words and a blunt exclusion of CGI language. Veo interpolates motion smoothly, so clay figures slide and morph like liquid instead of posing; request stepped motion and keep actions tiny. Mouths tend to flap or morph rather than swap discrete shapes, so dialogue is better dubbed over cutaways or held faces. Characters drift between shots in proportion and colour, and hands collapse into mush.

## Production cost

Character consistency is the main cost: faces and clay colours drift across shots, so every shot needs a locked reference image of the same figure. Lip-sync is poor, so plan for narration over mostly non-talking shots. Veo's smoothness is hard to suppress for long takes, so keep shots short and static-camera. Sets must be re-described identically each time. Moderate overall once a reference sheet exists.
