# Victorian wet-plate and carte-de-visite portraiture

> Slow, silvery 19th-century collodion portraits and street scenes in which people stand stiffly still and look straight at you.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The wet-plate collodion process (1850s onwards) required long exposures, so subjects held still, often in head clamps, and the images have a distinctive silvery, high-detail tonality with soft, swirling backgrounds. In Britain it produced the carte-de-visite craze, Roger Fenton's Crimea photographs (1855) and John Thomson's Street Life in London (1877), made with Adolphe Smith, which paired pictures of costermongers, chimney sweeps and street traders with text about their work and poverty. It is a photographic form of the 'condition of the working class' report, and still the earliest honest-looking image of British poverty.

## Signature traits

- Long-exposure stillness: sitters stiff, eyes unblinking, hands visible and held, no smiles
- Monochrome with silvery highlights, or sepia or brown tones in albumen prints and ambrotypes
- Shallow, swirly or vignetted focus in the lens, with sharp centre and soft edges
- Plate edges: uneven developer flow, chemical streaks, black borders, scratches, dust and cracks
- Painted backdrops and studio props in portraits; real pavements and walls for street scenes
- Motion appears as ghosts: moving people blur away or vanish, and only still objects stay sharp
- Clothing and occupations from the 1860s-80s: bowler hats, aprons, barrows, carried signs, tools
- Near-square or portrait oval crops, or card-mount borders with captions

## References

- *Street Life in London* — John Thomson and Adolphe Smith, 1877. The model for photographing urban working people with accompanying text and a reforming purpose.
- *Photographs of the Crimean War* — Roger Fenton, 1855. Early war photography in collodion, notable for stillness and what it was made not to show. **(Corrected: Roger Fenton's Crimean War photographs date from 1855 (he was in the Crimea Feb-June 1855); there is no single book by that title. Better: 'Roger Fenton, Crimean War photographs, 1855'. Fenton used wet collodion negatives (and some waxed-paper).)**
- *Gardner's Photographic Sketch Book of the War* — Alexander Gardner, 1866. Wet-plate battlefield photography that made death seem present and ordinary.
- *What Remains* — Sally Mann, 2003. A modern artist deliberately using wet-plate collodion, showing how flawed and ghostly it can look.

## What it carries

Eerie, formal, archival and strangely modern. It carries history, ghosts, 'this has happened before' and the long continuity of exploitation. It fights speed, comedy and naturalism, since nobody in these pictures moves naturally.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A narrator-from-the-future sequence that reaches back: 'We told you in 1877'. It suits stories about the repetition of exploitation, rent and gig labour as old forms in new clothes, and the record as evidence. It lets the satire say that this is not new. It undermines contemporary settings (anything with phones or screens looks like a gimmick) and any story needing expressive acting.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana handles 'wet plate collodion photograph, 1870s, silvery tonality, long exposure, sitters rigid and unsmiling, swirling soft lens edges, plate edge streaks and chemical marks, card-mount border' reasonably well; name the process and the dust and the stains. Negatives: 'no sepia filter overlay, no modern clarity, no smiling, no digital sharpness, no vignette effect'. In Veo, a still is best animated minimally: 'sitter stays almost frozen, tiny blink or breath, slight plate flicker'. Large movements make it look like a filter on live-action, and Veo tends to colourise or smooth the plate; keep clips short.

## How generators get it wrong

Most outputs are a sepia filter on a modern face: clean skin, flattering smiles, wrong clothing, and modern lens rendering. Real plates have odd tonality, strong skin texture and no smiling. Veo animation fills in natural movement and removes the ghostly stillness, and may smooth the plate damage. The fix is to specify stiffness, stains and damage, and to keep movement tiny.

## Production cost

Stills are straightforward. Video needs restraint: nearly all shots should be held images with micro-movement, which suits a narrated, essay-like film. Costume and period accuracy need checking every shot, and faces need references to stay consistent.
