# CCTV and surveillance camera

> Fixed high-angle camera, wide lens, low frame rate, timestamp: no one is directing and everybody is a suspect.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Closed-circuit camera footage from shops, car parks, corridors and streets is shot from a fixed high position with a wide lens, low resolution, compressed colour or night-vision monochrome, and a timestamp with camera number. Britain has among the densest CCTV coverage in the world and the look is part of everyday life, from shop-security stills on the news to doorbell cameras. The grammar is: no cuts, no camera movement, the subject is small in the frame, and the drama is in what happens in the corner.

## Signature traits

- Fixed high-angle, wide-angle, slightly fisheye lens covering a corridor, till, car park or street
- Low resolution, compression blocks, low frame rate (1-5 fps, jerky movement) and ghosting trails
- Burned-in white sans-serif timestamp and camera label (CAM 03) in a corner
- Night mode: grey-green or black-and-white infrared, glowing eyes and bright white clothing
- Split-screen grids of four, nine or sixteen feeds with different camera angles
- Long static takes: people enter and leave the frame; no coverage, no close-ups
- Overexposed windows and doorways, blown-out headlights, flat fluorescent interiors

## References

- *Cache (Hidden)* — Michael Haneke, 2005. A film built on static surveillance-style footage that makes the viewer an observer, the textbook case of the grammar.
- *Paranormal Activity* — Oren Peli, 2007. Uses a fixed bedroom camera with a timestamp to build dread through duration and stillness.
- *Big Brother (UK)* — Endemol for Channel 4, 2000-. A mainstream British show whose look is fixed cameras, night-vision and constant observation, familiar to a mass audience.
- *The Conversation* — Francis Ford Coppola, 1974. A surveillance thriller about recorded evidence and the moral cost of watching, which supplies the theme as well as the technique.
- *Time Code* — Mike Figgis, 2000. A feature shot as four simultaneous real-time takes in a split-screen grid, showing the multi-feed form.

## What it carries

Cold, voyeuristic and anxious. It carries menace, bureaucracy, the sense of being recorded and the dark comedy of being tracked by something that never blinks. It fights against warmth, emotion and anything that depends on faces.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Excellent for stories about power and accountability: who is watching the workers, the bosses' meeting recorded, a warehouse with automated management, the all-seeing system. The split-screen grid is a good format for a heist, a chase or several simultaneous events. It would undermine empathy-driven stories, because the frame refuses close-ups, and it fatigues fast if used for more than a short sequence.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

State the viewpoint and recording constraints: 'CCTV security camera footage, fixed high corner angle, wide-angle lens, low resolution, compression artefacts, timestamp and camera label in the top corner, fluorescent lighting, grey desaturated colour'. For night: 'infrared night-vision camera, monochrome grey-green'. For video, ask for 'locked-off camera, no movement, no zoom'. Exclude: cinematic grading, shallow depth of field, close-ups, camera movement, hand-held, lens flare. Add the timestamp and camera overlay and frame-rate reduction (resample to 4-8 fps) in the edit rather than in the generation, and build grids in the edit.

## How generators get it wrong

The model composes for beauty: a centred subject, neat symmetry, rich colour and good light. Real CCTV has people tiny, cut off and at the edge, so ask for 'subject small in the frame, off to one side, partly out of frame'. Timestamps and camera labels are rendered as scrambled text. Veo gives smooth 24 fps motion so the low frame rate must be applied in post. Night-vision images tend to glow like a stylised horror effect. Because the shot is wide, faces at a distance degrade and flicker.

## Production cost

Low for a single locked shot and cheap on character consistency since faces are small. Hard parts: keeping a static camera stable across a long take (Veo clips are mostly 8 seconds), keeping the same room layout between clips for multi-camera grids, and making different camera angles of the same room match.
