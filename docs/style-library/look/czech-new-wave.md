# Czech New Wave

> Gently absurd, cruelly funny 1960s Czechoslovak satire of bureaucrats and ordinary people in muted colour.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A 1960s Czechoslovak movement (Menzel, Chytilová, Forman, Němec and others) from the Prague film school FAMU. Films mixed non-professional actors, improvisation, black-and-white or pastel colour, and deadpan satire of officials, party meetings and small-town life, all under a communist state that eventually crushed the movement after the 1968 invasion. It is less known in the UK than the French and Italian waves, which makes it a genuine fresh option, and it is directly about how ordinary people accommodate unaccountable power. The comedy is human rather than ideological.

## Signature traits

- Static or gently moving observational camera with ordinary people in everyday spaces: train station, ball hall, village pub
- Black and white (Closely Watched Trains) or pastel and collage colour (Daisies), with sudden shifts between them
- Non-professional faces, odd and specific, often old or plain, in tight groups
- Deadpan staging with absurd details in the background: a raffle gone wrong, a stolen prize, a confused committee
- Official rooms: committee tables, ribbons, uniforms, badges, stamps, long corridors
- Understated, slightly stilted dialogue and comic misunderstanding, incident-driven episodes
- Collage, cut-and-paste and food-fight sequences in the avant-garde strand
- Dry, kindly humour that turns bitter in the last scene

## References

- *Closely Watched Trains (Ostře sledované vlaky)* — Jiří Menzel, 1966. A young railway apprentice in wartime; gentle comedy that turns tragic, in black and white.
- *The Fireman's Ball (Hoří, má panenko)* — Miloš Forman, 1967. A village charity ball where the raffle prizes are stolen; a satire of petty officialdom widely read as political.
- *Daisies (Sedmikrásky)* — Věra Chytilová, 1966. Two women wreck a banquet in collage and tinted colour; an anarchic critique of consumption.
- *A Report on the Party and the Guests (O slavnosti a hostech)* — Jan Němec, 1966. A picnic where guests are coerced into conformity; sharp political allegory with a simple staging.

## What it carries

Wry, kind, absurd, then suddenly sad. It carries satire of institutions with sympathy for the people stuck inside them. It fights against heroic stories and against slick spectacle; the humour is understated and a pure rage register does not suit it.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire of committees, consultations, shareholder or council meetings, a stage-managed celebration where the people in charge steal the raffle. It is an excellent fit for 'contempt for the mistake, not the people', because the camera likes everybody. It suits stories about bureaucracy, small-time corruption and complicity. It would undermine epic or action sequences and anything wanting punch or glamour, and few UK viewers know it, so it will read as 'old black-and-white comedy' unless the content is strong.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana: '1960s Czechoslovak film still, black and white 35mm, village hall committee seated at a long table with ribbons and badges, flat even light, non-professional faces, wide static composition, deadpan staging, slightly soft lens' or for colour 'pastel 1960s colour, faded, collage, paper cut-out backgrounds'. Name the specific props: stamps, ribbons, raffle tickets, urns, uniforms. Request wide static two-shots and group portraits with many distinct faces. For Veo: 'static camera, a slow comic mishap unfolds in the background, people shuffle and glance, no camera movement'. Exclude: 'cinematic drama lighting, fast cuts, lens flare, glamorous faces, modern clothing, clean digital sharpness, horror tone'. Translate to a UK setting with a parish council, working men's club or bingo hall to reach the target audience.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators over-glamorise faces and drift to a generic 'Eastern European' grey-misery look with Soviet icons and snow, missing the comic warmth. Fix by asking for amused, tired, ordinary faces and daylight domestic settings. Group scenes of many people produce duplicated or fused faces; keep groups to five or six or accept it as part of the absurd. The humour lives in timing and small background gags that Veo rarely stages on cue; plan the gag as a clear single action. Collage and cut-out styles tend toward digital scrapbook kitsch. Dry deadpan acting is hard to prompt; Veo often gives broad or smiling expressions.

## Production cost

Moderate. Static wide frames are easy for the models and cheap to keep stable, which is a real advantage. The cost is in group scenes (many faces to keep consistent) and in staging a gag that reads in an 8-second clip. The comedy relies on voice and edit timing, so narration and sound do most of the work.
