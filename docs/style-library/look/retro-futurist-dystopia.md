# Retro-futurist dystopia

> A bureaucratic future imagined by the 1960s-80s: pale sterile corridors, wide lenses and clumsy, over-engineered machinery.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Science fiction that pictures tomorrow as built by yesterday: white sets, Brutalist concrete, bulky CRT screens, pneumatic tubes and ducts, uniformed workers and paperwork. It runs from the clean-white systems of THX 1138 and Logan's Run to the cluttered, pipe-heavy world of Brazil. The target is the bureaucratic and corporate order, so the camera favours symmetrical corridors, wide-angle distortion and tiny people under enormous architecture. It treats technology as shabby and malfunctioning, which gives it humour as well as dread.

## Signature traits

- Wide-angle lenses (18-24mm look) with distortion at the frame edges and a very deep perspective down corridors
- Symmetric one-point-perspective compositions: long corridors, rows of identical desks, giant halls with tiny figures
- Two opposed palettes: sterile white/pale grey sets, or cluttered, greasy-green and brown with ducting and cables
- Bulky analogue technology: CRT monitors with green or amber text, keyboards, tubes, switches, tape drives
- Overhead fluorescent or diffused ceiling light with flat, shadowless fill; occasional harsh single spotlight
- Uniforms, grey suits and identical workers; forms, stamps, queues and counters
- Brutalist concrete, parking structures and new-town architecture used as the future city
- Grain, slightly desaturated 1970s-80s film stock; practical glow from screens

## References

- *Brazil* — Terry Gilliam, 1985. A paperwork dystopia built from ducts and wide lenses, in which one clerical error destroys a man
- *THX 1138* — George Lucas, 1971. White void sets, shaved uniform workers and a consumer-compliance state
- *Alphaville* — Jean-Luc Godard (cinematography Raoul Coutard), 1965. A future city made out of ordinary 1960s Paris offices and night streets, with no sets
- *Fahrenheit 451* — Francois Truffaut, 1966. Bright 1960s suburban colour and a monorail as the cheerful face of control
- *Soylent Green* — Richard Fleischer, 1973. A overcrowded, resource-starved city with a corporate food secret

## What it carries

Absurd, claustrophobic, deadpan and funny. It carries satire of bureaucracy and the feeling of being a number better than almost anything else here. It resists warmth and the individual's heroism, and it can look dated unless the joke is on that datedness.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about the machinery of the economy: the forms, the queue, the algorithm that says no, the call centre, the system nobody designed and nobody can change. Fits the future-narrator conceit of BadCode (a 'future that went wrong'). Names the process rather than the villain, which suits a working-class reader who has fought a benefits form. It would undermine anything needing rural warmth or human intimacy, and it risks the 'technology is the villain' trap unless a human decision-maker is named.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Describe the architecture and lens: 'one-point perspective, 18mm wide-angle, long white corridor, rows of identical grey desks, fluorescent ceiling panels, tiny clerks in grey uniforms'. Name the tech by what it is: 'bulky beige CRT monitors showing green text, pneumatic tube network, ducting along the ceiling'. For the cluttered version, 'overhead pipes and ducts, grimy green walls, tangled cables, paperwork piled everywhere'. For Veo, slow dolly down a corridor, symmetrical static shots with small repeated motion (stamping, typing) are reliable. Say 'matte film, 1970s-80s film stock, slightly desaturated' to avoid a modern neon cyberpunk look.

## How generators get it wrong

The strongest failure is drift to neon cyberpunk: purple-and-cyan glowing rain, holograms, glossy chrome, which is a different and overused look. The models also modernise tech into touchscreens and sleek devices. Symmetry breaks: corridors have uneven perspective, with repeated workers duplicated as obvious clones. Screen text and forms are gibberish. 'Retro-futurist' can produce 1950s atomic-age cartoon colour instead. Wide-angle distortion is applied to faces in an unpleasant way if the subject is close.

## Production cost

Moderate. Architecture and corridors are the strongest territory for Nano Banana, and static symmetrical clips are reliable in Veo. Cost lies in repeated identical-looking workers (the models produce variety when you want uniformity), readable on-screen text (do it in post), and keeping one consistent set across shots, which needs a reference plate for each space.
