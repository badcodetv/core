# Paint-on-glass oil animation

> Wet oil paint smeared and reworked with fingers on glass, photographed after each tiny change, so every image melts into the next.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A technique perfected in Russian and Canadian animation: oil paint (often slow-drying) is applied with fingers and brushes to sheets of glass, often lit from below, and the single image is continually reworked and photographed. Alexander Petrov won an Academy Award for The Old Man and the Sea in 1999 using this method, painting entirely with his fingers. Because nothing is cut or replaced, forms flow and transform continually. Colour and light are rich, with painterly, impressionistic edges.

## Signature traits

- Thick, smeared oil-paint texture with finger marks and visible streaks
- Forms that dissolve and re-form continuously, with no hard cuts between objects
- Glowing, luminous colour from back-lighting through the glass
- Soft, impressionistic edges and indistinct faces, painted rather than drawn
- Slow, fluid camera-like movement achieved by repainting the scene
- Strong colour harmonies: golden light, deep blues, sea greens
- Slightly breathing, shimmering surfaces from small frame-to-frame changes
- Very small cast and minimal setting, long contemplative shots

## References

- *The Old Man and the Sea* — Aleksandr Petrov, 1999. The most famous oil-on-glass film, with luminous, flowing imagery.
- *The Mermaid* — Aleksandr Petrov, 1997. Shows the same method on a fairytale subject, with fluid transformations.
- *My Love* — Aleksandr Petrov, 2006. A later Petrov film, showing the technique applied to a romantic period piece.
- *The Street* — Caroline Leaf (National Film Board of Canada), 1976. Paint-on-glass animation of a family death, using flowing transformation from one image into the next.

## What it carries

Lyrical, elegiac, dreamlike, tender; carries grief, memory and quiet dignity. It fights against jokes, speed, sharp irony and specificity.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Best for emotional anchors inside a satire: a flashback to a lost workplace, a childhood memory of a town before closure, a eulogy for a pit or factory. The flowing transformation also visualises money or time melting into other things. It undermines punchline-driven comedy and anything that needs the audience to read small text or distinct faces.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana: 'oil paint on glass animation frame, thick finger-smeared oil paint, visible streaks and fingerprints, luminous back-lit colour, impressionistic soft edges, no sharp lines, painterly faces without detail, warm golden light against deep blue'. Exclude 'photorealistic, digital art, smooth airbrush, sharp outlines, 3D render, cinematic bokeh'. Veo does a decent job of morphing transitions in this style: 'paint animation, shapes flow and dissolve into one another, continuous transformation, slow drifting movement, locked frame'. Keep prompts to one subject and one transformation per clip.

## How generators get it wrong

The image model tends to generate 'digital painting' with polished gradients and airbrushed lighting rather than the thick texture of finger paint, and the faces become detailed and glossy. Veo's natural tendency to morph is an advantage here, but it can also turn into generic swirly psychedelia or a slideshow of unrelated frames. Fix with strong material language (fingerprints, streaks, thick oil) and a single continuous action. Texture usually flattens in compression.

## Production cost

Low to medium, because Veo's morphing weakness becomes a feature and consistency is loose by design. The challenge is narrative clarity: with soft faces and no hard cuts it is hard to tell who is who or to land specific information. Best used in short, wordless inserts rather than as the main look.
