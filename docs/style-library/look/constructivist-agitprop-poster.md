# Constructivist agitprop poster

> Diagonal, red-black-cream geometric posters with photomontage and heavy sans-serif type, in the manner of 1920s Soviet agitprop.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A design language developed in Russia in the late 1910s and 1920s to send political messages to a mostly new and partly illiterate audience: simple shapes, strong diagonals, flat red, black and cream, with type treated as an image. Photomontage and cropped faces with hard-edged geometry served as arguments. It remains the shorthand for 'power, revolution, the future' and is borrowed constantly by corporate and political branding, which is also its limit.

## Signature traits

- Palette locked to red, black and off-white/cream, sometimes one muted blue or yellow
- Strong diagonal composition at around 30 to 45 degrees; wedge, circle, and triangle shapes as actors
- Photomontage: cropped, high-contrast halftone faces and hands pasted over flat colour
- Heavy, condensed sans-serif capitals as part of the picture, set at angles or stacked
- Extreme scale contrasts: tiny people against giant machines or fists
- Flat colour, hard edges, no shading, thick rules and bars
- Megaphone/arrow/gear/smokestack motifs

## References

- *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge* — El Lissitzky, 1919. The founding abstract agitprop poster: a red wedge pierces a white circle; the whole argument is shape.
- *Books! (Knigi!) poster* — Alexander Rodchenko, 1924. Photomontage of Lilya Brik shouting through a megaphone; defines the cropped-face plus hard type model.
- *Electrification of the Entire Country* — Gustav Klutsis, 1920. Montage of figures and mechanical forms arguing a national economic plan as a picture.
- *ROSTA Windows* — Vladimir Mayakovsky and ROSTA poster artists, 1919-1921. Hand-stencilled satirical news posters in windows; the closest precedent for punchline-driven poster satire.

## What it carries

Urgent, commanding, bold, optimistic-militant. It can carry sarcasm, mockery of the bosses and heroic scale. It fights against intimacy, subtlety and anything quiet.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about ownership and the means of production, who gets the surplus, the machine and the worker. A narrator with total authority sits naturally here, as does the 'we have a plan' energy. It is dangerous because it signals Soviet communism, which a UK working-class reader drifting right may read as an enemy badge or a cliché; use it ironically or mix it with British imagery, and watch for it feeling like fancy-dress. It would undermine personal, human-scale stories.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana renders this look reliably when told the exact devices: '1920s constructivist propaganda poster, flat red, black and cream only, bold diagonal composition, geometric wedge and circle shapes, halftone photomontage of a cropped face, heavy condensed sans-serif lettering'. Describe the layout in thirds and name what is on the diagonal. Exclude: 'gradients, drop shadows, 3D, realistic lighting, modern flat-design vector, soft colours, extra colours'. Veo image-to-video on posters tends to animate the poster as a surface, adding perspective, paper flutter or dimension; ask for 'flat graphic, shapes slide along the diagonal, no perspective, locked camera'.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators default to a generic 'Soviet' look: a red star, a hammer and sickle, a stern worker, a tidy symmetrical layout. The real thing is asymmetric, diagonal and abstract. Text is the biggest failure: lettering comes out as plausible nonsense, and pseudo-Cyrillic is common unless you steer it. Fix by removing text from the generation and setting type in editing. The cream often goes yellow and the red goes crimson-orange across a batch.

## Production cost

Stills are easy to produce at volume, and the restricted palette keeps a series coherent. The cost is in typography: real lettering must be added in Premiere or ffmpeg, which is time but gives full control. Motion works well as sliding and wiping shapes done in edit; AI animation of the full poster is unreliable.
