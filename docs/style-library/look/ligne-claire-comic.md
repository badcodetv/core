# Ligne claire comic

> Uniform clean outlines, flat colour, no hatching and exact, researched backgrounds, in the tradition of the Franco-Belgian adventure comic.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A drawing style developed in Belgium in the 1930s and named in the late 1970s: every object is drawn with one even-weight line, colour is flat and unshaded, and backgrounds are detailed and accurate while characters are simplified. The clarity makes it ideal for explaining, and the sunny surface allows dark content to slip through. It carries a deadpan, almost innocent tone, which is where its satirical power comes from.

## Signature traits

- Single, even line weight around all forms, black or dark brown, no thick/thin variation
- Flat local colour with no gradient, no cast shadows or only a single flat shadow shape
- Highly detailed, accurate backgrounds contrasted with simple, round, expressive characters
- Clean, bright, slightly pastel palette (sky blue, mustard, brick red, green)
- Eye-level or slightly high horizontal compositions, wide establishing shots, readable staging
- Characters with dot eyes, simple mouths, exaggerated but cool body language
- Sound effects and bubbles in clear hand lettering

## References

- *King Ottokar's Sceptre (Le Sceptre d'Ottokar)* — Hergé, 1939. Tintin adventure about a fictional Balkan state facing annexation; the political satire in a ligne claire frame.
- *The Blue Lotus (Le Lotus bleu)* — Hergé, 1936. The point where the style's researched, accurate backgrounds were fully established.
- *Tintin and the Picaros* — Hergé, 1976. Late Tintin satirising coups and media circuses; shows the style handling cynical politics.
- *Jimmy Corrigan, the Smartest Kid on Earth* — Chris Ware, 2000. American graphic novel that pushes ligne claire's flat, even line into deadpan emotional bleakness.

## What it carries

Deadpan, bright, adventurous, orderly. It can carry cold irony and the sense of a clear, rational world going wrong. It fights against messy emotion, atmosphere, night, and any scene that needs texture or dirt.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire of institutions, where the clean surface and the awful content make the joke: bureaucrats, bankers, committees, 'reasonable' men doing monstrous things. It is excellent for explaining a mechanism (how the money moved) because every object is readable. It is a very good match for a narrator who is certain. It would undermine gritty, visceral, hand-to-mouth poverty stories and horror.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana holds this style when told its rules: 'ligne claire comic panel, uniform thin black outline of constant weight, flat colour fills, no shading, no hatching, detailed accurate background, simple round characters with dot eyes, clear daylight palette'. Include 'European adventure comic, 1950s print' and name the setting concretely. Exclude: 'shading, gradients, hatching, dramatic lighting, realistic proportions, 3D render, anime, thick-and-thin ink'. For Veo, specify 'animated comic panel, characters move with limited animation, background static, flat colour, no camera move'; it often adds shading and lighting that undoes the style, so test on a single shot first.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators slip into soft cel-shaded illustration with gradients on skin and sky, variable line weight, and anime proportions. Background detail is the failure the other way: either bare or messy rather than precise. Characters lose the round simple faces and acquire realistic features. In video, outlines wobble and flicker and colour fills bleed, so clean line art in motion is hard to hold.

## Production cost

Stills are straightforward and character consistency is better than for painterly styles because characters are simple and the palette is flat; a reference sheet carries well. Motion is the cost: clean outlines flicker in Veo, so lean on panel-to-panel editing, camera moves in Premiere over still panels, and limited-animation clips of one moving element.
