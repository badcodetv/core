# Satirical latex caricature puppets

> Grotesque foam-and-latex puppets of the powerful, lit like a cheap TV studio and played for savage laughs.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Born in British TV satire of the 1980s, where Spitting Image built rubbery caricature puppets of politicians and puppeteered them in front of studio sets. The grotesque exaggeration (huge jaw, tiny eyes, sweating skin) is the argument: the face is the editorial. Latex sheen, visible paint stipple, wig hair and puppeteer-limited gesture (head, mouth, one arm) define the look. Flat multi-camera studio light keeps it feeling like a broadcast, not a film.

## Signature traits

- Oversized caricatured heads on small stuffed bodies, roughly 1:2 head-to-body ratio
- Glossy latex skin with specular highlights on forehead and nose, visible airbrush stipple and seam lines
- Limited puppet articulation: jaw flap, eye-rolls, one hand on a rod; bodies mostly static
- Flat, even three-point studio light, minimal shadow, 4:3 broadcast framing
- Painted flats and cheap set pieces: a lectern, a plywood front door, a news desk
- Medium shots and close-ups of faces; reaction cutaways held a beat too long
- Saturated primary-colour suits against a neutral backdrop

## References

- *Spitting Image* — Roger Law and Peter Fluck (ITV), 1984-1996. The defining British latex political puppet satire; the template for caricature as editorial.
- *Team America: World Police* — Trey Parker and Matt Stone, 2004. Marionette film satirising politics with deliberately visible strings and miniature sets.
- *The Muppet Show* — Jim Henson, 1976-1981. Warmer foam-and-felt puppet performance on a theatre stage; the gentler pole of the same craft.
- *Les Guignols de l'info* — Canal+, 1988-2018. French equivalent: latex politician puppets presenting a fake news broadcast.

## What it carries

Mocking, rude, communal; the laugh of a pub watching the telly. It can carry contempt for named decision-makers and the absurdity of power. It fights tenderness and slow grief: the puppets cannot cry convincingly.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Short satirical pieces aimed at office-holders, boardrooms and bankers where the caricature is the joke and the viewer is invited to laugh at power. Familiar to a UK working-class audience from TV. Undermines stories needing sympathy for ordinary people or sincere emotional payoffs, and risks reading as mockery of individuals rather than of the decision.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana responds well to material nouns: 'foam latex puppet', 'glossy painted rubber skin with airbrush stipple', 'visible seam at the jaw', 'wig hair', 'rod puppet hand'. Anchor the venue: 'cheap 1980s TV studio, flat even lighting, 4:3'. Describe the caricature by features (heavy jowls, tiny eyes, bulging forehead) rather than naming a real person; real-person likenesses of politicians are often blocked or softened, so invent composite types (the Minister, the Landlord). Veo handles puppet jaw flap and head turns if told 'stiff puppet movement, only head and mouth move, body rigid'. Negative: 'smooth CGI skin, realistic human, fluid walking, Pixar, claymation'.

## How generators get it wrong

The engine drifts to either photoreal humans or Pixar-smooth 3D toys; fix by repeating latex, stipple and seam language and forbidding 'cartoon 3D render'. Veo makes puppets walk and gesture with human fluidity, which kills the effect; specify a locked body and only mouth or head motion, and cut rather than animate travel. Faces collapse to one generic caricature across a cast; use a distinct feature list per character plus reference images. Mouth flap rarely matches dialogue, so plan voiceover over reaction shots.

## Production cost

Character consistency is the hard part: each caricature must hold across shots, which needs a fixed reference image per character. Motion is cheap if static, expensive if you want walking. Lip-sync is poor; short lines and reaction shots work best. Eight-second clips suit it because puppets mostly hold poses.
