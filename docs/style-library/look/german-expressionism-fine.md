# German Expressionism (painted-set cinema)

> Distorted, angular, shadow-slashed worlds where the set expresses the character's fear instead of describing a place.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Born in German painting (Die Brucke, 1905 on) and carried into cinema in the early 1920s after the First World War. Sets were painted and built with leaning walls, wrong-angled doors and shadows painted straight onto the floor, so architecture becomes psychology. Lighting is hard and theatrical, acting is stylised, and the frame is a stage for authority, fear and the crowd. It later fed film noir and horror.

## Signature traits

- Walls, doors and streets built at impossible angles, converging stairs and tilted windows
- Hard single-source light with black shadows, sometimes painted shadow shapes on walls and floors
- High-contrast near-monochrome (black, white, grey) or single-tint toning in blue, amber or sickly green
- Long thin figures, pointed hats, spiked railings and jagged roof lines as repeated shapes
- Low and Dutch-tilted camera angles looking up at authority figures
- Huge shadow of a figure cast ahead of or above the body
- Faces with exaggerated eye sockets and strong make-up, theatrical posture
- Crowds as a single mass moving in unison

## References

- *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* — Robert Wiene, 1920. The reference for painted, distorted sets and shadows drawn on the floor.
- *Metropolis* — Fritz Lang, 1927. Architecture and machinery as class power, with workers moving as one mass; the closest match to a political-economic subject.
- *Nosferatu* — F. W. Murnau, 1922. Shadow as a character; a looming figure on the stairs.
- *The Last Laugh* — F. W. Murnau, 1924. A doorman stripped of his uniform and status, told with mobile camera and almost no intertitles; working-class humiliation in this style.
- *The Scream* — Edvard Munch, 1893. The painted original of anxious, swirling space and sky.

## What it carries

Dread, paranoia, the sense that the system is a building that wants to swallow you. Can carry bleak comedy when exaggerated. Fights warmth, intimacy, naturalism, and any scene that needs the audience to relax or feel solidarity with ordinary home life.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about faceless institutions, landlords, banks, bosses or an elite seen from below; dystopian future-vision sequences; the cold-open warning. Undermines anything gentle or observational, and a long run of it exhausts a viewer, so it is best used for one sequence, not a whole film. Casting the powerful as monstrous shadow figures is effective but should aim at decision-makers, not at a technology or an outgroup.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana responds well to 'German Expressionist film still, 1920s, painted set' plus concrete geometry words: 'crooked buildings leaning inward, tilted doorway, jagged angular shadows painted on the floor, single hard spotlight, black and white with faint blue tint'. Dead-artist and film names such as Caligari are usually safe; describe rather than name any living director. Give the shadow shape explicitly ('a long shadow of a man in a top hat stretching up the wall'). For Veo, animate small things: a shadow creeping, a flickering lamp, a figure walking along a stair, with the camera locked; add 'silent film, slightly jerky 18 fps feel, film grain' and put 'smooth photoreal 3D, modern CGI, colour grading, lens flare' in the negative or exclude wording.

## How generators get it wrong

The generator tends to produce a generic 'noir' or 'spooky Victorian street': correct darkness but straight verticals and sensible perspective, so it loses the whole point (wrong angles). Fix by describing the angles in words and naming the leaning direction. Veo likes to straighten geometry and add a smooth modern camera move, and it adds volumetric fog and cinematic lens flare. Faces drift between shots, and the heavy make-up look flattens into a stock vampire. Intertitle text rendered by the image model is often garbled; add text in edit instead.

## Production cost

Character consistency is moderate because stylised faces with heavy make-up are forgiving. Geometry consistency across shots is the hard part: the same distorted street must look like the same street, so use one hero still as an image reference for every shot. Motion should stay small; large camera moves collapse the distortion into normal perspective. Clip length is not a problem since the stage-like tableau reads well in 8-second cuts.
