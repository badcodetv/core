# UK public information film (1970s)

> The cheerful, then terrifying, government-commissioned short: bright, flat, saturated colour, ordinary British locations, and a gentle voice warning of preventable disaster.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

From the 1940s to the 1990s the Central Office of Information made short films telling the British public how to behave, from crossing roads to surviving nuclear attack. The 1970s versions have a distinct, widely remembered look: 16mm colour with slightly bleached saturated colour, suburban locations, stock-company actors in knitwear, a mild narrator and then a sudden, sharp shock. The form is a rhetorical engine: reassurance, then a consequence nobody asked about, then a plain instruction. It is an unusually good match for a project that says 'we know how this ends, here is what to do'.

## Signature traits

- 16mm colour film, slightly faded saturation, warm skin tones, visible grain
- Ordinary British exteriors: terraced streets, canals, pylons, building sites, council estates
- Flat daylight, soft shadows, no dramatic lighting; the frame looks like an instruction diagram
- Static medium and wide shots with occasional hard zoom, a handful of cutaways to close-up objects
- Plain studio-voiced commentary or a mascot figure; blunt caption cards with a slogan
- Sudden tonal break: the same bright image continues while the sound or edit turns grim
- Period fashion: flares, nylon, anoraks, Ford Cortinas, Bakelite and early plastic objects

## References

- *Lonely Water* — Central Office of Information (narrated by Donald Pleasence), 1973. The canonical horror-register PIF: a hooded figure at the water's edge, which shows how cheerful format delivers menace.
- *Apaches* — Central Office of Information (dir. John Mackenzie), 1977. A children's farm-safety film in a brutally plain style; the template for the 'ordinary scene, terrible consequence' structure.
- *Charley Says* — Central Office of Information (Dave Gardner/Richard Taylor animation), 1973-1979. The animated-mascot form and the short direct-to-camera address that makes an instruction memorable. **(Corrected: COI series 1973-79, animation by Richard Taylor Cartoons, Kenneth Williams voiced the cat. The 'Dave Gardner' credit could not be confirmed; drop or verify.)**
- *Protect and Survive* — Central Office of Information / Home Office, 1975-1980. The nuclear-attack advice films: the most direct model for deadpan official calm over a catastrophe. **(Corrected: A civil defence campaign produced 1974-76, with the films made around 1975-76 for the COI; the pamphlet was published in 1980. Richard Taylor Cartoons made the animated films. The year 1976 is acceptable but approximate.)**
- *Threads* — Mick Jackson (BBC), 1984. A drama that deliberately adopts the visual grammar of the public information film, with captions and flat commentary, to deliver horror.

## What it carries

Warm, domestic, then cold. It carries reassuring menace, black comedy and the tragicomedy of being told what to do by people who are not going to help you. It fights against heroism, grandeur or any scene that needs speed or glamour.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

The best fit in this family for UK working-class satire: the audience already knows the voice, the locations are their own streets, and the form's built-in joke is that the government is calmly telling people to save themselves. Suits 'how to cope with the cost-of-living crisis' parodies, safety films about debt, rent or automation, and warnings in the narrator's voice. It would undermine anything that needs scale, spectacle or a sympathetic villain, and its nostalgia can make the target feel safely historical.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Specify a film stock and era rather than 'retro': '16mm colour film, 1970s British public information film, soft faded colour, flat overcast daylight, visible grain, static medium shot, terraced street, period anorak and flares'. Name the setting concretely (cul-de-sac, canal towpath, building site). Add 'slightly overexposed, low contrast, no lens flare, no shallow depth of field' to keep it flat. For video, ask for a static camera with a single slow zoom; hold the shot still while an element changes. Exclude: cinematic lighting, teal and orange grade, anamorphic flare, dramatic angles, modern cars, bokeh, glossy skin. Captions and slogans are best added in the edit since in-frame text is unreliable.

## How generators get it wrong

The look is usually overdone into 'warm nostalgia' orange with heavy vignette, which reads as a filter and as Instagram retro. Generators also add cinematic lighting and shallow depth of field by default, which breaks the flat information-film look; push 'flat, overcast, deep focus, no dramatic lighting'. Britain tends to look American (wide roads, wrong brick, wrong plugs, wrong road markings and number plates); name British specifics. The narrator voice from the video model is often wrong, so write and record it separately.

## Production cost

Moderate to low. The flat style forgives many errors and uses simple locations. The hard parts are keeping British period detail accurate (cars, clothes, plugs, signage) and consistent presenter or actor identity, because the faces are shown plainly in medium shots with no cinematic gloss to hide differences.
