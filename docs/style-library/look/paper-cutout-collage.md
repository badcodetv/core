# Paper cut-out collage

> Flat figures with jointed limbs cut from paper, card and found imagery, moved around like a pop-up stage.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Cut-out animation moves flat pieces of paper, card or photographic cuttings under a camera. It has a long tradition: Terry Gilliam's collage animations for Monty Python in the late 1960s and early 1970s made it the British voice of absurd satire, and the original South Park shorts used construction-paper cut-outs. Edges are crisp and slightly irregular, hinges show as pivot points, and movement is limited and jerky, which is part of the joke. Modern versions use photo cut-outs against painted backdrops.

## Signature traits

- Hard scissor-cut edges with slightly uneven outlines and visible paper thickness shadow
- Flat characters with jointed limbs that pivot at obvious hinge points (elbow, knee, jaw)
- Layered parallax planes with small drop shadows between layers
- Found-image collage: engravings, photographs, Victorian illustrations or magazine cuttings repurposed
- Visible paper grain, torn edges, tape or glue marks, and construction paper colours
- Limited, mechanical movement: slides, swivels, pop-ups, sudden replacement of a face piece
- Top-down or side-on flat camera, like a stage, with simple moves such as pans and pushes
- Direct, caption-like text and cut-out lettering in the frame

## References

- *Monty Python's Flying Circus (Terry Gilliam's animations)* — Terry Gilliam / BBC, 1969. The defining British collage satire: absurdist cutouts of Victorian engravings and photos used for politics, class and bureaucracy.
- *The Spirit of Christmas (Jesus vs. Santa)* — Trey Parker and Matt Stone, 1995. Construction-paper cut-out animation proving that crude, cheap-looking paper can carry offensive, topical satire. **(Corrected: There are two shorts: Jesus vs. Frosty (1992) and Jesus vs. Santa (1995, commissioned by Fox executive Brian Graden). 1995 is right for Jesus vs. Santa. The sources I found did not confirm the construction-paper medium, though that is widely known.)**
- *Tale of Tales* — Yuri Norstein, 1979. Shows a poetic, multi-layer cut-out technique on glass layers, at the opposite end of the emotional range.
- *The Adventures of Prince Achmed* — Lotte Reiniger, 1926. Early feature with cut-out silhouettes, relevant as the root of the technique.

## What it carries

Fast, irreverent, punk and cheap-and-cheerful; very good at absurdity and deadpan cruelty. It fights against emotional realism, tenderness and any sense of depth or physical presence.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Ideal for punchy, topical, sketch-like satire, explainers, and stories about institutions: politicians, bosses and bureaucrats can be a pile of cut-out heads on a stage. Collage lets real archive images (newspaper headlines, banknotes, cuttings) sit in the frame, tying satire to documents. It undermines stories that need empathy with one individual's inner life, because the figures read as pieces on a board.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana handles flat collage well: 'paper cut-out animation, flat figures cut from construction paper with crisp scissor edges, visible paper grain, small drop shadows between layers, torn paper background, photographed from directly above on a table, jointed limbs with brass-fastener pivots'. For the Victorian collage register, describe 'vintage engraving cut-outs, sepia and ink, mixed with coloured paper shapes'. Exclude 'gradient shading, 3D depth, soft airbrush, glow, realistic perspective, vector flat design'. For Veo, ask for 'cut-out puppet animation, pieces slide and pivot rigidly at joints, no deformation, limited jerky movement, locked top-down camera'. Text legibility is a separate risk, so add words afterwards in an edit.

## How generators get it wrong

Image output often drifts into clean vector flat design or soft digital 'paper-look' with gradients, losing physical edges and shadows. Veo wants to deform and morph the pieces, so rigid cut-out limbs bend like rubber and faces melt; it also adds spurious depth and camera perspective. Fix by asking for rigid pieces and a locked flat camera, and by animating mostly via simple slides in editing software. Generated lettering often misspells.

## Production cost

Lowest of the family, because flat pieces are consistent and cheap. Character consistency is easy with a flat design sheet. The strongest approach is to generate individual cut-out assets as stills and animate them in Premiere, rather than depending on Veo. Risk: looks cheap, so strong writing and timing are needed.
