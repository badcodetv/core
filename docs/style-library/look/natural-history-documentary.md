# Natural-history documentary

> Epic long-lens photography of animals and landscapes with a calm, authoritative narrator, applied to human behaviour.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The BBC Natural History Unit house grammar, from Life on Earth in 1979 through Planet Earth and Blue Planet II: high-end long-lens footage, slow-motion hero moments, sweeping aerials, orchestral scoring and a warm, knowing narrator who treats behaviour as drama. As satire it works by narrating the rich, the landlord class or commuters as a species with mating rituals and territory. The format is already tied to confidence and a sense of inevitability.

## Signature traits

- Long-lens close-ups with creamy background blur and a tiny depth of field
- Drone aerials and slow crane reveals of vast landscapes
- High-speed slow-motion for the hero moment (leap, strike, take-off)
- Rich saturated nature colour: golden hour, blue hour, mist
- Time-lapse of weather, light and crowds
- Narration in calm, curious, gently dry tone; 'Here, in the ...'
- Swelling orchestral cues with one solo instrument; natural sound dropped under
- Rule-of-thirds composition with animals walking into frame

## References

- *Life on Earth* — David Attenborough, BBC Natural History Unit, 1979. The founding television natural-history grammar and the template of the authoritative narrator.
- *Planet Earth* — BBC Natural History Unit, 2006. Defines the epic, high-definition, slow-motion look and orchestral scoring.
- *Blue Planet II* — BBC Natural History Unit, 2017. A modern reference for cinematic underwater photography and emotional storytelling.
- *Our Planet* — Silverback Films for Netflix, 2019. Shows the genre turning to an urgent policy message with the same visual language.

## What it carries

Awe, calm authority, inevitability, wry affection. Can make cruelty seem natural and so can make a system look like weather. Fights against intimacy, individual psychology and anything that needs the viewer to feel anger at a named person.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire of institutions and behaviours seen as species: 'the Landlord, in its natural habitat'. It makes a dry, funny delivery of the narrator voice easy. Undermines any point that requires blame, since the format naturalises behaviour; 'it's just nature' is the opposite of 'someone decided this'. Use carefully and name the decision-maker in the words.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana and Veo are strong here because it is a camera-language world: 'long-lens telephoto wildlife documentary footage, shallow depth of field, golden hour backlight, subject centre-left, slow motion, 4K, natural sound'. For human subjects, keep the identical animal-photography framing (crowd at a bus stop shot from far away through heat haze). Ask for 'drone aerial slowly descending' or 'macro lens' as needed. Veo will produce plausible ambient sound. Exclude: text overlays, logos, people looking at camera, unnatural CGI sheen, cartoon creatures.

## How generators get it wrong

The engines produce wonderful but generic 'screensaver' wildlife with oversaturated colour and impossible animal anatomy (extra legs, wrong tail on fast movement). Humans in a long-lens frame can look uncanny when closer than medium wide. Slow-motion is sometimes faked with mushy interpolation. Narration will not be right unless supplied separately; keep the voice in your own TTS or recording. Satirical captions or species labels garble in the image; add them in post.

## Production cost

Among the easiest to generate and the easiest to make look expensive. Consistency across shots is easier because animals and landscapes do not require a recurring face, though a recurring 'specimen' (the same man in a suit) requires references. Length is no problem since montage is the grammar. The real cost is writing narration with a dry authority the format demands.
