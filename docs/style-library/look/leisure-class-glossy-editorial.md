# Leisure-class glossy editorial

> Sun-drenched, perfectly composed colour photography of rich people relaxing in beautiful places, the visual language of the lifestyle magazine and the luxury advert.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Slim Aarons coined the idea in his own captions as 'attractive people doing attractive things in attractive places'. His Poolside Gossip-style pictures of Palm Springs, Capri and Monte Carlo from the 1950s to the 70s, collected in A Wonderful Time (1974), set the template for aspirational colour photography. Fashion editorial work of the 1970s, such as Guy Bourdin's Charles Jourdan advertisements, pushed the same polished composition into surreal, saturated territory. The pictures are staged, lit and printed to make wealth look natural and desirable.

## Signature traits

- Saturated, warm colour on slide film, with bright blue pools and skies, and clean primary accents
- Wide, deep-focus compositions with the group arranged in layers: foreground figures, pool, mid-ground terrace, distant mountains or sea
- Perfect light: late afternoon sun or bright midday with soft fill, no harsh shadows
- Elegant poses, designer swimwear, sunglasses, loungers, cocktails, parasols and private architecture
- Careful symmetry, strong horizontals and clean colour blocking
- Faces tanned, relaxed and unbothered; staff are visible but background
- Medium-format sharpness with fine grain and rich print colour
- Editorial variations: tight crops, surreal staging, gloss lip and nail colour, and stiletto-leg close-ups

## References

- *A Wonderful Time: An Elegant Excursion to the Lives of the Rich, Famous, and Beautiful* — Slim Aarons, 1974. The core book of this look: wealth at leisure as a photographic genre.
- *Poolside Gossip (photograph, Kaufmann Desert House, Palm Springs)* — Slim Aarons, 1970. The single most recognisable image of the style. **(Corrected: Slim Aarons, taken 1970 at the Kaufmann Desert House; the photograph was published later in his books. Fairly confident.)**
- *Charles Jourdan advertising campaigns* — Guy Bourdin, 1970s. Fashion editorial pushed into saturated, staged, unsettling territory.
- *Dolce Vita-era jet-set photography in Life magazine* — Various, 1960s. The press tradition of showing wealthy leisure as glamour. **(Unconfirmed on the web.)**

## What it carries

Seductive, aspirational and hollow, and the longer you look the more the gap shows. It is good for envy, privilege and the lie that wealth is deserved. It fights any scene that needs the audience to be near poverty at the same moment.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Pictures of the people at the top: billionaires, landlords, fund managers and the donors, shown exactly as they would want to be seen, while the narrator's voice undermines each shot. The contrast is the joke. It suits cutaways against work and rent sequences and the future-vault's old world. It undermines a whole film if it makes wealth look appealing without enough contradiction, and a working-class reader should never feel the film is selling them the dream.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana excels at this: 'mid-century colour slide photograph, medium format, late afternoon sun, luxury poolside terrace, desert modernist house, group of tanned people in designer swimwear, perfectly composed, saturated primary colours, wide deep focus'. Describe composition layers and architecture explicitly. For the satirical twist, state what is slightly wrong (a pool man in the background, a headline on a lounger). Negatives: 'no film grain overload, no dirty look, no handheld, no flash'. Veo handles slow cinematic drifts well on these pictures: 'gentle slow push-in, sun glitter on water, ice clinks'. It is one of the engine's most natural looks.

## How generators get it wrong

The engine tends to default to this look for anything it is told is 'beautiful', so it is easy to get but hard to make specific: results look like stock imagery or an Instagram travel feed with plastic skin and over-smooth gradients. Faces and bodies are too perfect and homogeneous. Retro colour often turns into an orange-and-teal grade. To fix, name the film-era palette and add flaws, such as wrinkles, real bodies and odd clothing. Hands, drinks and glassware are common error points.

## Production cost

Easy to produce and attractive, which is the danger: it can overpower the satire. Recurring characters (the same billionaire across shots) need reference sheets. Veo handles the gentle motion well, so volume is cheap. The real cost is making each shot say something rather than just look expensive.
