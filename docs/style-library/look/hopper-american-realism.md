# Hopper-style American realism

> Still, sunlit or lamp-lit modern interiors and streets, with lonely figures and hard geometry, as in Edward Hopper.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A twentieth-century American realist look from the 1920s to 1960s, used by painters of urban and small-town life in the Depression and after. Light is hard and clean, edges are crisp, people are placed in empty rooms or at windows, and nobody quite looks at anyone. It became a template for film noir, neo-noir and later cinema. It is quiet, observational and unemphatic, which makes it good for economic precarity without a sermon.

## Signature traits

- Hard low sun or artificial light cutting a clean-edged shape across a wall or floor
- Simplified geometric buildings and large flat planes of colour
- Large window or glass wall that lets the viewer look in from the dark outside
- One to three figures, separated, not speaking, staring away or down
- Muted palette with an off-key accent: a green wall, a red dress, sodium-lamp yellow
- Empty roads, diners, offices, gas stations, hotel rooms and stairwells at dawn or night
- Static or very slow framing, with a long hold
- Cropped low horizons, wide frame, clean horizontal bars

## References

- *Nighthawks* — Edward Hopper, 1942. The all-night diner seen through glass, the canonical lonely lit room.
- *Early Sunday Morning* — Edward Hopper, 1930. A row of shopfronts under flat low sun with nobody in the street.
- *Office at Night* — Edward Hopper, 1940. Workplace tension and unspoken hierarchy in a lamp-lit office.
- *Shirley: Visions of Reality* — Gustav Deutsch, 2013. A feature film that stages thirteen Hopper paintings as moving scenes; proof the look holds in time.
- *The Last Picture Show* — Peter Bogdanovich, 1971. Small-town economic stagnation in plain, hard black and white, with the same empty-street feeling.

## What it carries

Quiet melancholy, waiting, hollowing-out of a place. Can hold dry humour in its stillness. Fights spectacle, fast cutting, comic energy, and anything that needs a crowd.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories of closing shops, zero-hours work, lonely night shifts, a town after the factory leaves; the 'after' footage in a story where the narrator describes what went wrong. Its restraint serves a dark-comic narrator well because the picture is calm while the words are savage. Undermines big collective action, rage, or satire that needs caricature. In a British story it needs translating: a Greggs or a bus shelter in place of a diner, otherwise it reads as American cliche.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana renders 'Edward Hopper painting style, realist, hard directional sunlight, flat colour planes, empty interior, figure looking out of a window' well; describe the geometry (a rectangle of sun on the wall) and the single accent colour. Hopper is long dead, so naming is generally acceptable; also describe in words for safety. For Veo, request nearly static shots: 'locked camera, the only motion is a curtain moving, a car passing, a light turning on'. Exclude: 'crowd, lens flare, dramatic camera movement, text, neon glow overload, cartoon'.

## How generators get it wrong

The generator tends to deliver a polished 'cinematic neon diner' with teal-orange grade and smoky haze rather than flat, hard, daytime geometry. Fix by asking for plain daylight and flat colour planes and listing what is not there (no haze, no bloom). Figures often look at each other or the camera in a friendly way, destroying the isolation, so state where each looks. Veo adds slow cinematic dolly moves and soft focus. Signage text comes out garbled in a diner window.

## Production cost

Cheap in motion terms since stillness is the aesthetic, and a still shot with one animated detail is often the best Veo result. Character consistency is easier because figures are small, turned away or distant. The risk is monotony across a long film; it works best as one sequence or a cutaway layer.
