# Tinted silent film (1910s-20s)

> Black-and-white nitrate film dyed in single colours (amber for day, blue for night), with hand-cranked motion, iris shots and intertitles.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

In the silent era, prints were often chemically tinted (the whole image dyed in one colour) or toned: amber or sepia for interiors and daylight, blue for night, green for nature, red for fire or danger. Films had a squarer frame, hand-cranked movement that ran at variable speed, iris-in and iris-out transitions, theatrical acting, expressive shadows and written intertitle cards. It is the most formally stylised option in the family and can be used to make a political argument by exaggeration.

## Signature traits

- 1.33 to 1.37:1 frame with rounded corners and iris masks closing on a detail
- Black-and-white image dyed a single flat colour per scene (amber, blue, green, red)
- Strong chiaroscuro lighting, painted sets, kohl-darkened eyes, white-faced performers
- Exaggerated, gestural acting; broad pantomime in close-ups
- Intertitle cards with decorative borders and hand-lettered type, often with an ornate drawn frame
- Variable speed: slightly fast, jerky walking from hand cranking
- Nitrate damage: blotches, scratches, flicker, vertical lines

## References

- *Battleship Potemkin* — Sergei Eisenstein, 1925. The famous red flag hand-colouring in some prints and the montage of a crowd under violence make it the political model.
- *The Man with a Movie Camera* — Dziga Vertov, 1929. A city documentary built from rhythm and montage, showing silent-film technique used on ordinary working life.
- *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* — Robert Wiene, 1920. Expressionist painted sets and tinted prints exemplify the stylised, distorted look.
- *The Heart of the World* — Guy Maddin, 2000. A modern short that reproduces silent-era tinting, iris shots and frenzied editing at high speed in six minutes.
- *Nosferatu* — F. W. Murnau, 1922. Colour-tinted day/night scheme and expressionist shadows in a well-preserved feature.

## What it carries

Heightened, melodramatic, theatrical and archaic. It carries absurdity, grotesque caricature and political fury; it fights against sincerity, realism and anything that needs a natural voice.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Great for caricature: robber-baron villains with top hats, a banker as a vampire, the tycoon on the rooftop, with intertitles delivering the punchlines. It makes class conflict a pantomime, which suits satire. It would undermine a plain-spoken working-class voice, because its theatricality is distancing, and it needs the narration to become titles rather than a voice if kept pure.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Describe the print, not just the look: 'silent film 1920s, nitrate print, amber-tinted monochrome, 1.33:1, iris-in vignette, hand-cranked slightly fast movement, painted set, chiaroscuro, heavy white face makeup, exaggerated gesture'. For night: 'blue-tinted'. Generate as monochrome and apply the single-colour tint in the edit for consistency. Ask for 'frontal stage-like staging, static camera, theatrical performance'. Exclude: colour variety, modern faces and teeth, lens flare, shallow depth of field, sound, digital sharpness. For video, request 'jerky movement, slightly sped-up'. Generate intertitles in the edit.

## How generators get it wrong

The generated image is often a modern, sharp, colour picture with a sepia filter and an Instagram vignette. Performers look like modern actors in costume, with natural expressions and modern teeth; ask for exaggerated expression and 'stage makeup'. Veo gives smooth, natural movement instead of a hand-cranked rhythm; speed the clip in the edit and drop frames to 16-18 fps. The model adds sound and music, so mute. The tint should be consistent so it is better to apply it on the edit rather than request it per clip.

## Production cost

Moderate. The look hides some artefacts, but sets, costumes and acting need strong direction. Consistency is easier if tinting and grain are done in a single post pass. Intertitles are cheap (graphics). Longer sequences become tiring to watch, so it works best in short chapters or a cold open.
