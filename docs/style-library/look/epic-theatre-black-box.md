# Epic theatre black-box stage

> Actors on a bare black stage with chalk lines and one hard light, openly pretending the world is not there.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A stage-first look drawn from Brecht's epic theatre and minimalist theatre: no real set, a bare floor, props standing for places, performers in everyday clothes, and lighting that is visible as lighting. Lars von Trier's Dogville filmed a whole town as chalk lines on a soundstage, with walls absent so the audience sees through them. The artifice is the argument: we are watching a demonstration, not being drawn in. It suits moral cross-examination.

## Signature traits

- Pure black void background and black floor
- White chalk-line or tape floor marks for walls, streets and rooms
- A few real props (a table, a chair, a door frame) standing for entire locations
- Hard single-source top or side light with deep black shadow, a pool of light on the actors
- Static or slowly drifting overhead and eye-level wide shots showing all characters at once
- Ordinary contemporary clothes; direct address to camera allowed
- Captions or chapter cards announcing each scene like a stage placard

## References

- *Dogville* — Lars von Trier, 2003. Entire film on a soundstage with a chalk-outline town; the clearest screen version of this style.
- *The Threepenny Opera* — Bertolt Brecht and Kurt Weill, 1928. Source of epic theatre as political satire on capitalism, with placards and exposed stagecraft.
- *Manderlay* — Lars von Trier, 2005. Second film in the same bare-stage mode, applied to power and complicity.
- *Our Town* — Thornton Wilder, 1938. Landmark bare-stage play with no scenery, where a stage-manager narrator describes the world.

## What it carries

Cold, forensic, morally exposing; the viewer is asked to judge. It carries anger and shame without effects. It fights spectacle, warmth and immersion, and is hard to make funny unless the humour is deadpan.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Cross-examination stories where the point is who decided what: the doorstep, the boardroom, the benefits office as a bare stage with a few props. Fits a narrator with total authority. Undermines epic scale, fantasy, and anything that depends on a believable place.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana: 'bare black soundstage, white chalk lines on the black floor marking walls and doorways, single hard overhead spotlight, deep black shadows, actors in plain everyday clothes, wide static shot from above, 1.85:1'. Name the props and nothing else. Veo: 'camera locked or slow overhead drift, actors perform small naturalistic movements, chalk lines stay fixed'. Negative: 'detailed set, walls, background scenery, atmospheric haze, lens flare, colour grading, teal-orange'.

## How generators get it wrong

Models cannot resist adding walls, furniture and fog to the void; repeat 'empty black, nothing else'. Chalk lines wobble, change or vanish between frames in Veo, breaking the staging; keep shots short and the camera locked. Actors get cinematic polish and dramatic lighting rather than flat theatre light. Faces and clothes drift across clips, which shows more against an empty background.

## Production cost

The empty background simplifies the world but exposes any character inconsistency. Dialogue scenes need lip-sync care. Motion is modest, so eight-second clips suit well; floor-marking continuity is the main cost.
