# Film noir

> Black-and-white crime melodrama lit with hard top and side light, where the city is a trap and the powerful are never in the frame.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

US studio crime cinema of roughly 1941-1958, shot on black-and-white stock, with German Expressionist lighting carried over by emigre cinematographers. Low-key lighting built from hard sources, deep blacks, venetian-blind and stair-rail shadows, wet streets and rooms with low ceilings. Stories are about compromised people, money and a system that was rigged before the hero arrived. Canted angles and voice-over narration are part of the grammar, and that suits a narrator-led film very well. Neo-noir later reused the look in colour.

## Signature traits

- Hard single-source key light from above or the side, with deep unlit blacks and very little fill
- Shadow patterns thrown across faces and walls: venetian blinds, banisters, window frames, cigarette smoke in a light beam
- Black-and-white with a full range and crushed shadows; a fine film-grain texture
- Low camera angles that show ceilings, and Dutch (canted) tilts on moments of unease
- Deep-focus compositions with a foreground object (a desk lamp, a hand, a bottle) and a figure small in the background
- Wet night streets, reflective pavement, lit shop signs, car headlights
- Faces half in shadow, eyes lit by a thin strip of light
- 1.37:1 Academy frame, a squarer shape than modern 16:9

## References

- *The Third Man* — Carol Reed (cinematography Robert Krasker), 1949. Canted angles, wet cobbles and a racketeer story about who profits from other people's shortages
- *Double Indemnity* — Billy Wilder (cinematography John Seitz), 1944. Blinds-and-smoke lighting plus first-person confessional narration
- *Sweet Smell of Success* — Alexander Mackendrick (cinematography James Wong Howe), 1957. Neon night Manhattan and the power of a gatekeeper who can ruin anyone
- *Out of the Past* — Jacques Tourneur (cinematography Nicholas Musuraca), 1947. The textbook low-key look and a fatalist, doomed tone
- *Touch of Evil* — Orson Welles (cinematography Russell Metty), 1958. Wide-angle faces up close, a corrupt lawman and a long tracking opening

## What it carries

Fatalistic, sardonic, intimate and menacing. It carries dark humour and doom well and is good at making unseen money feel like weather. It resists warmth, hope and crowds, and a cheerful ending will read as false.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories of corruption, debt, rigged deals and the one honest person who can see it. It also suits a narrator who knows how it ends. It would undermine stories about collective action or solidarity, since noir is about isolated individuals. Heavy use may also read as an American period piece to a UK audience, so a British setting (fog, rain, bookie's, council offices) helps.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana handles 'black and white' and 'single hard key light, deep black shadows, venetian blind shadow pattern across the face' well, and describing the light direction and the shadow shape works better than the word 'noir', which tends to give a generic grey-teal mood. Describe the film stock ('fine 1940s black-and-white film grain, high contrast, crushed blacks') and the lens ('35mm lens, low angle, deep focus'). Put hard-light specifics first. Veo usually keeps monochrome in image-to-video from a B&W still, but occasionally drifts to a tint; keep motion instructions modest (slow push-in, smoke drifting) and add 'black and white' to the motion prompt as well. Veo also generates audio, so ask for quiet room tone and rain instead of jazz. Exclude (as phrasing for what you do want): describe the absence, e.g. 'unlit background, no colour', rather than a list of 'no' words.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators over-light: you get a brightly lit face with a 'noir' filter, grey midtones and soft fill, so the blacks are not black. Fix: specify a single source and say the rest of the room is unlit. Faces look too clean and smooth, with plastic skin; ask for film grain and skin texture. Venetian blind shadows often repeat in an obviously uniform pattern or fall on the wrong side. Women and men default to costume-party fedora and femme-fatale cliches, so describe ordinary clothes. Sepia or blue tints creep in. In video the shadows can crawl and flicker between frames.

## Production cost

Cheap on stills and one of the more consistent styles for character likeness, because monochrome hides colour drift. Hard-shadow patterns shift between shots, so continuity of the light direction needs to be repeated word for word in every prompt. Video holds up reasonably in 8-second clips. Wide shots of crowds in low light are the weak spot.
