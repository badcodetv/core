# Instant film (Polaroid and Instax)

> Square-ish, soft, colour-shifted snapshots with white borders, shot as private memory and pinned to a wall.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Instant photography, from the Polaroid SX-70 of the 1970s to Fuji Instax today, produces a single small positive print in minutes. Its look is soft focus, limited resolution, warm or green colour shifts and strong flash falloff, framed by a white border. Serious photographers used it too, including Walker Evans's late Polaroids (1973-74) and Andrei Tarkovsky's Italian Polaroids, collected in Instant Light. The frame says: this was somebody's real moment, private and unrepeatable.

## Signature traits

- Near-square frame (roughly 1:1 to 1.1:1) with a white Polaroid border, thicker at the bottom
- Soft focus and low micro-contrast; edges fall off and corners darken
- Colour casts: warm yellow-orange in tungsten rooms, green-cyan shadows, faded reds, milky blacks
- On-camera flash with bright subject and dark, muddy surround
- Chemical artefacts: developing marks, frame smudges, scratch lines, uneven edges
- Casual, personal subject matter: kitchens, cars, birthdays, rooms, a dog, a door
- Slightly crooked horizon, thumb-sized framing mistakes, and double exposures
- Props: the print held in a hand, taped on a fridge, or lying on a table

## References

- *Walker Evans Polaroids* — Walker Evans, 1973-74. A great documentary photographer using SX-70 to examine signs, objects and faces in colour.
- *Instant Light: Tarkovsky Polaroids* — Andrei Tarkovsky, 2006. Published collection of Tarkovsky's Polaroids from Russia and Italy, quiet, domestic and melancholy.
- *Memento* — Christopher Nolan, 2000. Uses the Polaroid as proof, memory and manipulation, and shows how a viewer reads one.
- *Uncommon Places* — Stephen Shore, 1982. Not Polaroid by process, but the colour snapshot-as-art tradition that Polaroid work sits within.

## What it carries

Intimate, fond, fragile and a little sad, and it reads as authentic evidence. It carries memory, family, loss and the idea of 'this really happened'. It fights scale, spectacle and epic power: the format is small.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Flashback and personal-proof sequences: the family before the factory closed, the evidence in a shoebox, the time before the product. Good as inserts inside a different main look, a way to say 'here is the receipt'. It undermines anything epic, anything that needs sharp detail, and a full film in it would feel twee and be tiring to watch.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana renders this well: 'Polaroid SX-70 instant photograph with white border, soft focus, faded colours, warm cast, on-camera flash, slightly crooked, scan of the physical print on a wooden table'. Specify the frame and the border; without them it gives a normal photo with a filter. Negatives: 'no sharp digital detail, no HDR, no perfect symmetry, no modern phone look'. Veo animates a print poorly if you ask for the content to move inside the border and keep the border fixed. Better is to ask for 'a hand lifts the print from the table' or a slow push onto the print, and to cut to a full-frame clip separately.

## How generators get it wrong

The most common failure is a clean modern photo with a decorative white frame stuck on, because the model treats 'Polaroid' as a border filter. Real instant film has low sharpness and strong colour casts, so ask for those directly. Border proportions often come out wrong. In video, the image inside the border tends to sharpen and become cinematic, which breaks the look. Faces sometimes look too polished.

## Production cost

Stills are easy and cheap. Video is awkward: motion inside a print breaks the illusion, so use stills, hand-held-print shots and cutaways. Consistency across shots is good because the look is simple, but recurring characters still need references.
