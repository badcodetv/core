# WPA / New Deal screenprint poster

> Flat, hopeful, heavy-shouldered figures in a few saturated screenprint colours, with the dignity-of-work look of 1930s American public posters.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Federal arts programmes in 1930s America commissioned posters for travel, health, safety and public works, printed by silkscreen in a handful of flat inks. The style monumentalises ordinary workers and public infrastructure: strong shapes, low horizon, big sky, simple type. Its political message is that the state builds things for the people, which makes it a rare style that is optimistic about collective action. The related Rural Electrification posters add a modernist, geometric edge.

## Signature traits

- Flat screenprint colour in 3 to 6 inks, slightly matte, edges a little soft where ink meets paper
- Low camera and a big sky; figures and structures are monumental against the horizon
- Simplified, rounded-block figures with smooth shapes and minimal facial detail
- Strong silhouette and graded sky bands (stepped, not smooth gradients)
- Bold, simple lettering in a clean block capital style at the bottom or top
- Heroic subjects: dams, power lines, mills, farm workers, bridges
- Limited, earthy palette: ochre, rust red, teal, cream, deep blue

## References

- *Rural Electrification Administration posters* — Lester Beall, 1937. A set of modernist screenprint posters promoting public electric power for farms; flat geometry and political purpose in one.
- *See America series (WPA National Parks and travel posters)* — Federal Art Project, 1930s. The best known set of WPA screenprints; defines the flat, bold, sky-heavy look.
- *We Can Do It!* — J. Howard Miller (for Westinghouse), 1943. The wartime worker poster that shows the register's collective-effort optimism, though not itself a WPA piece.
- *This Is Nazi Brutality* — Ben Shahn / Office of War Information, 1943. Shows the same family used for hard-edged propaganda, flat colour and a single image.

## What it carries

Hopeful, dignified, communal, proud, a little nostalgic. It carries earnest pride in work and public good and can sting when undercut by narration that says what happened next. It fights against cynicism that isn't ironic, and against fine detail.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A story that sets the promise (the state builds, the factory feeds a town) against the outcome, using irony: the poster-perfect past against the grey present. It suits the voice of a narrator who knows how the optimistic picture went wrong. It is American and may feel foreign to a UK reader unless the subject is made British (pit villages, shipyards, the NHS). It would undermine grim intimate stories or comedy that needs to be sharp and tabloid.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Nano Banana handles this well: '1930s WPA screenprint poster, flat colour, limited palette of ochre, rust and teal, matte ink, monumental low-angle figures, stepped sky bands rather than gradients, simple block lettering'. Name the subject as public works and describe the setting concretely (a dam, a power pylon line, a shipyard crane). Exclude: 'photorealistic, soft gradients, lens flare, 3D render, modern flat vector icons, drop shadows'. For video, ask for 'locked camera, slow drift of cloud bands and smoke, figures static'; Veo often adds volumetric light and camera push that spoil the flatness.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators drift to a glossy retro-vector 'mid-century' style with smooth gradients, lens-like light and cartoon proportions. Faces become modern and the monumental low angle gets lost in favour of a flat eye-level. Words on the poster come out garbled. Fix by adding the stepped-band sky instruction, forbidding smooth gradients, and adding type in post. Americana creeps in (stars and stripes, cowboys) unless the setting is explicitly British.

## Production cost

Stills are cheap and consistent; a flat-colour palette and heroic silhouettes hold across a series. Motion is limited: it reads best as slow cloud, smoke and steam movement behind static figures, which Veo can do if told to lock the camera. Whole-body animated walking in this style tends to lose the flat look.
