# Spaghetti western

> Sun-scorched widescreen with extreme close-ups of eyes, long silences and operatic, cynical violence.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Italian-made westerns of the mid-1960s to mid-1970s, shot cheaply in Spain, Italy and Almeria's desert and in widescreen (Techniscope). They traded the American western's moral certainty for mercenary cynicism: everyone is chasing money, and institutions (army, church, railroad, bank) are as corrupt as the outlaws. The grammar is the wide landscape against the extreme close-up of eyes, the stretched stand-off, and sound used like music. Post-dubbed dialogue gives it a slightly detached feel.

## Signature traits

- Extreme close-up of the eyes only, cut against a wide shot of tiny figures in a huge landscape
- 2.35:1 anamorphic-style widescreen, with the subject often at the edge of the frame and empty space beside
- Hard overhead sun, squinting faces, sweat, cracked lips, dust on skin and stubble
- Bleached ochre, rust and bone-white palette with saturated red/blue accent (a poncho, a sky)
- Slow zoom in on a face during a stand-off, and long takes where nothing happens
- Low-angle shots of boots, guns, spurs and hands before a draw
- Weathered, grotesque faces; dirty period costume; wide-brimmed hats casting a shadow across the eyes
- Church bells, wind and a single object (a pocket watch) as the sound/image motif

## References

- *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* — Sergio Leone (cinematography Tonino Delli Colli), 1966. The standard for eyes-and-landscape framing and the three-way stand-off, with a Civil War greed backdrop
- *Once Upon a Time in the West* — Sergio Leone, 1968. A railroad baron buying up land: the nearest to a political-economy western
- *A Fistful of Dollars* — Sergio Leone, 1964. A stranger plays two rival gangs against each other for profit
- *The Great Silence* — Sergio Corbucci, 1968. A snow western in which bounty hunting is legalised murder, ending in defeat
- *Django* — Sergio Corbucci, 1966. Mud, a dragged coffin and a ruined town; shows the grimy, cheap end of the style

## What it carries

Wry, patient and mythic, with the grandeur of a joke told very slowly. It can hold menace, absurd greed and rough comic timing. It fights against warmth, speed and anything small and domestic.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Showdowns between a lone figure and an owner-class: landlord vs tenant, bank vs town, the stand-off over who owns the land or the water. Strong for stories about greed with no moral high ground. It would undermine stories where the moral centre is a crowd, and it sets a 'the lone gunman fixes it' frame that works against a collective-action argument. A British audience will read it as pastiche, which is fine for satire (Wild West on a Swindon retail park).

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Describe the scale contrast explicitly: 'extreme close-up of narrowed blue eyes filling a 2.39:1 frame, dust, hard overhead sunlight, sweat' and, separately, 'extreme wide shot, tiny figure in vast bleached desert'. Name the lens/format ('anamorphic widescreen, 2.35:1, shallow focus on the eyes, soft film grain, 1960s Technicolor-era print with faded warm colours') rather than the director. Flow outputs 16:9 or 9:16, so a true 2.35:1 is cropped or letterboxed in post; prompt the composition for a wide strip (subject low in frame, lots of empty sky) so the crop works. Veo is good at slow push-ins and wind, dust and heat shimmer; ask for 'very slow push-in, locked focus, nothing else moves' to get the stretched stand-off. Veo's audio may add generic cowboy music; ask for wind and silence. Characters with guns can trigger policy blocks (see Flow policy notes), so keep it to the stand-off, hands and holsters, and avoid showing a shot hitting someone.

## How generators get it wrong

Generators give clean 'Hollywood western' costumes, pretty golden-hour sunsets, and symmetrical heroic poses instead of dirt, sweat and squinting; add specifics of grime and harsh midday sun. The extreme close-up of eyes often comes out as a normal portrait with a wide face; specify 'only the eyes and brow fill the frame'. Hands and revolvers deform. Faces turn the same handsome, squarish 'cowboy' type, losing the grotesque character faces. Colours drift to teal and orange, instead of bleached ochre. Stand-off clips drift into action: Veo adds movement that was not asked for.

## Production cost

Moderate. Landscapes are cheap and consistent, and eyes-only close-ups are forgiving for character continuity. Weak points are hands, props and horses in motion, and keeping a wide shot's tiny figures recognisable as the same character. Dialogue with lip-sync is harder than the style's usual dubbed detachment, but dubbed-feel narration over silent faces works naturally here.
