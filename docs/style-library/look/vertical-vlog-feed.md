# Vertical vlog and feed video

> 9:16 handheld selfie footage, fast jump-cuts, burned-in captions and direct address, built for a phone scroll.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The visual grammar of TikTok, Reels and YouTube Shorts: a person speaking to the phone, close, shot at arm's length or on a tripod, edited to a jump-cut rhythm with bold captions, stickers and trending sounds. Quibi tried to professionalise it for scripted drama in 2020; creator-native series such as Skibidi Toilet showed the grammar can carry an ongoing narrative. It is already how much of the target audience consumes news and opinion.

## Signature traits

- 9:16 vertical frame, face filling the upper two-thirds
- Selfie angle from slightly below or at arm's length, wide-angle phone lens distortion
- Jump cuts every 1-3 seconds removing all pauses
- Large burned-in word-by-word captions, often in bold sans with outline
- Direct address to lens: 'Right, so'; hook line in the first second
- Ordinary domestic backgrounds: bedroom, car, kitchen, street, with natural or ring-light glare
- Stickers, emoji, zoom punches and sound-effect stings
- Overlays: screenshot of a headline, a reaction cut-in, split-screen duet

- *Casey Neistat daily vlogs* — Casey Neistat (YouTube), 2015. The influential daily-vlog edit grammar of jump cuts, direct address and music, though shot horizontally.
- *Most Dangerous Game* — Nick Santora (Quibi), 2020. Early attempt at prestige thriller grammar adapted to the phone and the vertical frame.

## What it carries

Intimate, urgent, chatty, funny, conspiratorial. Carries 'let me explain this to you' and fast payoffs. Fights against slow, atmospheric work and anything that needs wide space or silence.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Short, direct explainers in the narrator's voice, plus clipped promos and reaction-style stories like a worker filming a day of cuts. Native to where the target viewer already is, so good for distribution cuts of a larger film. Undermines epic or patient storytelling, and it can feel like an influencer-style sell if the voice is not sharp. Direct address needs a face: the speaker must be a believable character, not a blandly polished presenter.

## Asking Flow (Nano Banana stills, Veo clips) for it

Set the aspect ratio to 9:16 for both Nano Banana and Veo and describe it as phone footage: 'vertical selfie video, front camera, slight wide-angle distortion, person talking to camera, messy bedroom background, ring-light reflection in eyes, handheld wobble, natural phone colour'. Keep each Veo clip to a single continuous take and build the jump-cuts in the edit. Burn captions in post. Exclude: tripod-stable cinematic look, shallow depth of field, studio lighting, text and logos in frame, app interface elements.

## How generators get it wrong

The faces come out too polished and influencer-perfect with smoothed skin. Speech mouths drift out of sync, and Veo's speaking voices sound uniform, so the 'real person' texture is easy to lose; record or dub the voice separately and keep lip-sync clips short. Vertical framing sometimes crops heads or produces odd background perspective. Captions in generated frames are garbled; always overlay. The format's authenticity cues (stutters, mistakes, ums) are absent unless scripted.

## Production cost

Cheap per clip since a single face and a bedroom are simple, but the format needs a consistent, believable presenter across many posts and heavy editing rhythm. Output length is no issue because the grammar is built on 3 to 8 second beats. Main cost is editing time for captions, cuts and sound.
