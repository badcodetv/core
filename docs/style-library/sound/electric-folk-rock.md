# Electric folk-rock

> Traditional songs and tunes played by a rock band with electric guitar, fiddle and a woman or man singing straight.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Born in 1969 when Fairport Convention made Liege & Lief and Steeleye Span and others followed, electric folk-rock plugged old ballads and dance tunes into drums, bass and electric guitar. It keeps modal tunes and narrative lyrics but adds drive, riffs and sometimes lead guitar solos. It has two moods: stately and ancient (Steeleye Span) and dark urban-rural weariness (Richard and Linda Thompson). Where brass band is municipal, this is the countryside as pub with amps.

## Signature traits

- Modal tunes (Dorian, Mixolydian) riffed on electric guitar with a clean, slightly jangly tone
- Fiddle and/or hurdy-gurdy trading lines with the guitar, often in unison
- Live-sounding drums with a tight shuffle or straight 4/4, bass walking under
- Vocals sung with a plain, English, unornamented tone, sometimes in unison harmony
- Long narrative ballad lyrics, 6 to 10 verses, with instrumental breaks between
- Mid-tempo 90 to 130 bpm, with a final speed-up jig or reel section
- Warm analogue 1970s room sound, tape saturation, minimal compression

## References

- *Liege & Lief* — Fairport Convention, 1969. The founding electric folk album, old ballads with a rock band
- *All Around My Hat* — Steeleye Span, 1975. Folk-rock got to the UK top five; chorus and fiddle drive
- *I Want to See the Bright Lights Tonight* — Richard and Linda Thompson, 1974. Darker, wry, working-class and urban edge to the sound
- *Muswell Hillbillies* — The Kinks, 1971. Roots-music-hall hybrid about the displaced working class

## What it carries

Weighty, rueful and sing-along at once; it makes old injustice sound current and allows a build to a finale. It carries anger and humour. It fights against fast edits and ironic distance because its sincerity is the point.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Longer pieces that need to build: a story of one family or town over time, a betrayal, an ending with a rolling jig. Good for a narrator-from-the-future ballad with a tale. It would undermine anything needing short, jokey punchlines, or an urban-youth voice.

## Asking Suno for it

Separate instruments: 'Telecaster-style clean jangle, fiddle, bass, brushed then driving drums'. Lyric structure: ballad verses with an [Instrumental Break] fiddle and a [Jig Outro] tag. Exclude: autotune, trap, EDM, synth pads, modern pop vocals, glossy compression, American twang, banjo.

## How generators get it wrong

The model slides toward American country or Mumford-style stomp-folk (banjo, gang shouts, big claps) and 'epic' cinematic Celtic. Vocals become slick and American. Fix: use 'English', 'no banjo', 'no stomp-clap', 'no cinematic', name the era (1970s, tape) and the fiddle/guitar unison; keep lyric verses long and unrhymed in odd places to avoid pop hooks. Tunes often resolve in major; ask for 'minor modal, Dorian'.

## Production cost

Medium. A band sound is reliable once found, but keeping the same singer, the same fiddle tone and the same mix across a film's takes is hard. Instrumental breaks and ending jig make the song longer than a short needs, so cut points must be planned.
