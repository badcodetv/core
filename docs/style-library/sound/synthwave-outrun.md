# Synthwave / outrun

> A modern revival of 1980s film-score synths and gated drums, neon-lit and nostalgic for a decade that the listener often did not live through.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A 2000s-2010s genre that imitates 1980s film and TV scores (John Carpenter, Vangelis, car-chase and action soundtracks) using analog-style synths, gated-reverb drums and driving arpeggios. It was popularised by the 2011 film Drive and games like Hotline Miami. It is a style about a mood of fictional 1980s, not the real 1980s. It is the most commercially known style in this family and the one most likely to read as a cliché.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 90-130 BPM, steady kick and a big gated-reverb snare
- Pulsing, sequenced analog bass in 16th notes
- Lush detuned saw pads and sweeping arpeggios
- Lead synth melody with a sad, heroic major/minor feel, often with a vibrato
- Sidechained or driving mix with heavy reverb and chorus on everything
- Sparse or vocoded vocals, or instrumental
- Cinematic build structure with a breakdown and a final lift

## References

- *Nightcall* — Kavinsky, 2010. The track that defined the revival, famously on the Drive soundtrack.
- *Halloween Theme* — John Carpenter, 1978. The source style: a repeating minimal synth pulse used as a film score.
- *Blade Runner (Original Soundtrack)* — Vangelis, 1982. The cinematic template: heavy pads, slow drama, long reverb. **(Corrected: Vangelis scored the film in 1982, but the soundtrack album was not officially released until 1994 (a 1982 'New American Orchestra' cover version circulated). Cite as 1982 score, 1994 album.)**
- *Dangerous Days* — Perturbator, 2014. A dark, driving, cinematic synthwave album.
- *Galactic Melt* — Com Truise, 2011. A more melodic, lo-fi, retro-futurist version of the genre.

## What it carries

Can carry romantic melancholy, lone-hero swagger, noir dread, retro-futurist longing. It fights against dry humour and against any attempt to look real or contemporary. It tends to flatter the listener's nostalgia.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories with a lone protagonist or a heist/chase structure, night-time city imagery, or the narrator as a cool figure from a future that already happened. It would undermine a down-to-earth working-class story, since neon cool is the opposite of a real high street, and the style is so familiar that it reads as borrowed.

Use [Instrumental] or sparse vocals with a vocoder or deadpan voice. Exclude: modern EDM drop, trap, autotune, big pop chorus, acoustic, guitar solo (unless wanted). Tag sections with [Intro], [Build], [Breakdown], [Final Lift] to control the arc.

## How generators get it wrong

This is the one style where Suno is already at home, so it tends to produce the same polished, neon, midtempo 'Drive'-like track every time, with the same arpeggio and a breathy female vocal. The failure is sameness and cliché across takes. Fix by changing the central mood word set and the tempo and by naming a different lead timbre (brass-like synth, FM bell) per story, or avoid the style if the story is grounded. Vocals often become generic dreamy pop.

## Production cost

Cheapest and most reliable to generate, which is also its risk: consistency is high, distinctiveness is low. Good for multi-cue consistency inside one story if the tempo and lead are varied deliberately.
