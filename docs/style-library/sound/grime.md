# Grime

> Aggressive, sparse, roughly 140 BPM London MC music built on cold synth stabs, sub-bass and rapid-fire spoken-sung bars delivered in a raw local accent.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Grime emerged around 2002 in east London (Bow, Bromley-by-Bow, Plaistow) from garage, jungle and dancehall, driven by pirate radio, clashes and cheap software like Fruity Loops. Its beat is stripped back: a syncopated kick and snare around 140 BPM with no four-to-the-floor pulse, cold square-wave or 8-bit synth lines, and heavy sub-bass. The MC is the point, writing about estates, money, police and ambition in a flat, fast, unglossed accent. It was a working-class art form first and a chart genre later, which gives it unusual credibility with exactly the audience BadCode is addressing.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 138 to 142 BPM, felt in a half-time 'eskimo' skank with a snare on beat 3 and a syncopated kick
- Cold, thin square-wave, saw and 8-bit style synth stabs and a minimal melodic riff, often a single repeating phrase
- Deep sine sub-bass that drops in and out, leaving gaps of near silence
- Dry, upfront vocal with little reverb, bars in 4/8/16 length, flat London delivery, often with a shouted hook or ad-libs
- Sparse arrangement: usually four or five elements at any time, no big melodic chorus
- Sound effects such as gunshot-style reloads, sirens, string stabs and orchestral hits used as punctuation
- Instrumental 'eski' versions that work as a hard bed under a narrator

## References

- *Boy in da Corner* — Dizzee Rascal, 2003. The founding album; raw bedroom-produced beats and a teenage east London voice describing estate life.
- *Eskimo (instrumental)* — Wiley, 2002. The template for the cold, sparse, off-kilter 140 BPM sound.
- *That's Not Me* — Skepta (feat. JME), 2014. Modern grime that is minimal and abrasive and openly about class and refusing to dress up success.
- *Konnichiwa* — Skepta, 2016. Mercury Prize winning album proving grime could carry an album-length argument.
- *Home Sweet Home* — Kano, 2005. Album showing the more melodic, storytelling end of the genre.

## What it carries

Confrontational, urgent, defiant, funny in a dry way. It carries anger, swagger and survival humour well. It fights against gentleness, nostalgia and anything that sounds like an authority figure talking down.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire that is angry at a named decision-maker: landlords, banks, a minister, a billionaire. A hook that is a flat, repeatable accusation. The audience (working-class UK) hears this as their own sound. It would undermine slow, tender or elegiac stories, stories that need a voice from above (the future narrator speaking calmly), or anything where an MC voice delivered by a synthetic singer would sound like a parody of the people it claims to speak for.

## Asking Suno for it

In the Suno Style box, name the facts: 'UK grime, 140 BPM, sparse half-time drums, cold square-wave synth stabs, deep sub-bass, dry aggressive male MC, London accent, raw'. Describing instruments and tempo works better than genre names alone, and living artist names are ignored or blocked. Put 'melodic chorus, autotune, trap hi-hat rolls, American accent, EDM drop, pop' in Exclude. Write lyrics in bar-length lines, one line per bar, and tag sections [Verse], [Hook]. Short lines of about 8 to 10 syllables keep the flow tight; long lines get rushed into mush. Instrumental-only prompts ('instrumental, no vocals') give a usable narrator bed.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to slide towards American trap (rolling hi-hats, 808 glides) and melodic rap-pop with a sung chorus; it also tends to give a smooth, mid-Atlantic accent rather than a flat London one and to even out the swing so it sounds like a metronome. Fix by naming 'no hi-hat rolls', 'half-time skank' and 'London accent', keeping the arrangement to a few elements, and rerolling for the voice. Dense multi-syllable flow of a real MC is rarely reproduced; write simpler bars. The audience will notice a fake accent quickly, so test the voice early.

## Production cost

Voice consistency across takes is the main cost: accent, timbre and delivery drift between generations, so one story needs one chosen voice kept through every take. Instrumentals are easy and consistent. Vocal bars need rerolls to land on the beat. Length is fine for 2 to 3 minute tracks.
