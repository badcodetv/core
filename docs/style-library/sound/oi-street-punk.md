# Oi! and street punk

> Stripped-back, chanted, football-terrace punk about work, pubs, police and the dole, sung by people from the estates.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A 1979-82 offshoot of punk from London and the Midlands and North that favoured working-class solidarity over art-school provocation. Bands like Cockney Rejects, Sham 69, Angelic Upstarts, Cock Sparrer and the 4-Skins mixed punk with terrace chants and pub-rock plainness, with a bigger, slower, singalong feel. It was compromised by far-right associations in the early 80s, which matters for how it is read now.

## Signature traits

- Tempo 130-170 bpm, mid-fast, with a stomping rather than racing feel
- Big gang vocals in unison on choruses, football-chant melodies, hands-in-the-air 'oi' shouts
- Meaty, plain guitar with little distortion variety; chords from an A-D-E rock vocabulary
- Heavy kick-snare backbeat, simple fills, sometimes a rolling bass line
- Lead vocal gruff, working-class London or northern accent, shouted rather than sung
- Lyrics about the factory, the pub, the dole queue, the police, loyalty to mates
- Dry and live-sounding band-in-a-room recording

## References

- *If the Kids Are United* — Sham 69, 1978. The model unity chant, with a huge singalong chorus.
- *Teenage Warning* — Angelic Upstarts, 1979. Class politics from a South Shields working-class band.
- *Shock Troops* — Cock Sparrer, 1983. Terrace-anthem street punk, still sung in pubs.
- *Greatest Hits Vol. 1* — Cockney Rejects, 1980. Archetype of the East End street-punk sound.

## What it carries

Solidarity, loyalty, defiance, humour and a pub-night warmth. It carries 'we are the ones doing the work' very well and comes with beer-and-mates warmth that a shouted solo voice lacks. It fights against subtlety, irony and anything that sounds educated.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about workers, estates, redundancy, zero-hours work and being ignored, where the point is that people are together. It would undermine a story about migrants, race or national identity, given the genre's history with far-right capture and the reader brief's warning against feeding nativism. It also undermines anything delicate or ironic.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to: British Oi! street punk, 150 bpm, gang vocals, football chant chorus, gruff working-class London male lead, crunchy mid-gain guitar, live pub recording, stomping backbeat. Name the gang vocal explicitly and use [Chorus] with 'all together' lines for repeated unison phrases. Exclude: pop punk, emo, synths, falsetto, polished, metal, screamo, American accent. Do not write nationalist or skinhead-coded lyrics; keep them to work, wages and landlords.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno gangs vocals are often thin (two or three voices layered, not twenty) and the chanting turns into a choir; ask for 'crowd shouting along, pub, many men' and exclude choir. It slips to generic skate punk or even metalcore if tempo and accent are loose. The lead can come out as friendly pop voice with no grit; add 'gruff, hoarse, shouting'.

## Production cost

Cheap and consistent. Gang vocals are the hard part to keep realistic across takes. Visual pairing (terraces, estates, council flats) is easy but must avoid skinhead iconography.
