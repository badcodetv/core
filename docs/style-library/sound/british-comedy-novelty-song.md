# British comedy novelty song

> A fast, story-driven comic song in music-hall, skiffle or brass-band clothes, performed with a straight face and a regional accent.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A British tradition running from music hall and Noel Coward's patter songs through 1960s and 70s chart novelties. The song is a short narrative with a sting in the last verse, sung in a broad local accent, over an oompah, honky-tonk piano, brass or banjo arrangement. The tune is deliberately simple and bouncy so the words land. It is the main British format for delivering a grim joke as a singalong.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 110-150 BPM, two-beat oompah or shuffle feel with a bouncing bass line
- Honky-tonk or upright piano, tuba or sousaphone, banjo, kazoo, brass band or Hammond organ as the lead colour
- Verse-verse-chorus with a spoken or half-spoken patter section and a punchline in the last verse
- Vocal is conversational, clearly enunciated, regional accent (West Country, Cockney, Northern), little vibrato
- Key changes up a tone or a mock-dramatic slowdown before the final line
- Stop-start arrangement: the band cuts out so the punchline is heard alone
- Short length, usually under 3 minutes, with a call-and-response or crowd chorus on the hook

## References

- *Ernie (The Fastest Milkman in the West)* — Benny Hill, 1971. Pure narrative comic song with a dark sting at the end, number one at Christmas 1971.
- *Right Said Fred* — Bernard Cribbins, 1962. Working-class labour as farce, patter verses over a jaunty band.
- *Combine Harvester (Brand New Key)* — The Wurzels, 1976. Regional accent and rural setting as the vehicle; a chart number one built on a singalong.
- *Mad Dogs and Englishmen* — Noel Coward, 1931. The patter-song ancestor: crisp, authoritative, and contemptuous of its subject in the politest possible voice.

## What it carries

Warm, communal and cheeky, with a bite hidden behind the bounce. It can carry rage as long as the delivery stays cheerful. It fights against solemnity: any earnest lyric sung this way turns into a joke at the singer's expense.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Strong for a punchline story with a clear villain and a sting, such as a landlord, a boss or a bank, in which the audience laughs first and gets angry afterwards. Good for a narrator-persona that wants to sound like the pub's resident storyteller. It would undermine any piece whose emotional payoff is grief, awe or tenderness, and a long argument that needs more than three verses.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds well to named instrument sets and an era, so a style box such as: 'British music hall comedy song, honky-tonk upright piano, tuba oompah bass, banjo, brass band, kazoo, bouncy 2/4 shuffle, 130 BPM, spoken-sung patter verses, broad West Country male vocal, big crowd singalong chorus, 1970s novelty record' tends to work. Put the punchline in its own short final section tag, and use [Spoken] for patter. Accent control is partial: Suno will pick up 'Northern English' or 'Cockney' as a hint but drifts toward neutral or American. Exclude: 'auto-tune, trap hi-hats, EDM drop, polished pop production, American accent, rap, power ballad'. Lyrics must scan strictly (same syllables per line across verses) or the tune collapses into stilted prose.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno writes the joke too politely: it keeps a pop-song rhythm and sounds like a children's party rather than a comedy record. Humour comes from timing, and the engine rarely stops the band for a beat, so the punchline is buried under the backing. Accents slide back to generic. Lyrics it generates itself are weak rhymes with no turn; write the words yourself. Fix: write the final-line pause into the lyrics with a tag or a line break, ask for 'band stops, solo voice' on the last line, and keep the vocal front-and-centre.

## Production cost

Cheap and fast: one voice, one short song, instruments Suno understands. The hard part is consistency of accent and persona across several songs. Takes need many re-rolls to get the stop-start punchline right. Length is naturally under 3 minutes, which suits the engine.
