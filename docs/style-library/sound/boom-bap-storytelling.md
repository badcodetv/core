# Boom bap storytelling rap

> Sample-based, swung drums and a dense, rhymed narrative verse, the 90s New York template for telling a story in character.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Boom bap grew out of late 80s and early 90s New York production: a kick and snare break chopped from old records, dusty jazz or soul loops, and a rapper writing multi-syllable internal rhyme. The name is the sound of the kick (boom) and snare (bap). Storytelling is a sub-tradition where each verse is a scene with a character, a turn and a consequence. It is the most listenable form for dense, rhymed argument because the beat is sparse enough to leave the lyric in front.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 85 to 95 BPM, swung 16th feel with the snare on 2 and 4
- Chopped sample loop of 2 to 4 bars: a muted piano, jazz guitar or soul horn stab with vinyl crackle
- Hard, slightly distorted kick and a crisp, dry snare with little reverb
- Bassline is a simple repeating sub or upright bass figure
- Verses of 16 bars, 4-beat lines, with internal and multisyllabic rhyme
- Vocal dry and close, mid-forward, doubled only on the last word of a line
- Record scratches or a cut-up vocal sample for the hook
- Sparse arrangement: the loop drops out for a bar before a punchline

## References

- *Illmatic* — Nas, 1994. The reference for scene-by-scene street storytelling over jazzy loops
- *Mass Appeal* — Gang Starr, 1994. Archetypal boom bap drums, sparse loop and a pointed critique of the industry
- *C.R.E.A.M.* — Wu-Tang Clan, 1993. A piano loop under a hook that is itself an economic argument
- *Fight the Power* — Public Enemy, 1989. Sample-collage version where the beat is as loud as the politics

## What it carries

Authoritative, knowing and a little weary; it can carry anger, grim humour and a plain-spoken moral. It fights against softness and anything that needs warmth or ambiguity, and its US idiom can sound borrowed on a British story.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories that are literally told in scenes (a character, a bad decision, a consequence), and arguments built as lists or ledgers. The hook-as-slogan structure suits an economic point. It would undermine intimate or uncertain material, and a British working-class story if the accent and slang drift to American.

## Asking Suno for it

In the Suno Style box, name the elements rather than an artist: 'boom bap hip-hop, 90 BPM, dusty sampled piano loop, hard dry drums, vinyl crackle, upright bass, male rapper, dry close vocal, internal rhyme'. Use [Verse] and [Hook] tags and keep each line to a similar syllable count so the flow stays on the grid. Put 'trap hi-hats, autotune, EDM, pop chorus, singing, 808 sub' in Exclude. Say the accent explicitly if it matters (for example 'British accent') but expect inconsistency across takes. Spell-check unusual words phonetically in lyrics.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno rap tends to fall into a generic, evenly stressed delivery with end-of-line rhymes only, and rhymes are often forced or filler. The hook usually gets sung melodically even in a rap track. Sampled-loop texture can come out too clean or too modern. Fix: write multisyllabic rhyme into the lyrics yourself, keep line lengths consistent, add 'vinyl crackle, lo-fi, dusty' and exclude 'singing, melodic hook', and make the hook a spoken shout line by tagging it [Chant] or [Spoken].

## Production cost

Moderate. Vocal consistency across several verses is hard because each generation can change the voice and flow; plan for one long take or extending rather than stitching. Rhythmic locking to picture needs a fixed tempo, and a dense verse often has words that the model slurs, so expect several re-rolls per verse.
