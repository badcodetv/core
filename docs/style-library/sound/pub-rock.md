# Pub rock and rhythm-and-blues rock

> Lean, swinging, bar-band rock and roll with sharp working-class storytelling, the quieter ancestor of punk.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A 1972-77 London and Essex scene (Dr Feelgood, Eddie and the Hot Rods, Brinsley Schwarz, Ian Dury and the Blockheads) that took American R&B back to the pub, with small venues and cheap records, in reaction to bloated stadium rock. Dr Feelgood from Canvey Island, with their stark suits and Wilko Johnson's choppy guitar, are the signature sound. Ian Dury's Blockheads added funk and music-hall wit, with lyrics of a London working life. It is the missing link between the pub and punk and has a humane, comic edge.

## Signature traits

- Tempo 120-170 bpm, a driving shuffle or straight boogie rhythm
- Choppy rhythm-and-lead guitar played in a single, trebly, clipped tone (Telecaster-style)
- Harmonica and piano or sax in the arrangement
- Raw bluesy lead vocal, spoken-sung, in a London or Essex accent
- Tight live band recording, almost no studio treatment
- Lyrics: stories about work, women, drink, landlords, the street
- Short songs with a stop-start groove

## References

- *Down by the Jetty* — Dr. Feelgood, 1975. The archetype: stark, lean, live-in-a-room R&B from Canvey Island.
- *New Boots and Panties!!* — Ian Dury, 1977. Music-hall wit, funk and London working-class storytelling.
- *Teenage Depression* — Eddie and the Hot Rods, 1976. The speed-up that fed straight into punk.
- *Stupidity* — Dr. Feelgood, 1976. A live album capturing the bar-band energy.

## What it carries

Gritty, funny, sociable, resilient; a drink in your hand. It carries humane storytelling and dry comedy; it fights against cold abstraction or apocalyptic grandeur.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Plain-spoken, character-led satires about landlords, bosses, bailiffs and the bar-stool view of the economy, and for narration with a talking voice over a groove. It would undermine futuristic, superintelligence-from-the-future framing unless used as deliberate contrast, and it will not carry heavy spectacle.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno: 1975 British pub rock, 140 bpm, choppy clipped Telecaster guitar, harmonica, driving shuffle drums, raw bluesy spoken-sung male vocal, Essex accent, live in a room, dry. For Ian Dury-style variants, ask for funk bass, saxophone, music-hall talky vocal. Exclude: pop, polished, autotune, electronic, metal, heavy distortion, American accent, country twang. Write lyrics as a story with place names and objects.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno slips to generic blues rock or American roots-rock with a southern drawl; naming the British accent and 1975 helps, but harmonica and shuffle may be missed. Vocals come out too smooth and clean; ask for hoarse, raw, talky. Sax and piano can swamp the clipped guitar, which is the sound's identity.

## Production cost

Cheap and consistent. A talky vocal is difficult to keep stable across takes. Visual pairing (pubs, caffs, suits, tungsten light) is easy.
