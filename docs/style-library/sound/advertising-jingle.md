# Advertising jingle and sung slogan

> A 15-30 second earworm built around a brand-style slogan, sung bright and sold hard.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The commercial jingle: a short, melodically simple tune with the product name repeated in the hook, usually a sung slogan plus a tag. In Britain it was a mid-century TV and radio institution, and a satirical story can use the form to sell the 'product' of a bad idea. Its power is that people know the grammar instantly and so the sinister version reads at once. Everything is engineered for memorability: a rising hook, repeated phrase and bright major key.

## Signature traits

- 15-35 seconds total, with the slogan repeated at least twice and a final 'tag' line
- Major key, simple stepwise melody, hook that rises on the brand name
- Bright, forward instrumentation: brass stabs, bouncy piano, jangly guitar, handclaps, tubular bells or a chorus of sunny backing vocals
- Lead vocal polished, smiling, close-miked; backing vocals in tight harmony on the slogan
- Voice-over line spoken over the final bars with a mock-official tone
- Hard stop on a final chord with a stinger (a bell, a cymbal swell or a whistle)
- Key change or tempo lift on the final repeat

## References

- *I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing (In Perfect Harmony)* — The New Seekers (from the Coca-Cola 'Hilltop' commercial), 1971. The jingle that became a chart hit; shows the form scaled up to a choir and a manifesto.
- *Hovis 'Bike Round' (Boy on a Bike) advert* — Directed by Ridley Scott, using a brass-band arrangement of Dvorak's New World Symphony, 1973. A British ad that sold working-class nostalgia with brass band music; the sound of a lie told fondly.
- *The Oscar Mayer Wiener Song* — Richard Trentlage / Oscar Mayer, 1963. A textbook jingle: nursery-simple melody with the product name as the hook.
- *R. Whites Lemonade 'Secret Lemonade Drinker' advert* — R. Whites / Ronnie Hazlehurst-era TV ad music tradition, 1973. A British TV ad song that people still sing; useful as a reference for tone and length. **(Corrected: The 1973 advert is real, but the maker credit is wrong. The song was written and sung by Ross McManus (Elvis Costello's father, with Declan on backing vocals); Rod Allen of Allen Brady & Marsh devised the campaign and slogan (some sources credit him with the jingle). Hazlehurst is not credited.)**

## What it carries

Chirpy, relentless and slightly false. It carries irony and menace better than anything else on this list, because the cheerfulness reads as a lie. It fights against subtlety and against length: past about a minute the form stops being a jingle and becomes a bad song.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Excellent for short punchy segments, a mock product for a bad policy (privatisation, a zero-hours contract, a 'luxury' that used to be a right), bumpers between scenes, and the story's end-card. It would undermine anything that needs the audience to believe the singer, and a sustained emotional beat.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno handles a short brief well if you name the target length and the register: 'upbeat 1970s television advertising jingle, 20 seconds, major key, bright piano and brass stabs, handclap rhythm, tight smiling backing vocals, sung slogan repeated twice, final rising tag, cheerful female lead'. Use a tiny lyric (2-4 lines) in a single [Chorus] section so the engine doesn't extend it into a full song. Exclude: 'verse, bridge, rap, guitar solo, sad, minor key, long intro, fade out'. Duration targeting is imperfect: ask for short and then trim in edit. Brand names and real slogans should be avoided in lyrics to stay clear of trademarks.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno wants to make a full song, so it pads the jingle with a verse and an outro; it also over-produces the vocal into modern pop gloss that sounds like a streaming-era advert rather than a 1970s one. The satirical turn usually gets sung as sincerely as the rest, so the menace is lost. Fix: keep the lyric to the slogan only, specify the vintage era and a mono/AM radio quality in the style, and put the sinister line in the spoken voice-over rather than the sung hook.

## Production cost

Very cheap because it is short, but it needs a good hook and the hook is the hard part: the engine will not write a genuinely memorable melody on demand, so expect many re-rolls and write the slogan to scan with a built-in tune. Voice consistency across several jingles is easy because the singer can be a generic 'ad voice'.
