# Punk poetry and rant

> A sharp-tongued, unmelodic British voice declaiming rhymed or free verse over a thin, repeating bass or guitar bed.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

This is the British tradition of performance poetry crossed with punk: John Cooper Clarke in the late 70s, then Sleaford Mods in the 2010s, with a nasal, fast, sneering voice reciting social observation over a looped bassline and a basic drum machine. The music is minimal on purpose so the lyric, its venom and its jokes dominate. It is rooted in the dole queue, the pub and the council estate, and carries an unpolished, anti-slick attitude.

## Signature traits

- Voice is spoken or half-shouted, nasal and sneering, in a regional British accent
- Rhythm follows speech stress with a rant-like acceleration, not a bar grid
- Single looping bassline of a few notes, often a bass guitar or synth bass
- Plain drum machine at 100 to 125 BPM with no fills
- Little or no melody; the hook is a repeated phrase
- Dry vocal, very upfront, with rough breath and spit
- Sparse arrangement that never builds to a big chorus
- Lyric packed with brand names, slang, insults and absurd comparison

## References

- *Evidently Chickentown* — John Cooper Clarke, 1980. A sneering short-line rant on small-town Britain with sparse backing **(Corrected: John Cooper Clarke poem written 1977; the commonly cited recording is on the 1980 album Snap, Crackle & Bop. Not confirmed by search.)**
- *Jobseeker* — Sleaford Mods, 2013. Looped bass, drum machine and a furious monologue about the dole
- *Divide and Exit* — Sleaford Mods, 2014. Album-length demonstration of the format and its British political anger
- *I Wanna Be Yours* — John Cooper Clarke, 1982. A poem set to music that shows the comic, rhythmic poetry voice **(Unconfirmed on the web.)**

## What it carries

Bitter, hilarious, impatient and scornful; it speaks for people who feel ignored. It fights against polish and hope, and it can read as bleak if a story needs a warm turn.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Direct satire of named decision-makers and institutions, and the working-class reader's own frustration. It suits monologues and lists of absurd facts. It would undermine stories that need beauty or a love story, and it may tire at feature length because of the single mode.

Use [Spoken] tags, and lyrics written as one clipped line after another with the rant's own punctuation. Exclude: 'singing, melodic chorus, rap flow, autotune, polished production, strings'. Add 'lo-fi' to keep it rough.

## How generators get it wrong

The model leans toward melodic singing or a smooth rap delivery and tends to produce a tidy, cleaned-up sound. Tone can go cartoonish when asked for 'angry'. Accent is not reliably held. Fix: stress 'spoken, not sung' in both Style and Exclude, tell it 'monotone' and 'half-shouted', and use simple repeating bass terms. Long unbroken text works best when divided into short stanzas.

## Production cost

Cheap musically, since the bed is simple, but voice consistency and an authentic rough voice are hard. Single-take rants of one to two minutes work best; longer ones lose the voice.
