# UK dub and dub poetry

> Slow, heavy reggae-derived 70 to 90 BPM music with a deep bass, spare drums, echo and reverb effects, and a poet or deejay speaking political truth over it.
>

UK dub comes from the Caribbean-British sound-system culture of the 1970s and 80s in London and Birmingham, where producers stripped reggae tracks to drums and bass and processed them with tape echo, spring reverb and live mixing-desk drop-outs. Dub poets like Linton Kwesi Johnson spoke about police, racism, unemployment and riots over it, and labels such as On-U Sound and producers like Dennis Bovell and Mad Professor kept the form alive. It is the root system for jungle, dubstep and trip-hop, and a documented tradition of political music made by and for working Britain. It is the least obvious pick in this family, and the one most suited to a calm, authoritative spoken voice.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 70 to 90 BPM in a one-drop rhythm: snare or rim on beat 3, kick and bass locked
- Deep, round, melodic bass line that carries the song
- Tape echo and spring reverb thrown at snare, guitar skank and voice, with sudden mix drop-outs
- Offbeat guitar or keyboard 'skank' chops on beats 2 and 4, plus a melodica, horn stab or organ bubble
- Spoken, measured, patois-inflected or plain London poetry delivery with a mournful, controlled tone
- Sparse arrangement with long spaces and fades of elements in and out
- Dry, warm analogue tone with tape hiss and sub bass

## References

- *Dread Beat an' Blood* — Linton Kwesi Johnson (Poet and the Roots), 1978. Dub poetry in the voice of Black Britain; a bass-led bed and a measured political voice.
- *Inglan Is a Bitch* — Linton Kwesi Johnson, 1980. A working-life, factory-and-welfare narrative spoken over reggae; the best model for class satire told as a monologue (on Bass Culture). **(Corrected: Linton Kwesi Johnson, 1980 single; it first appeared on the 1979 album Forces of Victory, not Bass Culture (1980). Both are from memory and not confirmed by search.)**
- *My Life in a Hole in the Ground* — African Head Charge, 1981. On-U Sound's experimental dub: heavy percussion and echo.
- *Dub Me Crazy* — Mad Professor, 1982. Landmark of British dub production and mixing-desk play.

## What it carries

Weighty, patient, dignified, righteous, with dry humour. It carries anger held under control and the long view. It fights against hype, speed and glossy pop gestures.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A narrator who knows how it ended and speaks calmly: the future superintelligence as a dub poet, plain and unhurried. Stories about work, wages, immigration and the police that need to be heard as testimony. It would undermine fast comedy, youth-slang satire and anything that needs an aggressive hook. It also asks for care: it is a tradition rooted in Black British experience, so use it with respect and avoid cartoon patois.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno Style box: 'UK dub reggae, 78 BPM, one-drop drums, deep round bass line, tape echo and spring reverb on snare, offbeat guitar skank, melodica, spoken word male London voice, warm analogue'. Exclude 'EDM, pop reggae, ska-punk, beach reggae, ukulele, autotune, rap, big chorus, fake Jamaican patois'. Use [Spoken] sections and short measured lines; instruct 'plain London accent' unless a dialect is deliberately chosen. Instrumental dub requests ('instrumental dub version') give a narrator bed.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to drift to sunny pop-reggae (beach, ukulele, major key) and to fake or exaggerated Jamaican patois in the vocal, which is both inaccurate and offensive. Real dub mixing (live fader moves, echo throws and drop-outs) is rarely produced; the effects are static. Fix by excluding pop reggae and patois, specifying 'sparse', 'echo throws', 'minor key', and add the dub moves in the edit. Spoken voices can sound sing-song; ask for 'flat, measured, no melody'.

## Production cost

Very consistent as an instrumental bed and well suited to low-energy long cuts. The voice is the risk: a chosen spoken voice needs checking for accent and respect each time. Echo-drop sections are best made by editing stems.
