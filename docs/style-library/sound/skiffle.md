# Skiffle

> Fast, cheap, homemade 1950s folk and blues played on guitar, washboard and tea-chest bass by working-class teenagers.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Skiffle was a British craze of 1955 to 1958, sparked by Lonnie Donegan's version of Rock Island Line, in which amateur groups played American folk and blues songs on acoustic guitar, washboard, banjo and a bass made from a tea chest, broom handle and string. It needed no money or training, so hundreds of thousands of teenagers formed groups, including the Quarrymen. It is DIY from the start: speed, rhythm and enthusiasm replace skill. It is the bridge between folk revival and British beat.

## Signature traits

- Acoustic guitar played in fast down-strummed chords with a driving 4/4 shuffle
- Washboard scraping and thimbles keeping a rattling backbeat
- Tea-chest bass plucked on one string with a slapped, thudding note
- Nasal, shouted, urgent lead vocal with an imitation-American accent
- Starts slow and accelerates through the song, a trademark of the style
- Simple three-chord progression (I, IV, V), 12-bar blues or folk-song form
- Dry, thin mono 1950s sound with tape slap echo

## References

- *Rock Island Line* — Lonnie Donegan, 1956. The record that began the craze, with its speeding-up structure
- *Freight Train* — Chas McDevitt Skiffle Group with Nancy Whiskey, 1957. A hit with a female lead and homemade-instrument sound **(Corrected: Real: Chas McDevitt Skiffle Group with Nancy Whiskey, UK hit 1957. Search returned nothing, so unconfirmed.)**
- *Don't You Rock Me Daddy-O* — The Vipers Skiffle Group, 1957. Coffee-bar skiffle, a pure example of the form **(Unconfirmed on the web.)**
- *Putting on the Style* — Lonnie Donegan, 1957. Satire of pretension in skiffle form; proof it can sneer

## What it carries

Eager, restless, rebellious and unpolished, with youthful impatience at the grown-ups. It can carry anger lightly and sound like a bunch of kids refusing to wait for permission. It fights against anything stately or sorrowful and against modern production.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Short, quick, DIY-spirited songs about making do, doing it yourself, and the powerful being pompous; it fits a story about ordinary people building their own alternative. It would undermine a grave or elegiac story and anything wanting authority, and its American blues roots need British lyrics to read as ours.
