# Football terrace chant and stadium anthem

> A massed, stomping, shouted singalong built on a borrowed tune, with a lyric you could bellow in a pub after a defeat.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The collective voice of the stands: chants that reuse a well-known tune (hymns, pop hooks, nursery songs) with new, topical and often rude words, shouted by thousands. The music is a rhythm and a shout rather than a refined melody: stomps, claps and a limited melodic range. It has a distinctly working-class British identity and a history of being turned into pop singles. As a style it is about belonging and defiance.

## Signature traits

- Tempo 90-120 BPM, heavy on the beat, stomp-stomp-clap or four-on-the-floor kick pattern
- Massed male crowd vocal, unison, shouted rather than sung, with a limited range of about five notes
- Reused tune from a hymn, pop song or nursery tune with new words
- Brass or a terrace-style drum (big bass drum), and sometimes a Hammond or football-rattle sound
- Short repeated phrase of 4-8 syllables, repeated 3-4 times, with a rhyming punch line
- Call-and-response between a leader and the crowd, or between two stands
- Raw, distorted, live-in-the-stadium recording with echo off the stands

## References

- *Three Lions* — Baddiel, Skinner and The Lightning Seeds, 1996. A chart anthem built from terrace-chant grammar, mixing self-mockery and hope.
- *Vindaloo* — Fat Les, 1998. A deliberately dumb, beery football chant turned record; a template for mass shouting as the hook.
- *Blue Is the Colour* — Chelsea FC squad, 1972. A club-anthem single that shows the stadium style translated to a studio record.
- *You'll Never Walk Alone* — Gerry and the Pacemakers (adopted by the Liverpool Kop), 1963. The key example of a pop song claimed by a crowd and transformed in meaning.

## What it carries

Defiant, tribal and loud. It carries solidarity, mockery and rage; it can be funny and moving in the same line. It fights against irony delivered quietly, individualism and anything slow.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Excellent for a chorus where the 'people' take on the powerful, a victory or humiliation scene, or a refrain the audience can join. A fit for a working-class UK audience because it is their own folk music. It would undermine a story where the villain needs to be subtle, and it risks tribalism and a drift into nationalism, so a 'we' needs a clear enemy that is a decision-maker, not a group of people.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to concrete crowd words: 'football terrace chant, massed male crowd shouting in unison, stomping and clapping, big bass drum, brass band, 100 BPM, raw live stadium recording, echoing, call and response, no melody to speak of'. Lyrics as short repeated lines with [Crowd] or (shouted) markers. Write a familiar-sounding but original tune style, and avoid quoting real chants or songs, as real melodies can be flagged. Exclude: 'solo singer, smooth, studio polish, autotune, pop ballad, electronic'. Real club names and real players should not be used.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno produces a 'crowd' that is a small, well-mixed choir singing in tune rather than the ragged, loud and slightly out-of-time roar of a real terrace. The result sounds like a cinema-trailer choir or a rugby-themed pop song. It also tends to smooth the melody toward a pop chorus. Fix: ask for 'out of tune, ragged, drunken, live recording from the stands, distorted', keep lyrics very short and repetitive, and consider layering a real or recorded crowd bed.

## Production cost

Moderate: one chant is easy, but the stadium texture is hard to hold across takes and a real crowd recording is better. A little goes a long way: use as a short refrain rather than a full song.
