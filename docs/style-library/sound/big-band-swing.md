# Big-band swing and jump blues

> A fourteen-to-eighteen-piece dance band of brass, saxes and rhythm section playing riffs and call-and-response between sections, with a swinging four-beat bar.
>

> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The dance music of the 1930s and 1940s, centred on Black bandleaders in Harlem, Kansas City and Chicago, including Duke Ellington, Count Basie, Chick Webb and Cab Calloway. Arrangements stack sections (trumpets, trombones, saxes) against each other in short riffs over a four-to-the-bar rhythm. The smaller offshoot, jump blues (Louis Jordan and his Tympany Five), shrank the band to a hot small group with comic lyrics and a punchline chorus. The sound is hot, funny, fast and sophisticated, and was the pop music of its day.

## Signature traits

- Brass and sax sections trade riffs in call and response
- Walking upright bass, four-on-the-floor ride cymbal or snare swing, and a rhythm guitar playing chords on every beat
- Swing eighth notes (long-short feel) at 120-200 beats per minute
- Muted trumpet and plunger-mute growl, clarinet or tenor-sax solos, and a short shout chorus at the end
- Vocal delivered as a scat or a patter, sometimes comic, with call-and-response with the band
- Head arrangement: a riff, a solo chorus for each player, then a loud out-chorus
- 1930s-40s recording sound: narrow, warm and slightly boxy, with room ambience
- Titles and lyrics full of jive slang, wit and punchlines

## References

- *Take the 'A' Train* — Duke Ellington and His Orchestra, 1941. The theme of the Ellington band, with a classic brass-section arrangement written by Billy Strayhorn.
- *One O'Clock Jump* — Count Basie and His Orchestra, 1937. A riff-based Kansas City swing piece, showing how a simple blues can drive a whole band.
- *Minnie the Moocher* — Cab Calloway and His Orchestra, 1931. A call-and-response comic story-song, with a narrator and a hey-de-hi-de-ho response.
- *Is You Is or Is You Ain't (Ma' Baby)* — Louis Jordan and His Tympany Five, 1944. A jump-blues hit with a comic lyric and tight small-group arrangement.
- *A-Tisket, A-Tasket* — Ella Fitzgerald with Chick Webb and His Orchestra, 1938. A hit that put a nursery rhyme through a swing arrangement; shows the form can be playful.

## What it carries

Fast, hot, witty, sophisticated, and cheeky. It can be romantic, conspiratorial, and breezily cynical. It fights against sincerity that is slow or heavy, and anything that needs a small intimate sound.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire about the rich, parties of the powerful, a boardroom or ballroom, bankers dancing while the roof falls in, and fast montage scenes where cutting to the beat gives energy. A jump-blues patter song is a very good vehicle for a punchline narrator and a comic list of grievances. Dance-band styling gives a fancy-dress glamour that can ironise a topic of wealth. It would undermine a scene of real suffering or hardship, where it could read as mockery, and anything set in a modern setting without a deliberate anachronism.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno's 'swing' often comes out as a modern retro-pop or a 'swing revival' track with a crooner and a clean digital horn sample and too-even, quantised eighth notes; the feel is stiff. Horn sections sound like a synth patch unless you add detail. It often defaults to a smooth white-crooner lead or a Christmas-album feel. Extended solos repeat the same lick. The comic patter lyric is the part Suno does worst: it writes generic rhymes without a punchline, so write the lyrics. It rarely builds a real shout chorus. Fix: specify era, mono, 'live in a ballroom', list the instruments, and exclude 'crooner', 'Christmas' and 'lounge'.

## Production cost

Medium. A big-band arrangement is a lot for the engine to hold, and the vocal and the horns often disagree on tempo. Expect many takes. Instrumental cues (stings, 20-30 seconds) are easier than full songs. Vocals with a specific voice are inconsistent from take to take.
