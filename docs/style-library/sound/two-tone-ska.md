# 2-Tone ska

> Fast, skanking, minor-key British ska-punk hybrid from 1979-81 Coventry, with pub-band urgency and a mixed-race line-up singing about dole queues and riots.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

2-Tone was the label and movement started in Coventry in 1979 by the Specials' Jerry Dammers. It took 1960s Jamaican ska and rocksteady and ran them at punk speed with a Farfisa-style organ, choppy guitar upstrokes and a horn section. Bands were deliberately black and white, and the music was fiercely about unemployment, racism, Thatcher and police. It is the one style in this family that is already a British working-class protest sound. The later ska revival and American third-wave ska-punk are a different thing and a common mistake.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 120-160 BPM with a walking bass line, often a bar of rocksteady-slow 'ghost' tempo at about 70 BPM for the sombre songs
- Offbeat guitar upstroke 'skank' on beats 2 and 4, clipped and dry
- Rimshot or snare on the third beat, hi-hat tight, sound closer to a live pub band than a polished studio
- Cheap-sounding combo organ or Farfisa stabs and a ragged three-piece horn line (trumpet, trombone, sax)
- Vocals shouted or deadpan, often in chanted group choruses, thick English and Midlands accents, not patois
- Minor-key verses about specific British misery (dole, closures, riots), with a bright major or sing-along chorus
- Short songs, usually 2:30 to 3:30, with a sax or trombone solo and a count-in or spoken intro
- Dry, boxy 1979 recording with a little spring reverb, no modern compression

## References

- *Ghost Town* — The Specials, 1981. Number one in the summer of the 1981 riots, an eerie slow ska dirge about closed clubs and unemployment; the template for sombre 2-Tone.
- *Too Much Too Young* — The Specials, 1980. Breakneck live-EP-style ska with a cheeky political punchline about contraception and young motherhood.
- *Stand Down Margaret* — The Beat, 1980. A direct named-target anti-Thatcher single over a bright ska bounce.
- *One Step Beyond* — Madness, 1979. The comic, theatrical, pub-piano end of the sound, with a Prince Buster cover as the title.
- *On My Radio* — The Selecter, 1979. A female-fronted 2-Tone classic, tighter and more new-wave than the Specials.

## What it carries

Can carry anger, gallows humour, communal swagger and sudden melancholy within one chorus. Ska makes bitterness danceable. It fights against anything that needs to feel sleek, futuristic or luxurious, and against earnest solo introspection.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire about British working life: bosses, landlords, the dole, privatisation, a politician with a named face. The group chant chorus suits a mass or crowd scene and the rude-boy swagger suits a likeable rogue. It would undermine a lonely, quiet or cosmic story, and a long slow-build argument.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to 'ska', '2 Tone', 'British ska', 'late 1970s', 'rocksteady' and 'skank guitar' in the Style box. Name the band format in plain description instead of living artists: 'two guitars, Hammond-style organ, trumpet trombone and tenor sax, walking upright bass, dry live room'. Ask for 'English working-class male lead, shouted group chorus, no accent exaggeration'. Specifying BPM (about 135 for up-tempo, about 75 for ghost-town slow) helps. Exclude: 'ska punk, pop punk, third wave ska, American, skate punk, distorted guitar, EDM, reggaeton, polished'. Lyrics tags such as [Verse], [Chorus - group shout], [Sax solo] work.

## How generators get it wrong

The default pull for 'ska' is 1990s American ska-punk: distorted power chords, too fast, shiny, with brass sounding like a pop-punk sample pack. Fix this with 'dry, thin, 1979 pub-band recording' and by adding 'distorted guitar' to Exclude. Vocals tend to come out as generic mid-Atlantic pop or a mock Jamaican accent, and the fix is to specify an English regional voice. The horn lines are often too busy and too legato, losing the choppy stabs. Chorus 'gang vocals' tend to sound like a clean pop choir rather than a pub crowd, so lyrics need short shout-friendly lines.

## Production cost

Cheap to generate, hard to keep distinctive. The horn section and the rimshot feel drift between takes, and the 'live band in a dry room' character is hard to hold across a whole soundtrack. A recognisable lead voice can't be guaranteed from take to take unless a Voice or Persona is used. Songs are naturally short, which suits the 8-second-clip workflow because choruses are easy to cut to.
