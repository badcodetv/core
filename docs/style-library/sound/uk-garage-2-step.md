# UK garage and 2-step

> Skippy, swung 130 BPM club music with a shuffled broken-beat kick pattern, warm chopped vocal samples, rubbery bass and a sung or chatted topline.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

UK garage grew in the mid 1990s from London and Bristol house scenes, speeding up American garage and house with jungle's low end. In 1998 to 2000 the '2-step' variation dropped the steady four-on-the-floor kick for a skipping, syncopated pattern, giving the genre its shuffled feel and crossing it into the charts. Its sound is warm and glossy: pitched and chopped vocal snippets, soulful R&B hooks, sub bass, and a knowing, a bit-too-smooth 'champagne' look. Later strains (speed garage, dark garage, Mike Skinner's spoken-word garage) went rougher and more observational.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 128 to 135 BPM, kick on beat 1 and beat 3 off-grid so the groove skips (2-step) with swung 16th hi-hats
- Snare or clap on beats 2 and 4 plus ghost snares and shuffled percussion
- Pitched, chopped and time-stretched vocal snippets used as a rhythmic instrument
- Warm, round 'reese' or sine sub-bass that wobbles on the offbeat
- Smooth R&B-influenced sung hook with melismatic vocals over a brighter, jazzy chord (Rhodes, strings, piano stabs)
- Spoken or chatted verses are common; MC 'shout-outs' and a 'rewind' moment
- Dark garage variant: minor keys, ominous strings, sparse bass

## References

- *Re-Rewind (The Crowd Say Bo Selecta)* — Artful Dodger feat. Craig David, 1999. The record that took 2-step into the charts.
- *Sincere* — MJ Cole, 2000. The warm, string-led, jazzy end of the sound. **(Corrected: MJ Cole's single 'Sincere' came out in 1998-99; the album Sincere is 2000. Year 2000 fits the album, not the single.)**
- *Sweet Like Chocolate* — Shanks & Bigfoot, 1999. Pure skippy pop garage with chopped vocal and bass.
- *Original Pirate Material* — The Streets, 2002. Garage rhythms used for sharp, funny, spoken observations about ordinary working-class British life, the closest model for narrated satire.
- *Untrue* — Burial, 2007. Ghostly, broken 2-step shuffle with pitched vocal fragments; the dark, melancholy extreme.

## What it carries

Warm, nocturnal, romantic, wry. It carries nostalgia, comic everyday detail, longing and flirtation very well, and can turn into melancholy. It fights against raw aggression and against anything formally 'epic'.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories told as a deadpan observational monologue over a bouncy, familiar groove (a bank, a high street, a council office), where the contrast between breezy sound and bitter content is the joke. Also nostalgic or melancholy pieces about what was lost. It would undermine stories that need menace, rage or grandeur unless the dark-garage variant is chosen.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to 'UK garage, 2-step, 132 BPM, skippy shuffled drums, chopped pitched vocal samples, warm sub bass, Rhodes chords, smooth soulful female vocal' and to 'spoken-word verses, deadpan British male voice' for the observational variant. Exclude 'four-on-the-floor house kick, EDM, big room drop, trap, rock guitars'. Add 'dark garage, minor key, ghostly vocal fragments' for the Burial-like end. Keep lyrics short with a clear hook line; tag [Spoken] verses in plain language to get the narrator feel.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to default to straight four-on-the-floor house or to modern pop-house with an EDM build, because it matches the genre name 'garage' to house. It also tends to make sung hooks glossy and generic. The skippy missing-kick feel is the hardest thing to get; repeat the phrase 'no kick on every beat, syncopated kick' and list '2-step' prominently. Pitched vocal chops come out as a generic vocal sample rather than a deliberate one, so say 'pitched vocal chops as rhythm'.

## Production cost

Easy to keep consistent across a story because the groove and palette are mostly instrumental. The sung topline voice is the drift risk; a spoken-word narrator is more stable than a sung one. Length is not an issue.
