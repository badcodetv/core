# Protest and union folk

> A topical song with a plain voice and acoustic guitar, written to be sung at a picket line, demo or meeting.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The British protest tradition runs from broadsides and Chartist songs through Ewan MacColl and Peggy Seeger in the 1950s and 60s, to Leon Rosselson, to Billy Bragg in the 1980s Miners' Strike era. A single singer with acoustic or lightly electric guitar delivers argued lyrics: names, dates and places, a clear target, and a chorus meant to be sung back. Lyrics lean on historical or religious reference and plain moral anger. The tune is often borrowed from an older song, so the words are the point.

## Signature traits

- Single acoustic guitar, strummed or picked, or a lone electric guitar with treble and bite
- Plain, regional, speaking-singing voice delivered flat and direct
- Dense wordy verses with names, places and a clear villain
- Refrain or chorus written for a group to sing back, often with a rhyme on a slogan
- Tempo 90 to 130 bpm, steady, no virtuosic breaks
- Borrowed or traditional-sounding melody; minimal harmony
- Close mic'd, dry, a little rough

## What it carries

Earnest, angry, funny in a dry way and solidaristic; it asks you to join. It fights against irony without conviction and against audience members who feel lectured, which matters given the brief on not telling the reader they have been duped.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Direct arguments about wages, housing, ownership and who decides; a song that names the decision-maker. It would undermine a piece that must not feel like a sermon, and the genre is easily heard as lecturing by those not already converted, so humour and story have to lead.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to 'British folk protest song, solo acoustic guitar strummed, plain regional male voice, spoken-sung delivery, union hall, dry close-mic, singalong chorus, 1980s'. Lyrics should have named people and places, and a [Chorus] that is short and repeated. Exclude: pop production, drums, orchestral swell, gospel choir, American Dylan-style nasal, reverb wash, autotune. Give the accent (Northern English, Midlands, Scots) as a descriptive phrase.

## How generators get it wrong

The model produces a tidy, emotional singer-songwriter with an acoustic ballad build, strings and a big last chorus, or American folk with harmonica. The vocal is breathy and pretty not plain and angry; lyrics become abstract ('rise up, stand tall') rather than named and specific. Fix: write the concrete lyrics yourself, ask for 'speaking voice, little melody, no vibrato', exclude 'harmonica, strings, build, cinematic', and keep the chorus to one line.

## Production cost

Low. One voice and guitar are easy to keep consistent. The real cost is the writing: a protest song only works if the lyric has specifics, and Suno will not supply those.
