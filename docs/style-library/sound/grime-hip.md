# Grime

> Fast, aggressive, bass-heavy British MC music at 140 BPM, built on square-wave synths, dry drums and clipped, rapid delivery.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Grime emerged in East London around 2002 from garage, jungle and dancehall. Its sound is cold, digital and stripped back: 140 BPM, a sparse kick-snare pattern, sine sub-bass, harsh synth stabs in a minor key, and MCs delivering clipped, fast bars in a British voice. It carries a strong sense of place and class, and has been used for both boasting and political anger. Pirate-radio culture and the sixteen-bar freestyle are part of its form.

## Signature traits

- 140 BPM, half-time feel with kick on 1 and snare on 3
- Sparse beat with long silences and lots of space between hits
- Square-wave or sawtooth synth lead playing a short, cold, repeating melody
- Deep sine sub-bass that carries the pitch
- MC delivers 16 bars in short, clipped syllables with dense, rapid-fire rhyme
- Raw close vocal with a gritty, shouty edge, sometimes lightly distorted
- Pirate-radio style shout-outs, reloads and crowd chants
- Stark, lo-fi, 'cheap software' sound, with few reverbs

## References

- *Boy in da Corner* — Dizzee Rascal, 2003. The foundational grime album, bleak and political, from an East London estate
- *Eskimo* — Wiley, 2002. The instrumental that set grime's cold, sparse template
- *Shutdown* — Skepta, 2015. Mainstream grime revival, minimal beat and a clipped, confident delivery
- *Shut Up* — Stormzy, 2015. A freestyle-over-instrumental that shows the form: one voice, one grime beat

## What it carries

Urgent, hostile, defiant and funny in a hard way; it can carry rage and swagger about class and place. It fights against reflection and gentleness, and the pace limits how much argument fits in a verse.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Short, high-energy pieces: a charge sheet against a decision-maker, a chant, a shock opening, or a climax. It suits a British urban young audience. It would undermine slow explanation, tenderness, and any piece whose point needs the listener to follow a long sentence.

## Asking Suno for it

Style box: 'grime, 140 BPM, sparse dry drums, cold square-wave synth stabs, deep sine sub-bass, aggressive British MC, clipped fast delivery, raw close vocal, East London'. Use short lines of 4 to 8 syllables and [Verse] tags with clear bar counts. Exclude: 'trap hi-hats, autotune, pop chorus, singing, orchestra, American accent'. Hi-hat and instrument terms matter more than genre words alone.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno frequently produces a polished, mid-tempo UK rap or drill-ish track in place of a true grime beat, and the beat can sound too full and modern. Rapid delivery gets slurred, and words are dropped. Fix: describe the beat in elements (sparse, 140 BPM, no hi-hat rolls), keep lyrics short and phonetic, and avoid long tongue-twisting lines that the model will mumble.

## Production cost

Fast flow means more re-rolls to get intelligible words. A single MC voice is easier to keep consistent than a crew. Hard to make a believable multi-MC cypher; layering takes in a DAW is the usual route.
