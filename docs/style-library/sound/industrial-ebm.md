# EBM / industrial

> Hard, sequenced, military-precise electronic body music with shouted or chanted vocals and a menacing, mechanical pulse.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A European and British strand from the late 1970s and 80s (DAF, Throbbing Gristle, Nitzer Ebb, Front 242) that fused synth sequencing with the sound of factories and drill. Where synth-pop used machines for polish, EBM used them for force. It grew out of industrial working-class cities (Sheffield, Düsseldorf, Brussels, Essex). The vocal is barked, the bass is a locked sequence, and the lyrics are short, commanding, and ambiguous.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 110-130 BPM, rigid 16th-note sequenced bass, no swing
- Hard, dry, heavily compressed drum machine kick and snare; metallic percussion hits
- Short shouted or chanted vocal lines, often single words repeated, with a drill-sergeant delivery
- Distorted analog synth stabs and noise sweeps
- Minimal harmony; one riff, one chord, forward drive
- Sound-design from factory recordings: hammering, steam, machinery
- Spare structure with a hard drop-out and a shout of a title phrase

## References

- *Der Mussolini* — DAF, 1981. A stripped-down sequencer and drum track with a barked vocal that made the template for EBM.
- *Join in the Chant* — Nitzer Ebb, 1987. Shouted call-and-response chant over a rigid sequence; a model of crowd command.
- *Headhunter* — Front 242, 1988. A cold, driving, club-ready EBM track with hard percussion and distorted vocals.
- *20 Jazz Funk Greats* — Throbbing Gristle, 1979. A landmark industrial album that sets deadpan lounge against dark experimental electronics.
- *Warm Leatherette* — The Normal, 1978. An early minimal synth single that shows how a few machine sounds can be chilling.

## What it carries

Can carry menace, authority, anger, regimentation, grim comedy about power. It fights against warmth and against sympathy for a character. It risks sounding like it endorses the authoritarian posture it is portraying.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about those in control: bosses, officials, the machine of work and discipline, the factory floor, a regime that speaks in orders. A shouted chorus can work as a satirical slogan. It would undermine a story about people's own small lives, and risks being read as glorifying authority if the satire is not clear.

## Asking Suno for it

Describe the machinery and the delivery: EBM, rigid sequenced analog bass, 120 BPM, hard compressed drum machine, metallic percussion hits, distorted synth stabs, barked shouted vocal, short chanted lines, factory noise, dry and cold. Use short repeated lines in the lyrics and tag [Chant] or [Shouted]. Exclude: melodic pop chorus, sung vocals, reverb wash, soft pads, guitar solo, EDM drop, autotune, symphonic.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to soften industrial into modern dark synthwave or metal-tinged pop, with a sung vocal and a melodic chorus. It struggles with truly barked, unmusical vocal delivery and tends to sing the line. Distortion often sounds like a filter on a clean mix. Fix with 'shouted, barked, unsung, military chant', short lines, and exclude 'melodic', 'emotional', 'pop'. Takes often add a dramatic cinematic build.

## Production cost

Rigid pulse is easy for the engine, but vocal delivery is the unstable part and takes vary a lot, so keeping a single menacing voice across a story needs careful repeat prompting or a saved voice. Short chants work better than long verses.
