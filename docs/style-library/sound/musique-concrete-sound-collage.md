# Musique concrete and sound collage

> Music assembled from recorded real-world sound (voices, machines, radio, tape) cut, looped and layered into a montage.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Musique concrete began in 1940s Paris when Pierre Schaeffer built compositions from recorded noises instead of written notes. It later fed the BBC Radiophonic Workshop (tape-spliced sound for TV and radio), the Beatles' experiments, ambient and sampling culture, and British comedy-sound work. The material is sampled, slowed, reversed and juxtaposed so that the sounds make an argument by collision. For satire, collage lets the powerful speak in their own words, edited.

## Signature traits

- Built from recorded sounds: speech, radio chatter, machinery, crowds, tape hiss, clocks, doors
- Tape techniques: looping, reversing, pitch-shifting, speed change, hard splice cuts
- Layered, non-metrical or loosely metrical texture, with repeating loops as the only beat
- Voices cut into fragments and re-sequenced to change meaning
- Wide dynamic jumps: silence to noise to a single sound
- Spatial placement and sudden stereo pans
- Lo-fi, degraded fidelity: crackle, tape wow and flutter, room tone

## References

- *Etude aux chemins de fer* — Pierre Schaeffer, 1948. The founding piece: a rhythm built from recorded locomotives.
- *Doctor Who theme (realisation)* — Ron Grainer, realised by Delia Derbyshire at the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, 1963. Tape-spliced and manipulated sound that reached a mass UK audience.
- *Revolution 9* — The Beatles, 1968. A tape collage of voices and found sound inside a mainstream album.
- *Blue Jam* — Chris Morris (BBC Radio 1), 1997-1999. Sound-design-led British comedy radio using ambient music, speech and unsettling juxtaposition.

## What it carries

Uncanny, intelligent and cold, though it can also be hilarious when voices collide. It carries paranoia, institutional absurdity and dread; it fights against warmth, singalong appeal and simple emotional clarity.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A fit for a story about institutions and their language: committee speak, press briefings, adverts and sirens cut into a portrait of a system. Works well for a section where the 'machine' is shown eating the human voice. It would undermine a broad, accessible comic song, and risks losing a working-class audience if it reads as art-school.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno is weak at true collage because it generates a mix, not editable sources, but it can approximate the mood: 'experimental musique concrete, tape loops, found sound, radio static, clock ticks, reversed voices, spliced tape, sparse irregular rhythm, lo-fi, 1960s electronic studio'. Use [Instrumental] and non-lyrical structural tags. The better method is to build the collage in an editor from real recordings and use Suno only for textures or a tonal bed. Exclude: 'pop, beat drop, melody, vocals, polished, uplifting'. Do not use identifiable real speeches or broadcast audio without clearing the rights.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno resolves everything toward tonality and a steady beat, so 'experimental' comes back as ambient pop or a vaguely spooky synth pad, and 'found sound' is a clean synthesised imitation rather than real recordings. Vocal fragments become sung words, not cut speech. Fix: ask for texture and noise rather than 'experimental', generate stems of individual sounds, and do the cutting yourself.

## Production cost

High if done properly, because the real work is editing and sourcing recordings, which the engine can't do. Cheap if used only as a texture. The collage must be hand-built and kept consistent; it is a good fit for makers who can edit audio.
