# Spaghetti western score

> A sparse, echoing, theatrical Italian western sound of twanging guitar, whistling, choir and sudden gunshot percussion.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

In the mid-1960s Italian directors shot westerns in Spain and Italy, and composers such as Ennio Morricone made music that was deliberately strange: instruments chosen for their sound rather than tradition, like whistling, whip cracks, electric guitar, jaw harp, ocarina and wordless choir. Scores build from long silences to explosive operatic climaxes, drawn out to match the slow standoffs. It conveys fate, greed and moral emptiness.

## Signature traits

- Tremolo electric guitar with twang and heavy spring reverb, plus acoustic guitar
- Solo whistling, ocarina, harmonica, jaw harp, bells and occasional whip-crack or anvil percussion
- Wordless male chorus or a soaring female wail, and sudden operatic trumpet
- Tempo slow to medium, about 60 to 100 bpm, with long held notes and silent gaps
- Repeating bass ostinato under a lonely melody
- Wide, cavernous reverb, close dry foreground against distant echo
- Sudden build to a crashing climax in the last third

## References

- *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (score)* — Ennio Morricone, directed by Sergio Leone, 1966. Its main theme with coyote-howl vocals is the archetype of the style.
- *Once Upon a Time in the West (score)* — Ennio Morricone, directed by Sergio Leone, 1968. Shows the sparse, tragic, operatic side, with a soprano as a character theme.
- *A Fistful of Dollars (score)* — Ennio Morricone, directed by Sergio Leone, 1964. The origin of the whip-crack and whistle sound.
- *Django (title song and score)* — Luis Bacalov, directed by Sergio Corbucci, 1966. A rival, more melodic and brooding approach with a sung theme.

## What it carries

Lonely, ironic, fatalistic and grandly cynical; it can turn a greedy man's fall into mythic tragedy or a standoff into dark comedy. It fights against urgency, fast cutting and any warm, communal feeling.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A standoff between a debtor and a banker, a story about greed with a lone figure against a system, or narration of a heist as a duel. It makes small stakes feel mythic. It would undermine quick explanatory comedy and anything set in a very modern office unless the clash is the joke.

## How generators get it wrong

Most common failure is generic 'Americana', modern country or a gunslinger-cliche loop with a stock guitar twang. The silence, which gives the style its tension, is filled in with constant pad and drums. Choir often sounds like a gospel or cinematic choir rather than eerie wordless voices. Fix with 'sparse', 'silence between phrases', 'no drums until climax', and exclude 'country, Nashville, gospel'. Lengthy builds often do not pay off; tell it where the climax lands with section tags.

## Production cost

Low. It is instrumental so it is cheap to keep consistent, and works for long beds. The hardest piece is getting real restraint and a structured build rather than a looping pad. A sung theme with vocals is much less reliable.
