# Requiem and dies irae (choral-orchestral lament)

> A Latin or English mass for the dead scored for choir and orchestra, from soft consolation to apocalyptic brass and drum.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The Requiem Mass has been set by composers since the 15th century, and the 'Dies irae' (day of wrath) sequence gives it a famous four-note chant motif and a scene of the Last Judgment. Mozart, Verdi and Fauré each defined a different mood: terror, spectacle, consolation. In the 20th century Britten set Wilfred Owen's war poems against the Latin text, showing the form could be used for political witness. It is the sound of a society grieving for something large.

## Signature traits

- Mixed choir (40-100 voices) plus orchestra and sometimes organ, in a large church or hall acoustic
- Slow tempo, 50-70 bpm, for Lacrimosa and Introit; sudden violent Allegro for Dies irae
- Falling chromatic sighs and minor-key suspensions in the strings
- Bass drum, timpani and offstage brass for 'Tuba mirum' judgment fanfares
- Latin text sung in unison or homophonic blocks, with plainchant fragments
- Dies irae four-note motif (a falling semitone then back) quoted in orchestra
- Sudden drop to near silence after a fortissimo, then solo soprano
- Final soft cadence and sustained organ or choral chord ('In paradisum', 'Lux aeterna')

## References

- *Requiem in D minor (Lacrimosa)* — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, 1791. Rising and falling choral sighs; the unfinished work whose mystique is part of the sound.
- *Messa da Requiem (Dies irae)* — Giuseppe Verdi, 1874. Theatrical terror: bass drum strokes and off-stage trumpets.
- *Requiem (In paradisum)* — Gabriel Faure, 1887-1900. Gentle consolation; proves the form can be calm.
- *War Requiem* — Benjamin Britten, 1962. Latin Mass interleaved with Wilfred Owen's poems; the template for political grief.
- *Requiem (as used in 2001: A Space Odyssey)* — Gyorgy Ligeti, 1965. Dense micropolyphonic choral clusters; the sound of dread without any tune.

## What it carries

Can carry mourning, awe, menace, the weight of judgement, consolation. It fights against humour, speed and anything light; it makes the topic look like an irreversible catastrophe.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Closing sections that mourn what a story's world lost: a town, an industry, a generation's prospects, the 'what could have been' coda. Also the single dramatic fall in a dystopia. Undermines mid-story satire and anything the audience should be able to laugh at, and risks pomposity if used for a small loss; also heavy Latin text may feel remote to the target audience unless the lyrics are English.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno's Latin pronunciation is unreliable and often drifts into Italian or gibberish. Choirs are mushy pad-like voices with weak consonants; the 'big' sections often sound like a synthesised choir patch with a trailer boom. It may add pop-ballad structure (verse, chorus) to a text that should stay through-composed. Dies irae is frequently imitated by generic dark epic scoring without the actual motif. Fix with English text, short lines, and fewer voices.

## Production cost

High for convincing full choir; lower for a single solo voice or organ and strings. Take-to-take consistency is moderate. Short cues of 20-40 seconds are far more reliable than a full-length requiem structure.
