# Hard funk

> A locked, repetitive groove built on the first beat of the bar, with choppy guitar, slapped bass and a rhythm section that does the arguing.
>

> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The sound that James Brown's band hardened in the mid-1960s, which emphasised 'the one' (the downbeat) and treated every instrument as a drum. It was extended by Sly and the Family Stone, the Meters in New Orleans and the P-Funk collective (Parliament and Funkadelic). Songs are built on one or two chords and a repeating groove, with a shouted vocal or a chant over the top. The grooves can run for minutes, and the horns punch rather than play melodies. The politics were direct: 'Say It Loud' and 'Chocolate City' put identity and place to a beat.

## Signature traits

- One or two chords held for the whole song, with the groove as the main event
- Emphasis on the downbeat ('the one'), with a tight, syncopated sixteenth-note drum pattern
- Sharp, muted, scratchy rhythm guitar played as percussion
- Thumping, slapped or very rounded bass with a repeating riff
- Horn section playing short stabs on off-beats
- Vocal: shouted, grunted or chanted, with call-and-response between the singer and the band, and with band-leader calls ('hit me', 'take it to the bridge')
- Tempo 95-115 beats per minute, but felt as a much faster pulse
- Live-in-the-room recording with close dry drums and an upfront bass

## References

- *Papa's Got a Brand New Bag* — James Brown and the Famous Flames, 1965. The record that fixed the funk sound on the one, with chicken-scratch guitar and horn stabs.
- *Say It Loud - I'm Black and I'm Proud* — James Brown, 1968. A call-and-response chant with a crowd of children's voices: funk used for an open political statement.
- *Cissy Strut* — The Meters, 1969. A purely instrumental groove, showing how much the rhythm section can say without vocals.
- *Thank You (Falettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)* — Sly and the Family Stone, 1969. A slapped-bass monster with sung-shouted vocals and a message about identity and masks.
- *Chocolate City* — Parliament, 1975. A funk satire that treats a city as a political body, with a sly spoken-word voice and horns.

## What it carries

Strutting, physical, sly and confident. It carries swagger, defiance, humour and menace. It is good at 'the powerful are ridiculous' and at making people move. It fights against sentimentality, long melodic lines and anything slow.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Scenes of a heist, a swindle, a boss strutting, a market rigged, the rich in control, or the moment the cast fight back. A funk groove under a list of absurd facts works very well as a narration bed because the loop never changes. It suits comic villains and brisk, sarcastic narration. It would undermine a story that needs emotional vulnerability or a sad ending, and a story that depends on the lyric being heard clearly in a long melodic line.

## Asking Suno for it

In Suno, the Style box works well with rhythm words: 'raw 1960s-70s funk, locked one-chord groove, tight sixteenth-note drums, muted chicken-scratch rhythm guitar, slapped electric bass riff, horn stabs, shouted vocal with band calls, dry close-mic live band, 108 bpm'. Exclude: 'EDM, house, disco strings, synth bass, pop chorus, autotune, trap, ballad, reverb wash, slick production'. In the lyric box use short chant lines and bracketed band calls ('[Shout] Hit it!'); avoid a long melodic chorus. For an instrumental bed, add 'instrumental, no vocals'.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno funk tends to drift to disco (strings, four-on-the-floor kick, wah-wah pop) or to a clean modern 'nu-funk' with a smooth lead and a synth bass, and it often lacks the tight micro-timing and rests that make funk. It tends to resolve chords and add a melodic pop chorus, breaking the one-chord rule. Bass can come out as muddy or as a generic synth. The shout and the band calls are weak, and the vocal can sound polite. Fix: say 'one chord', 'dry', 'live band', exclude 'disco', 'strings' and 'chorus hook', and keep the groove section long. Funk grooves loop well, so a short good loop can be extended in post.

## Production cost

Easy for instrumental beds, medium for vocal tracks. The groove is repetitive, which helps consistency from take to take. Hard to get micro-timing right; a take either locks or it doesn't, so expect to audition several. Great for loops under narration at 30-90 seconds.
