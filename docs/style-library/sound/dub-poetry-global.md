# Dub poetry

> Spoken-word verse delivered in Caribbean-inflected English rhythm over a dub or reggae bass line, used for protest and reportage.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Dub poetry came out of Jamaican performance poets and was made a British form by Linton Kwesi Johnson, whose records with Dennis Bovell and the Dub Band from 1978 set poetry about police, unemployment and the 1981 uprisings over heavy reggae. Benjamin Zephaniah and others developed it into a wider performance practice. It is the least obvious entry in this family: it is rhythm-led speech that sits between song and monologue, which makes it close to what a narrator already does. It is a form where the voice is the instrument and the bed stays under it.

## Signature traits

- Spoken or half-chanted delivery with a steady, deliberate cadence that locks to the bass line
- Lyrics in phonetic dialect written down as sound, with an indictment structure (a statement of what happened, then what it means)
- Tempo about 65-80 BPM, heavy bass and drum, minimal harmony and sparse horn
- Backing is often a plain repeating riff that leaves space for every syllable
- Short, hard-edged lines, repeated phrases, and a refrain returned to as a hook
- Subjects are specific: a named street, a named law, a named event
- Voice dry and close in the mix, music rolling underneath, sometimes with a horn answering at line ends
- Usually one voice, with no singing and little chorus

## References

- *Dread Beat an' Blood* — Linton Kwesi Johnson (Poet and the Roots), 1978. The founding British dub poetry album, poems about police and fascism over bass-led reggae.
- *Sonny's Lettah (Anti-Sus Poem)* — Linton Kwesi Johnson, 1979. A letter-form poem about a stop-and-search arrest, a model of story-in-a-voice with a punchline of injustice.
- *Inglan Is a Bitch* — Linton Kwesi Johnson, 1980. A migrant's work-life told in plain, repeated, bitterly ironic lines over reggae.
- *Rasta* — Benjamin Zephaniah (with the Wailers), 1983. A British poet's album, showing a wider, more humorous and performative cousin of the form.

## What it carries

Authoritative, dry, bitter, witty, patient. A single voice with total authority is exactly the shape of the BadCode narrator. It fights against anything busy, and against any voice that sounds like it is performing someone else's culture.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Narration as the lead element: a monologue indicting a policy, a ledger read aloud, a story of one worker's life. The strongest fit of any style here for dry bureaucratic satire. It would undermine ensembles, cheerful pop structures, and a narrator who is not able to own the register honestly: cultural borrowing needs care.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno is not a spoken-word engine, so this is best made in two layers: the bed from Suno ('instrumental dub reggae, 70 BPM, sparse, heavy bass, space for a voice') and the spoken voice from a TTS or recorded source. If done in Suno alone, use 'spoken word, deadpan, rhythmic speech over reggae bass' with [Spoken] lyric tags and exclude 'singing, rap, autotune, melody'. Write lines in the target voice's own natural English, not phonetically. Exclude: 'rap, hip hop, trap, singing, choir, pop'.

## How generators get it wrong

Asked for 'spoken word reggae', Suno most often turns the lines into rap or sung-chant with a melody, and it adds backing vocals. Voices drift into a stage-Jamaican caricature or a slam-poetry 'performance' tone with swelling emphasis, which is the opposite of the form's flat precision. The bed will tend to over-fill the space. Fix with a separate dry voice layer and a sparse instrumental bed made separately.

## Production cost

Layering a voice over a bed is cheap in a timeline but the voice itself is the hard part: a TTS voice that carries a flat, rhythmic, regional register without sounding mocking is difficult, and a fabricated Caribbean accent from a non-Caribbean voice is a real reputational risk. Using a British regional voice in the same cadence is the safer route. Each take is easy to align because of the steady pulse.
