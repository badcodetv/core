# Jazz poetry and spoken-word groove

> A deadpan speaking voice delivering sharp political verse over congas, a simple bass and loose jazz or soul instrumentation, halfway between a poem and a song.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The proto-rap tradition of the late 1960s and 1970s, from the Last Poets in Harlem and Gil Scott-Heron with the pianist Brian Jackson. The voice is half-spoken, half-sung, close to conversation, with a laid-back band that never competes. It is the style most like the BadCode narrator: an authoritative, sarcastic voice talking directly to the listener about politics and money, with jazz underneath. It is the 'less obvious' entry here because it is closer to narration than to a song, which makes it the most directly usable as a narration bed.

## Signature traits

- Baritone speaking voice, deadpan, with tight timing and dry comic punches
- Lyrics as a list of ironic observations, ending in a refrain that repeats the title line
- Congas and hand percussion as the main pulse, with a minimal drum kit or none
- Upright or electric bass playing a repeating one- or two-bar riff
- Flute, Rhodes electric piano or soprano saxophone answering the voice
- Tempo from 80 to 105 beats per minute, relaxed and conversational
- Sparse arrangement with space left for the words
- Live studio or club recording sound, dry and warm

## References

- *The Revolution Will Not Be Televised* — Gil Scott-Heron, 1970. A conga-and-voice poem on first release, later a band version: the model of a sarcastic list over a groove.
- *Whitey on the Moon* — Gil Scott-Heron, 1970. A spoken satire about the space budget and poverty at home: close to BadCode's economic-satire target.
- *Winter in America* — Gil Scott-Heron and Brian Jackson, 1974. A sung-spoken, jazz-soul piece with a Rhodes piano and flute: the mature, mournful side of the style.
- *The Last Poets* — The Last Poets, 1970. The debut album of a Harlem group chanting poems over congas: the rawest form of the tradition.

## What it carries

Dry, knowing, sarcastic, cool and angry underneath. It sounds like an intelligent person who has seen it all and is amused despite themselves. It fights against sentimental lyrics, big hooks and anything bombastic.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Narration beds, a cold-open monologue, a poem over images, a list of absurd facts, and any scene where the narrator speaks to the audience. It is the best match in the family for BadCode's voice: sarcastic, authoritative and political. It is easy to listen to under visuals. It would undermine a story that needs a big singalong, an emotional climax with sung vocals, or a very young audience. For a UK working-class audience, the spoken delivery is accessible, but the speaker should use their own accent and not an American one.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno is not strong at pure spoken word, so the Style box has to push it: '1970s jazz poetry, deadpan spoken-word baritone, half-spoken half-sung, congas and hand percussion, upright bass riff, Rhodes electric piano, flute, sparse arrangement, relaxed 90 bpm, dry warm studio'. Exclude: 'rap flow, trap, hip-hop beat, singing melody, big chorus, choir, autotune, pop, synth, distorted guitar'. Put lines in [Spoken] or [Verse] blocks with natural punctuation and one repeated refrain. For narration used with BadCode picture, an alternative is to generate the band in Suno as an instrumental and record or generate the voice separately in a TTS tool.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno strongly prefers sung melody, and a spoken-word prompt tends to produce rapping with a trap-lite beat, or a sung vocal on a pitch. Deadpan is hard: the voice often adds emotion and a performative 'poetry reading' lilt. Its word timing drifts and syllables get swallowed in dense lines. The 'jazz' is frequently a polished lounge sound. Fix: keep lines short, write 'speak, do not sing', exclude 'rap' and 'melody', and expect to produce the band as an instrumental and lay the voice over it. If a vocal is needed, try several takes and pick the driest. Lyrics from the engine are cliché, so write them.

## Production cost

Low for the instrumental bed, high for a convincing spoken vocal in Suno. A separate voice is more controllable but needs a TTS or recorded narrator. The simple band holds consistent across takes, so a story could use one bed for the entire film. Length is no issue because the groove can loop.
