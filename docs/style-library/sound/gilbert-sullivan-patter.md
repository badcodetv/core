# Gilbert and Sullivan patter song

> A fast, crisp comic list-song in Victorian operetta style where the words are the punchline.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The patter song comes from the Savoy operas of W. S. Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan, 1870s to 1890s, light comic opera mocking British institutions: the Navy, Parliament, the House of Lords, the law. A baritone rattles out dense rhymed lists at speed over a bouncing orchestra, each verse escalating, ending in a breathless final stanza. It is the most British form of political satire in music and survives in Tom Lehrer, Flanders and Swann and musical theatre comedy.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 150 to 190 bpm, steady 2/4 or 6/8 bounce with a clipped staccato accompaniment
- Light pit orchestra: pizzicato strings, woodwinds, bassoon doubling the bass line, piano, occasional brass sting
- Syllabic setting: one syllable per note, rapid triple-rhyme lines
- Crisp, upper-class, over-enunciated baritone with every consonant audible, little vibrato
- Verse structure that speeds up or adds a twist on each repeat
- Orchestral stab and pause before the punchline rhyme
- Key change or accelerando in the last verse

## References

- *The Pirates of Penzance ('I Am the Very Model of a Modern Major-General')* — W. S. Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan, 1879. The archetype of the patter song.
- *The Mikado ('As Some Day It May Happen', the Lord High Executioner's list)* — W. S. Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan, 1885. A list of people who would not be missed, used to satirise authority.
- *Iolanthe (the Lord Chancellor's nightmare song)* — W. S. Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan, 1882. Patter at its most frantic, mocking the House of Lords and the law.
- *'The Elements'* — Tom Lehrer, 1959. Proof the form works for modern comic lists.

## What it carries

Brisk, clever, gleefully dry; it handles outrage as comedy and makes pompous people look absurd. It fights against sincerity, slow emotion and anything that needs a long held note.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire of institutions and the people who run them: a minister reciting excuses, a list of what the rich are owed, the fiction of 'we can't afford it' as an escalating list. Very good for explaining a policy joke fast. It undermines a story that needs a working-class voice that does not sound posh unless the posh voice is the target, which it can well be.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno can do the bounce but struggles with true syllabic speed. Prompt: 'Victorian comic opera patter song, light orchestra, pizzicato strings, bassoon, piano, fast staccato, crisp clear baritone, rapid-fire rhymed list, theatrical, British'. Lyrics matter more than the Style box: write lines of 10 to 12 even syllables with strict rhyme and put a [Patter] or [Verse] tag per stanza; short, evenly metred lines survive best. Exclude: 'opera vibrato, soprano, choir, pop, hip hop, electronic'. Avoid naming the composers or Lehrer.

## How generators get it wrong

Two common failures: the singer slows down and holds notes, turning patter into a ballad; and the voice becomes a full operatic vibrato with wrong stresses. It also tends to mumble fast syllables, dropping words so the joke is lost. Fixes are shorter lines, even metre, 'staccato, clear diction, no vibrato', and re-rolling many takes. Rhymes stay sound-alike but not funny if the lyric is weak, so the writing must carry it.

## Production cost

Words-first and very take-hungry because fast intelligibility is hit and miss; expect to re-roll or fix lines. One voice per story is easy if the baritone is specified. Short tracks of 1.5 to 2.5 minutes work best. Lyric clarity needs checking by ear every take.
