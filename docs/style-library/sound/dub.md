# Dub

> A reggae track stripped to drums and bass and then played through the mixing desk with echo, reverb and drop-outs as the lead instrument.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Dub grew out of Jamaican studios in the late 1960s and 1970s, where engineers such as King Tubby and Lee Perry remixed instrumental versions on the desk by muting channels and throwing echo and spring reverb. In Britain it fused with sound-system culture and post-punk, via Dennis Bovell, Adrian Sherwood's On-U Sound and others. It is mostly instrumental, built on silence as much as sound. For a film it works as a score because there is room for voice.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 65-80 BPM, heavy one-drop drums with snare hits sent into long echo
- Bass is huge, rounded and forward, often in a repeated two-bar riff
- Vocal or horn fragments appear for a word or two and are thrown into delay then vanish
- Mixing-desk moves audible: sudden mute of the whole band, filter sweeps, spring-reverb crashes, tape-delay feedback spirals
- Sparse organ stabs and guitar chops that drop in and out
- Long tracks, 4-8 minutes, with space and repetition rather than verses
- Dark, wet, tape-saturated sound, with low end that dominates

## References

- *King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown* — Augustus Pablo, 1976. The canonical Jamaican dub record, built on desk-played echo and silence.
- *Super Ape* — The Upsetters (Lee Perry), 1976. Dub as strange, eerie, studio-as-instrument texture.
- *Brain Damage* — Dennis Bovell (as Dennis Bovell Dub Band), 1981. A London-made dub album by the producer behind much British reggae.
- *My Life in a Hole in the Ground* — African Head Charge, 1981. On-U Sound's British dub with percussion and tape-made strangeness; shows dub moving beyond reggae.
- *Dread Beat an' Blood* — Linton Kwesi Johnson (Poet and the Roots), 1978. Poetry set on a dub bed, the model for voice over dub in Britain.

## What it carries

Hypnotic, ominous, spacious and slightly sinister. It can carry paranoia, surveillance, haunting and a sense that the system is machinery. It fights against jokes, quick plot beats and dense lyrics, because it wants room.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A narrator speaking over a bed: the future-warning voice is a natural fit. Strong for scenes about surveillance, ghost towns, closed factories, emptiness, and for transitions and drops. It would undermine a punchline-per-line comedy song or anything that needs to be sung along to.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno responds to 'dub', 'dub reggae', 'instrumental dub', 'tape delay', 'spring reverb', 'heavy sub bass', 'drop-outs', 'one drop', and 'mixing desk effects'. Put 'instrumental' in Style and leave lyrics empty or use [Instrumental] tags to avoid it adding vocals. Ask for 'echo on the snare, sudden muted drop-outs, bass-forward, 70 BPM'. Exclude: 'dubstep, EDM, wobble bass, drop, trap, vocals, rapping, house, pop'. Ask for a simple repeating bass riff so it stays under a voice.

## How generators get it wrong

The word 'dub' routinely tips the model toward dubstep (wobble bass, big EDM drops), which is a different genre. The fix is to name 'reggae dub, 1970s, analogue tape' and put 'dubstep' and 'wobble' in Exclude. The effects are usually baked-in smears, so true desk moves such as a whole-band mute then a snare crash seldom land on cue. Tracks often get busy, with too many melodic layers where real dub is mostly empty. It also tends to add a vocal unprompted.

## Production cost

Easy to get a loop, hard to get a performance with deliberate drop-outs, which usually need stem editing in Suno Studio or in a DAW. Because it is instrumental, voice consistency is not a problem, and the repetitive nature makes it good for long loops under narration.
