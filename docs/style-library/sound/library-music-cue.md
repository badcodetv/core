# Library music and broadcast cue

> Instrumental production music of the KPM and BBC-era kind, made to sit under pictures, with named moods like 'Industrial Progress' or 'Tension'.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Library (production) music is catalogued, instrumental music made to be licensed for TV, film and advertising, particularly strong in 1960s-80s Britain (KPM, De Wolfe, Bruton and others). Cues are named for a mood or a scene, often come in 30- and 60-second versions, and use real session players on funk, jazz, early synths and orchestral textures. Later reissues gave it a cult audience. It is the sound of British daytime TV, schools programmes and public-information broadcasting.

## Signature traits

- Instrumental, with a clear mood title and a standardised cue shape: intro, body, button ending
- Funk or jazz-funk rhythm section: tight drums, fuzzy electric bass, wah guitar, Hammond organ, Moog or ARP synth lines
- Brass and string stabs, vibraphone or flute carrying the melody
- Dry, close-miked 1970s studio sound with tape saturation
- Strong rhythmic loop that can sit under dialogue without clashing
- Short 'stinger' and 'button' endings, and alternative lengths (30s/60s/full)
- Stylised moods: 'Sport', 'Industry', 'Suspense', 'Space', 'Corporate Progress'

## References

- *Grange Hill theme ('Chicken Man')* — Alan Hawkshaw, 1978. A library composer's cue that became one of Britain's most recognisable TV themes. **(Corrected: Alan Hawkshaw wrote 'Chicken Man' as library music (c.1975, Munich) and Grange Hill used it from its first series in 1978. The year 1978 is the TV debut, not the composition.)**
- *The Willows* — Belbury Poly (Ghost Box), 2004. A modern album that recreates the half-remembered sound of British library and radiophonic music.
- *Mind How You Go* — The Advisory Circle (Ghost Box), 2005. Takes the sound of public-information and educational TV music and makes it uncanny.
- *The KPM 1000 Series* — KPM (Keith Prowse Music) library, 1960s-80s. The catalogue itself: the canonical reference for the era's cues and their naming conventions.

## What it carries

Brisk, authoritative and impersonal. It carries tension, momentum, bureaucratic cheer and nostalgia; it can underscore a satirical 'official broadcast' beautifully. It fights against a human voice and emotional intimacy.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Strong as a score or narration bed for a story told in the voice of an institution: a mock-documentary, a government briefing, a corporate video. Good for a UK audience that grew up on it. It would undermine a story that needs emotional vulnerability, or one where a lyric is the point.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno is quite good at instrumentals with era and instrument detail: 'instrumental 1970s British library music, jazz-funk, tight drums, fuzzy bass, wah guitar, Hammond organ, Moog lead, brass stabs, 105 BPM, dry close-miked, tape saturation, cue with a button ending'. Use [Instrumental] and omit lyrics, or put only structure tags. Exclude: 'vocals, lyrics, modern EDM, trap, polished 2020s production, autotune'. Cue 'mono' or 'vintage broadcast' to avoid clean modern masters. Ask for a specific mood name as the title idea, e.g. 'tense industrial progress', but no real cue titles.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno drifts toward 'lo-fi beats' or generic funk that sounds like a modern YouTube background track, and its endings often fade rather than finish with a button. It tends to add vocals or vocal chops unless excluded. Its idea of 'vintage' is often a filtered modern mix rather than a dry 1970s studio. Fix: specify dry, close-miked, tape, name period instruments, require a 'hard stop button ending' and exclude vocal textures explicitly.

## Production cost

Cheap and robust: instrumentals are Suno's strongest category and consistency across cues is easy because the genre is built to be uniform. The risk is blandness; the cues must be editable to length and fit under narration, which needs trimming in an editor.
