# Chiptune / 8-bit

> Music made from the square, pulse, triangle and noise waves of old home computers and games consoles, fast, bright and melodic.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Chiptune comes from the sound chips in 1980s machines (Commodore 64's SID, the NES, Game Boy), where composers like Rob Hubbard and Koji Kondo wrote complete tunes with three or four channels. It was revived by demoscene and bedroom musicians and mixed with punk and dance in the 2000s. It is catchy, compact and heavily associated with games, childhood and a particular working-class bedroom computer culture in 1980s Britain.

## Signature traits

- Square and pulse waves for lead, triangle wave for bass, noise channel for hi-hat and snare
- Only a few simultaneous voices; fast arpeggios stand in for chords
- Tempo often 130-170 BPM with a tight, rigid grid and no swing
- Very short, repeating melodic loops with immediate hooks
- Small number of sections, loop-ready structure
- Bright, harsh high end; no reverb, no natural sound
- Pitch bends, vibrato and duty-cycle sweeps on the pulse wave as the main expression

## References

- *Super Mario Bros. (Ground Theme)* — Koji Kondo, 1985. The most recognisable piece of 8-bit music; shows how much melody three channels can carry.
- *Monty on the Run* — Rob Hubbard, 1985. A Commodore 64 SID score that was a landmark for British computer music.
- *Spacejunk* — Chipzel, 2012. ## What it carries

Can carry playfulness, childhood, comedy, frantic energy, game-over absurdity, and a surprising poignancy when played slow. It fights against gravity, and against any scene that needs to feel real or tragic at face value.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satire about 'the game': a system that is a game board, levels, scores, high-score tables, players who cannot win. It fits stories that treat economics as a rigged videogame or a loading screen. It would undermine serious testimony or a scene of real human suffering, where the toy sound turns it flippant.

## Asking Suno for it

Describe the chip: 8-bit chiptune, square wave lead, pulse wave arpeggios, triangle wave bass, noise channel drums, Commodore 64 SID sound or NES style, 150 BPM, no reverb, no samples, game-music loop. Use [Instrumental] for most uses. Exclude: realistic instruments, reverb, orchestral, guitar, vocals, modern EDM, pop production. Naming a specific console helps the timbre; naming a game is less reliable.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to produce a modern 'chiptune-flavoured' pop or EDM track, with a full-range mix, sidechain and a supersaw, using a few bleeps as decoration. It also adds reverb and polished stereo. Real constraints (three voices, no reverb, hard pitch steps) are rarely held. The fix is to repeat 'only square, triangle and noise waves, no reverb, no pads, dry mono-like mix' and exclude modern production words. Vocals added to chiptune usually sound like a pop singer and break the illusion.

## Production cost

Cheap, fast and consistent because the palette is tiny, and loops cut easily to picture. Risk is novelty fatigue and a mismatch with any serious subject. A story must commit to the game conceit for it to work.
