# UK conversational rap

> Half-spoken, off-the-beat British storytelling about ordinary life, delivered in a regional accent over loose garage or hip-hop drums.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

This is the British working-class strand of hip-hop that talks rather than boasts. Mike Skinner defined it in 2002 with Original Pirate Material: a flat, deadpan, mostly spoken voice over UK garage and hip-hop beats, telling day-to-day stories about rent, a lost wallet, a night out. Later artists pushed it into social realism (Plan B's Ill Manors) and politics. The craft is detail: specific brands, bus routes and sums of money, delivered like a mate telling you something over a pint.

## Signature traits

- Tempo 130-140 BPM half-time feel, or a 2-step garage shuffle around 130 BPM
- Vocal is conversational, only loosely on the grid, with pauses and sentence rhythm rather than bar-locked rhyme
- Regional accent kept, with glottal stops and local slang left in
- Simple, repetitive keyboard or piano riff, plucked strings or a cheap-sounding synth
- Dry, intimate close mic, little effect, sometimes a slightly out-of-tune sung hook
- Lyrics full of mundane specifics: prices, brands, places, times
- Humour and self-deprecation in a deadpan voice, with a sudden sincere turn
- Occasional second voice answering or interrupting the narrator

## References

- *Original Pirate Material* — The Streets, 2002. The founding album: spoken storytelling about UK ordinary life over garage and hip-hop
- *Ill Manors* — Plan B, 2012. Narrative social-realist rap with strings and a big, angry, orchestral beat
- *Let Them Eat Chaos* — Kae Tempest, 2016. Spoken-rapped, one-night-in-one-street narrative with a political undertow
- *Nothing Great About Britain* — slowthai, 2019. Raw working-class UK rap that mixes humour, class anger and punk energy

## What it carries

Warm, funny, tired and honest; it can carry quiet dread and anger because the voice sounds like someone you know. It fights against grandeur and authority, so it undercuts the total-certainty narrator unless that is made a feature.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A working-class British reader is the home audience. It suits satire built from small, true details, and monologues about wages, housing, the job centre, the group chat. It would undermine epic or cosmic stakes, and anything needing fast dense wordplay.

## Asking Suno for it

Style box: 'British spoken-word rap, conversational deadpan delivery, half-rapped half-spoken, UK garage 2-step drums, 130 BPM, simple piano riff, dry close vocal, working-class London (or Northern) accent'. Tag lyrics [Spoken] and [Verse] and write them like speech with natural sentence breaks; avoid forced rhyme. Exclude: 'American accent, trap, autotune, melodic singing, choir, big pop chorus'. Naming a specific city or region helps but is only partly followed.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno often defaults to an American or neutral accent and to a polished melodic rap, and it may sing the hook in a smooth pop voice. Deadpan humour gets lost because delivery is too energetic. Fix: write short, flat sentences, put a stage-direction-style cue in the lyrics where it helps, repeat the accent in Style, and keep the arrangement very simple so the model does not add a pop build.

## Production cost

Voice consistency is the hard part; regional accents drift between takes and across a full story. Spoken-style tracks are forgiving on timing, which helps cutting to picture. Short stand-alone pieces are easy; a 5-minute narrative needs a fixed voice plan.
