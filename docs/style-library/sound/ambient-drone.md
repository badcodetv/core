# Ambient / drone

> Slow, beatless washes of sustained synth and tone designed to sit under narration and set a mood without demanding attention.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A style that emerged from Brian Eno's 1978 definition of ambient music as something that is as ignorable as it is interesting, then grew through British chill-out and electronic ambient in the early 90s. It uses long-held pads, slow filter movement, tape-like loops and space rather than beat or melody. It is the most useful style here as a score for a spoken narrator because it leaves frequency room for the voice.

## Signature traits

- No drums, or only a very soft pulse; no clear tempo
- Long sustained synth pads with slow attack and long reverb tails
- Slowly evolving texture: a filter opening over minutes, or two detuned tones drifting
- Very sparse melody: a few piano notes or bell tones over a drone
- Low dynamic range and low overall level so it sits under speech
- Stereo width and heavy reverb or tape delay for a sense of place
- Long form; sections change by texture rather than structure

## References

- *Music for Airports* — Brian Eno, 1978. The founding ambient record, designed to be ignorable and interesting at once.
- *Selected Ambient Works 85-92* — Aphex Twin, 1992. Warm, melodic electronic ambient, with soft beats that still work under a voice.
- *Adventures Beyond the Ultraworld* — The Orb, 1991. British ambient house built from samples and long drifting pads.
- *Apollo: Atmospheres and Soundtracks* — Brian Eno, 1983. Ambient made for film; shows it working as a score for images.
- *The Tired Sounds of Stars of the Lid* — Stars of the Lid, 2001. A long-form drone record, built from sustained strings and synth with no pulse.

## What it carries

Can carry awe, calm, loneliness, vastness, dread by slowness, and the cold perspective of looking from far away. It cannot carry jokes, urgency or a call to action. It fights against anything that needs energy or an argument pushed forward.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

The narrator's long explanatory passages, the 'view from the future' stretches, a still or slow-moving image sequence, the aftermath of a disaster. It would undermine satire that depends on timing and attitude, or a call to anger; used alone it can feel like a corporate wellness video.

## Asking Suno for it

Use [Instrumental] and describe: beatless ambient, slow evolving analog pads, long reverb, sustained drone, sparse piano notes, no drums, no melody hook, very quiet, wide stereo, tape hiss. Say 'no percussion' in the style box and add 'drums, beat, bass drop, vocals, melody hook, build-up' to Exclude. Ask for a low-register, dark colour or a bright, open colour to choose mood. Song length can be set by the generation limit; very long drones usually need extending or looping.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to add a beat, a lead melody, or a chord progression 'journey' with a crescendo, turning ambient into generic cinematic or meditation music. It also fills space with arpeggios and pianos. Tracks often end early with a fade, and the same sound repeats across takes, so a story's cues can feel identical. Fix with explicit 'no drums, no melody, no build, static' and Exclude entries; vary the mood with a specific timbre (detuned organ, glass, tape choir) rather than adjectives.

## Production cost

Very consistent, cheap and easy to loop, which makes it the safest bed under narration. The risk is blandness: it is easy to get the 'spa' sound that signals nothing. Needs a distinctive timbre choice per story to avoid being forgettable.
