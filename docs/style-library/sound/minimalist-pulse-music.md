# Minimalism (pulse music)

> Steady repeated patterns that shift gradually, pulsing eighth notes in pianos, mallets and voices, building trance-like momentum.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A style that began in 1960s New York and California (Reich, Glass, Riley, Adams) built on short repeating cells, additive rhythm and slow phase or harmonic change. A constant pulse (often marimba, piano, wordless voices, clarinet) runs underneath, so the listener perceives change as an accumulating pressure. It has been the default 'significant, inevitable' sound of documentaries and dystopias for forty years.

## Signature traits

- Constant eighth-note pulse at 120-160 bpm, no swing, no fills
- Repeating 2-8 note cells that add or drop one note at a time (additive process)
- Two instruments playing the same pattern slightly out of phase, drifting apart and back
- Arpeggiated major/minor triads in piano, organ or marimba; tonic and mediant shifts rather than melodies
- Wordless soprano or choir singing 'ah' or solfege syllables on the pulse
- Bright, dry, undistorted acoustic or lightly processed tone; no kick-snare backbeat
- Very long form: one idea for 5-15 minutes, with climax as texture density, not melody
- Sudden harmonic pivot every 16 or 32 bars as the only 'event'

## References

- *Music for 18 Musicians* — Steve Reich, 1976. The pulsing marimba-and-voices reference; harmony changes in cycles over a constant pulse.
- *Einstein on the Beach* — Philip Glass and Robert Wilson, 1976. Counted numbers and solfege sung over arpeggios; shows repetition as drama.
- *Different Trains* — Steve Reich, 1988. String quartet shadowing recorded speech melodies; direct model for narration-as-music.
- *Koyaanisqatsi (score)* — Philip Glass, film by Godfrey Reggio, 1982. Pulse music welded to images of mass systems, the template for technology-and-society montage.
- *Short Ride in a Fast Machine* — John Adams, 1986. Woodblock pulse with brass; the exhilarating, slightly threatening side of the style.

## What it carries

Can carry inevitability, vertigo, systems running without anyone steering, awe, anxiety, and ecstatic release. It fights against warmth, jokes and intimate confession; it sounds impersonal by design.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about machinery that runs without people: automation, algorithms, markets, the same day repeating. Excellent under narration because the pulse leaves the spoken line clear, and a narrator reading statistics over a slow harmonic climb has a strong shape. Undermines a story whose emotional point is one particular person's anger or tenderness; the pulse flattens individuals into parts of a system, which can be the point or a failure.

## Asking Suno for it

Words Suno responds to: 'minimalist', 'pulse music', 'repetitive piano arpeggios', 'marimba', 'phasing', 'additive rhythm', 'wordless soprano vocalise', 'chamber orchestra', 'steady eighth-note pulse', 'gradual build', 'dry acoustic'. Give a bpm. For voices put [Instrumental] or lyrics like '[Verse]\nOoh\nAh' to get vocalise. Exclude: 'drums, drum kit, bass drop, synth lead, EDM, trailer hit, riser, lyrics, rap'. If narration is to sit over it, ask for 'sparse, no melody in the vocal register, leaving room for spoken voice'.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to treat 'minimalist' as cinematic ambient: slow piano with big reverb and no real pulse, or it adds a trailer-style riser at the build. True phasing between two parts is not reliably produced; expect a locked loop instead. Long repetition can degrade into a glitch or a sudden unrelated section around the 2-minute mark. Fix by asking for 'strict repetition, no new instruments, no riser' and by accepting short takes spliced in an editor.

## Production cost

Low cost per take and very tolerant of looping, so cheap to extend. The risk is sameness across takes (everything comes out as piano arpeggio in C minor) and drift late in long generations. Easy to cut to 8-second visual rhythm because the pulse gives natural edit points.
