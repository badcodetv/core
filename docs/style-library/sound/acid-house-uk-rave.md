# Acid house / UK rave

> Squelching 303 basslines over a hard 909 four-on-the-floor, built for dancing and collective release, with samples and shouted hooks.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Born in Chicago in 1987 and exploding in Britain in 1988-89, when warehouse and field parties made it a mass working-class youth movement that the government and tabloids panicked about. The sound is a Roland TB-303 bass synth with a squelching filter sweep over a Roland 909 drum machine, with vocal samples and piano stabs. It is as much a social event as a style, and carries the history of an unlicensed gathering that the state tried to ban.

## Signature traits

- Tempo around 118-130 BPM with a heavy four-on-the-floor 909 kick, open hi-hat on the off-beat, handclaps on 2 and 4
- TB-303-style resonant bassline with a filter that sweeps open and closed over minutes (the 'acid' squelch)
- Short looping riff patterns; tension built through filter movement and layered percussion, not melody
- Sampled vocal fragments, shouts or a looped phrase rather than a verse-chorus lyric
- Piano or organ stabs and breakdowns that drop the beat then crash back in
- Raw, loud, slightly distorted drum machine sound; little polish
- Long tracks (6-8 minutes) designed to mix, not to tell a story

## References

- *Acid Tracks* — Phuture, 1987. The record that defined the 303 acid sound.
- *Pacific State* — 808 State, 1989. A Manchester acid house classic and a key UK dance record.
- *What Time Is Love?* — The KLF, 1988. Acid house with chants and sampled hooks; an exemplar of rave as a joyful, ironic spectacle.
- *Born Slippy .NUXX* — Underworld, 1996. A working-class British dance record that became the sound of a generation's comedown, famously on the Trainspotting soundtrack.
- *Chime* — Orbital, 1989. A British rave record built from a simple repeating riff and 303 sweeps, made in a bedroom.

## What it carries

Can carry euphoria, defiance, collective release, hedonistic anger, and a threat to the order. It can turn sinister with a sustained 303 and no vocals. It fights against subtlety, intimacy and anything that needs the words to be understood.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about the state versus ordinary people, bans, curfews, crowd energy, or a short cutting montage where momentum is the point. A good bed under a fast, many-shot montage. It would undermine long narrated explanation, because the 303 and the kick compete with a speaking voice.

## Asking Suno for it

Describe the elements: TB-303-style squelchy acid bassline with resonant filter sweeps, 909 four-on-the-floor kick, open hi-hat, handclaps, 124 BPM, piano stabs, looping vocal sample, raw warehouse mix. Use [Instrumental] and [Breakdown] / [Build] / [Drop] tags in the lyrics to shape structure. Suno handles 'acid house' as a term but needs 'late 80s' and 'raw' to avoid a clean modern club sound. Exclude: EDM festival drop, supersaw, vocals with verses, pop chorus, trap, dubstep wobble, autotune.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends toward polished modern club or EDM, with a festival build-and-drop, a clean sidechained bass and an anthemic vocal. A true 303 filter sweep that evolves slowly is hard to get; takes often loop a static bass pattern. Vocal samples tend to be rendered as sung hooks, not sampled fragments. Fix with 'raw', 'lo-fi', 'warehouse', no 'drop' language, and instrumental-first prompts; mix will always be cleaner than real tape and drum machine sounds.

## Production cost

Easy for the engine to produce a pulse at constant tempo, so consistency is decent, but narrative control is poor: it is hard to land a track to match picture beats. Tracks are best used as a whole and cut in the edit rather than steered by lyrics.
