# Agitprop workers' chorus and march song

> A plain, hard-driving mass song or ballad for a crowd, written to be sung on a picket line and heard as a call to act.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A tradition from 1920s and 30s socialist theatre and film that put political argument into marching songs and workers' choruses. Hanns Eisler's songs for Brecht and for the film Kuhle Wampe were written to be learned and sung by crowds, with a driving rhythm and plain verses. In Britain Ewan MacColl and Charles Parker's BBC Radio Ballads (1958 onward) set working people's recorded voices to folk-based music, and Marc Blitzstein's The Cradle Will Rock was a left-wing musical that was nearly banned. It is the least ironic form in this family and the most direct.

## Signature traits

- Tempo about 100 to 120 bpm march or steady walking pace, strong downbeat
- Unison or two-part male and mixed chorus, with a solo verse and a crowd refrain
- Piano, brass band or accordion and drum, sometimes a plain acoustic guitar
- Simple diatonic melody in a minor key lifting to major on the last line
- Short repeated slogan refrain easy to learn on one hearing
- Plain, declarative, concrete lyrics about wages, hunger and solidarity
- Rough, unpolished, live-sounding recording with the sense of many voices in a hall

## References

- *Solidaritätslied (Song of Solidarity), from Kuhle Wampe* — Hanns Eisler, lyrics by Bertolt Brecht, 1932. A film workers' anthem written to be sung by crowds.
- *Einheitsfrontlied (United Front Song)* — Hanns Eisler, lyrics by Bertolt Brecht, 1934. A plain march built to be learned by a mass audience.
- *The Ballad of John Axon (BBC Radio Ballad)* — Ewan MacColl, Peggy Seeger and Charles Parker, 1958. British working-class story told through folk song and real voices.
- *The Cradle Will Rock* — Marc Blitzstein, 1937. A pro-union musical about a corrupt steel boss, famously staged without sets after a shutdown.

## What it carries

Earnest, defiant and communal; it can hold anger, pride and hope without a smirk. It fights against irony, cleverness and any hint of looking down on the people singing.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

A story's moral turn or ending, where the argument stops being a joke and becomes a call to stand together; works for strikes, wages and housing. It would undermine a satirical or cynical narrator and can feel preachy or like a lecture if used for the whole piece, which the reader research warns against.

## Asking Suno for it

Say 'folk singer solo verse, crowd refrain'. Lyrics need a very short, repeated refrain tagged [Chorus]. Avoid naming Eisler, MacColl or Billy Bragg. Exclude: 'pop, EDM, autotune, polished, studio gloss, rock band, trap, electronic'.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to produce a glossy inspirational 'protest anthem' with stadium drums and polished gospel choir, or a folk-pop singalong, rather than rough and unadorned. Crowd vocals come out as a smooth studio choir. Lyrics get abstract and slogan-heavy; keep them concrete. Fix with 'rough, unpolished, live in a hall, brass band' and exclude 'stadium, gospel, inspirational pop'. Without care it also becomes sentimental.

## Production cost

Low-to-moderate. Short simple lyric and repeated refrain stay consistent. The crowd texture is the hard part; a single lead voice with a chorus may need layering across takes.
