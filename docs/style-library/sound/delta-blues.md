# Pre-war delta blues

> One voice and one acoustic guitar, bottleneck slide or hard-stomped rhythm, singing a short repeated line about money, work and trouble.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

The blues of the Mississippi Delta in the late 1920s and 1930s, recorded on location by labels for the 'race records' market and later by folklorists. Its form is the twelve-bar (or loose, irregular) AAB verse: a line, the same line again, then an answer that twists it. The sound is raw, rhythmic, and often broke: sharecropping, the landlord, the levee, the hard times of the Depression. It is the oldest and sparest style in this family, and the one with the strongest 'a man alone telling you the truth' authority.

## Signature traits

- Solo male voice, rough and close-miked, with spoken asides and growled or falsetto breaks
- Acoustic guitar in open tuning, bottleneck slide squealing on the answer line
- Thumb plays a steady alternating bass while fingers answer the voice (call and response between voice and guitar)
- AAB lyric structure: line one, line one repeated, line three punches or reverses it
- Irregular bar counts: the singer holds or cuts a bar when the line is done, no click track
- Tempo 60-100 bpm, a drag and a stomp rather than a swing
- Recorded sound: mono, room noise, a hiss and a narrow bandwidth
- Lyrics concrete and economic: the boss, the rent, the crop, the road out

## References

- *Hard Time Killing Floor Blues* — Skip James, 1931. Falsetto, minor-key guitar, and a lyric about the Depression, with no relief in it.
- *Cross Road Blues* — Robert Johnson, 1936. The canonical slide-driven delta recording, with walking bass and the sung line answered by guitar.
- *Pony Blues* — Charley Patton, 1929. Rhythmic, shouted, percussive guitar: shows how loud and driven the earliest delta style was.
- *Death Letter* — Son House, 1965. Slide and voice with huge intensity; the 1965 recording shows the stark style at full strength.
- *Parchman Farm Blues* — Bukka White, 1940. A first-person song about forced prison labour, direct and political without sermonising.

## What it carries

Lonely, dry, knowing, dangerous and darkly funny. It can carry grim humour, grievance and a quiet warning. It fights against cheerfulness, large scale, optimism-by-default and anything that needs a crowd.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Satires about debt, rent, one boss taking all the value, a single character trapped in a system, or a narrator who has seen the whole thing before. A short punchline verse works very well because the form already repeats and then twists. It would undermine an epic, a collective uprising or a big institutional story with many characters, because a single voice and guitar cannot feel like 'everyone'. For a UK working-class audience, it lands best as the narrator's own voice, as long as the lyrics are not written in invented Southern dialect.

## Asking Suno for it

In Suno, the Style box responds to concrete production words: 'pre-war acoustic delta blues, solo male vocal, open-tuned acoustic guitar with bottleneck slide, alternating thumb bass, foot stomp, mono, close-miked, room noise, 1930s field recording' works better than 'sad blues'. Describe the AAB form in the lyrics by writing the first line twice and then the answer, and put [Verse] tags with no [Chorus]. Exclude: 'drums, bass guitar, electric guitar, backing vocals, choir, reverb, modern production, rock, pop, polish, autotune'. Names of living artists are ignored, so describe tuning, tone and room instead. Keep verses to four lines and set the tempo low in words ('slow shuffle, dragging').

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to hand back a clean, full-band 'blues rock' or 'country blues' mix with a drummer, a bass and a tidy guitar solo, because those dominate its blues training pool. Fix: exclude drums, bass and electric guitar by name, and ask for 'solo' twice. The vocal often comes out as a smooth contemporary singer doing a growl; ask for 'rough, untrained, spoken-sung'. It will fill every bar and keep perfect time, losing the irregular held bars that make delta blues feel human; short lyrics help. It tends to write lyrics in cartoon dialect ('Lawd, I's gonna...') if you let it write them; write the lyrics yourself in plain English. Authentic mono crackle is rarely convincing, and the fake vinyl hiss often sounds like a plug-in; consider adding no noise and degrading the audio in post.

## Production cost

Cheap and easy to keep consistent: one voice, one guitar, short songs. The hard part is the voice, since Suno has no single stable 'persona' across takes, so use a locked Voice/Persona if available or accept that each take is a new singer. Length is fine at 1.5-3 minutes. A narration bed in this style is practical because it is so sparse that spoken words can sit on top.
