# Hauntology / BBC-era library music

> Warped, tape-worn electronics that sound like a half-remembered public information film or 1970s schools broadcast, haunted by a lost future.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

A British strand, from the late 1990s and 2000s (Boards of Canada, the Ghost Box label, The Caretaker, early Burial), built on the sounds of the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, library music and children's television. The name comes from the idea of mourning a future that never arrived: the postwar promise of public goods, new towns and the welfare state. The sound is deliberately degraded: wobbling tape, detuned synths, field recordings and old-fashioned educational voices.

## Signature traits

- Detuned, wobbling analog synths with tape wow and flutter
- Lo-fi texture: vinyl crackle, tape hiss, bandwidth-limited high end, worn cassette sound
- Short, simple, nursery-like melodic fragments on old-sounding oscillators, in minor or modal keys
- Loose, slow, dusty breakbeat or no drums at all
- Sampled or imitated voices from public information films, schools broadcasts or archive audio
- Tempo slow, around 70-95 BPM or free time
- Sound-design details like radiophonic bleeps, spring reverb, tape echo and tape-stop effects

## References

- *Music Has the Right to Children* — Boards of Canada, 1998. The defining record of warm, worn nostalgia with a creeping unease, built from old synth and sampled children's TV.
- *An Empty Bliss Beyond This World* — The Caretaker, 2011. Old ballroom records looped and degraded until memory itself sounds like it is failing.
- *The Willows* — Belbury Poly, 2006. A Ghost Box release that explicitly evokes British folk horror, library music and radiophonic electronics.
- *Mind How You Go* — The Advisory Circle, 2004. A record made in the language of public information films.
- *Doctor Who theme (original 1963 realisation)* — Delia Derbyshire and Ron Grainer, 1963. The root of the sound: electronic music made from tape manipulation in a BBC studio.

## What it carries

Can carry elegy, nostalgic unease, institutional chill, grief for a lost public promise, a slightly sinister kindness. It cannot do fast, big or punchy. It fights against bright optimism and against any pure comedy.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Stories about what Britain used to promise and where it went: public ownership, privatisation, the NHS, the new town, the school film that told you everything would be fine. A natural fit for the 'received wisdom from a future that went wrong' framing, where the narrator is an archive broadcast. It would undermine a story set in the present tense that needs pace, or any story where nostalgia could slide into 'things were better then' without a point.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno tends to make lo-fi that is either clean with a cosmetic crackle added, or generic 'lo-fi hip hop beats to study to', with a jazzy chord and a dusty drum loop. Real tape degradation, wobble and detune are hard to get; it often sounds too tidy or too cheerfully nostalgic. Vintage announcer voices come out as modern voice acting. Fix with a very specific mood word set (unease, institutional, worn) and exclude 'lo-fi hip hop', 'chill', 'study'. The same dusty piano loop appears often, so vary the instrument and key.

## Production cost

Fairly cheap and forgiving because imperfection is the point, so wobbles and artefacts read as intentional. Consistency within a story is good with a fixed description. A recurring risk is that it stays at one mood across every take.
