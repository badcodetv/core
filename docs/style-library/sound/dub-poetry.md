# Dub poetry

> Poetry in Caribbean-British English performed over a slow, heavy reggae dub rhythm with deep bass and echo.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Dub poetry came out of Jamaican and Black British communities in the 1970s. Linton Kwesi Johnson brought it to Britain: a measured, rhythmic voice reciting protest verse over roots reggae and dub, with the bass line as a second voice. It was made for the audience of the Brixton and Notting Hill communities and dealt with policing, work, racism and class. It is the least obvious of this family, and the musical frame (slow, heavy, spacious) gives words weight and gravity.

## Signature traits

- Tempo 65 to 80 BPM, one-drop drum pattern with the kick and snare on beat 3
- Deep, round, melodic electric bass that plays a repeating line
- Offbeat guitar skank and organ bubble on the upbeats
- Spring-reverb and tape-echo throws on drums and vocal
- Voice is measured and deliberate, in a Caribbean or Black British accent, recited rather than sung
- Horn section (trombone, trumpet) playing short riffs between verses
- Verses often 4 to 8 lines with a repeated refrain
- Sparse mix with gaps filled by echo

## What it carries

Weighty, dignified, angry in a slow burn and sometimes tender; it carries moral gravity and community memory. It fights against jokes that need speed, and it needs authenticity because the style belongs to a particular community.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Slow, serious stretches: a history, a grievance told in order, a closing statement. Strong for stories of work and exploitation. It would undermine fast satire, and it must be used with care and not as a costume, since a mismatch of voice and subject can feel appropriative.

## Asking Suno for it

Style box: 'dub poetry, roots reggae dub, 72 BPM, one-drop drums, deep melodic bass, offbeat guitar skank, tape echo, spring reverb, spoken Caribbean British voice, measured delivery'. Use [Spoken] and short stanzas, spelling dialect phonetically if you want it. Exclude: 'rap, trap, autotune, EDM, pop, fast tempo, singing chorus'. Mixed echo terms (dub echo, delay throws) help create the space.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno turns it into a generic reggae pop song with sung melody, or mixes the voice too clean and dry. Dialect spellings are read unpredictably. Fix: say 'recited, not sung' and 'dub mix with heavy echo', keep stanzas short, test dialect spelling with a short generation before committing, and have a human voice or consultant check anything that depends on dialect authenticity.

## Production cost

Good bass and dub echoes come out reasonably well; the hard part is a voice that holds a consistent accent over a whole piece. Tempo is slow so cutting to picture is easy. Cultural risk is higher than technical risk.
