# Public information film soundtrack

> The sparse, eerie or jaunty music of 1970s British warning films, where a friendly voice tells you how you will die.
>
> Library entry: read only by the `story-style` interview. A story that picks this copies what it
> needs into its own look.md / sound.md; it never links here as its style.

Public information films (PIFs) were short government-commissioned warnings broadcast on British TV, particularly in the 1970s: drowning, electrical safety, farm accidents, nuclear attack. The music is a distinctive blend: thin synthesisers, a lone electric piano, folk flutes, a children's-TV jauntiness or a droning, empty menace under an authoritative male voice. The effect is an institutional calm that makes the threat worse. This is the 'official voice' register, and a UK audience carries the memory.

## Signature traits

- Sparse arrangement: a few instruments, a lot of empty space
- Early synth drones, spring reverb, wobbling oscillators, tape-echo
- Electric piano or music-box motif repeating a simple 3-5 note phrase
- Plain, measured male narrator, RP or avuncular, speaking over the music
- Bright folk-flute or recorder in a children's-TV tone shifted into a minor key
- Sudden silence or a single sound effect (a splash, a crackle) for the shock
- Short length, 30-120 seconds, ending abruptly on a slogan or a still

## References

- *Lonely Water* — Central Office of Information (narrated by Donald Pleasence), 1973. The defining menace PIF: a hooded figure, a minimal score and an unhurried voice.
- *Apaches* — Central Office of Information, 1977. A farm-safety film that moves from childlike play to a funeral; shows the whiplash of tone.
- *Charley Says* — Central Office of Information, 1973. A cartoon-and-song PIF that shows the friendly, singalong end of the range.
- *Protect and Survive* — UK Government civil-defence film series, 1975-1980. A bureaucratic voice instructing citizens on nuclear war, the template for deadpan institutional horror.

## What it carries

Chilly, polite and dread-laden, with a surprise streak of kindness: it is the voice of an authority that really does want you safe and also sounds as if it has given up. It can carry gallows humour and horror. It fights against hype, warmth and anything fast.

## What it suits, and what it would undermine

Near-perfect for the BadCode premise of a calm authority from the future issuing warnings: the narrator's voice and this score are the same thing. Strong for 'this is how it will go wrong' explainers and for deadpan horror. It would undermine energetic call-to-action pieces and any story that needs a crowd or a hero.

## Asking Suno for it

Suno handles this with the right instrument words: 'sparse 1970s British public information film score, wobbling analogue synth drone, electric piano repeating a three-note minor motif, recorder, spring reverb, tape echo, slow 70 BPM, eerie, dry, mono television sound, no drums'. For a narrated version, keep vocals out of Suno and record the voice separately, or use [Spoken] with a plain male RP voice. Exclude: 'drums, bass drop, full orchestra, big chorus, modern cinematic trailer, epic, bright pop'. Be sparing with the descriptor 'horror' as it triggers cinematic trailer clichés.

## How generators get it wrong

Suno inflates sparse requests into cinematic scores with risers, big drums and low brass, because it equates 'ominous' with 'film trailer'. It also fills the empty space this style depends on with extra layers. Narration sung or spoken over the music tends to be too emotive, where the form needs a flat, kindly delivery. Fix: say 'no trailer, no riser, no drums', request 'few instruments, long silences', and keep the voice on a separate track in a flat register.

## Production cost

Cheap in music terms and the instrumental texture is stable across takes. The hard part is the narrator's voice, which should be a single consistent flat, avuncular register across all pieces, which is better done with a dedicated voice tool than inside Suno.
