# Camping

## Key concept

Automation does not stop at the bottom. The same logic that replaces the
warehouse and the call centre comes for the manager and the financier too. The
smug man and the broken man are not different kinds of people — and this telling
proves it both ways round: **Bob isn't just a wasteman — he's got a reason. And
Tarquin isn't just a c*** — he's got empathy.** The story front-loads both
backstories so every judgement the audience makes gets dismantled by something
they already know.

## The structural unlock

The big move from the master notes: **take what the old versions tried to do at
the end and shift it to the beginning.** Meet Tarquin in 2008 — he was
instrumental in the financial crisis: c***. Meet Bob in 2008 — nice guy, wife
died: that's sad. Fast-forward eighteen years: Tarquin's king of the world, Bob
is f*****. It lines up before the story proper even starts, and the "aha"
twists (Tarquin's empathy, the ayahuasca, the five-year jump) land on top of a
foundation the reader already trusts.

## Beats

### 1. 2008 — meet Tarquin

City of London, the **old NatWest Tower** — 2008 territory. The year lands big
on screen. Before we've met anyone, the reader thinks: *this is going to be
about the financial crash.* Then: meet young Tarquin, up-and-coming star of the
trading desk, betting heavily on leveraged ETFs, a great career ahead of him —
**he was the reason 2008 hit.** A plucky young prick. We blame him.

### 2. 2008 — meet Bob

Meet Bob: a normal, nice guy — a middle manager, and the job must stay
deliberately unremarkable. Him and his wife are driving off somewhere — a
holiday. Without ever seeing a tent, we already like Bob.

### 3. The crash

The car crash happens on screen. **No narration. We do not say who died** — we
just show the crash, and when we later meet Bob alone, the reader joins the
dots themselves. (Decided: spelling out the wife's death — or hanging it on a
wank-tank driver — is too much. Leave it blank.) Fade to 2026.

### 4. 2026 — the Shard

The mirror of scene 1: the same City-of-London framing, but now it's **the
Shard**, and the year reads **2026**. Tarquin runs the biggest private equity
firm in the country. The current play: **buying up all the social housing they
can get their hands on — in lots of cases not even renting it out, because the
asset inflation makes more money than the rent ever would.** What a brilliant
businessman. King of the world. (Explicitly out of scope: currency debasement —
one idea per story, don't cram.)

### 5. The drive home — the crack in the armour

Night. The X8 wank tank pulls away from the Shard. Tarquin sees homeless people
on the streets of London and chuckles to himself: *interest rates go up 1% next
week — you lot are going to have loads more mates. And I cannot wait for my
bonus; I've laid heavy bets on it.* He laughs at his own joke — **and the smile
fades.** Hold on the face; no narrator while it drops. Then the narrator lands
the U-turn, and this is a *spell-it-out* moment (everything so far said "this
guy's a c***" — the reversal earns plain words): there's another part of
Tarquin, one he doesn't show on the outside, that feels uncomfortable with how
he just reacted. Maybe he isn't as big a c*** as we all think.

The empathy itself stays small and sideways, never guilt: **he's imagining how
cold it is out there — because his car heater broke last week.** "You'll have
more mates next week, haha… f***ing hell, you lot must be cold."

### 6. Bob outside Waitrose

First sight of the tent. Bob, ignored by every customer walking past to buy
their duck breast. Nothing plot-shaped happens — the scene *is* the point:
every reader recognises their own reaction to a homeless person outside a shop
(ignore; politely reject — which is just a kinder way of ignoring; or actually
engage — the rare one). And the front-loaded backstory pays off: *ah, that's a
shame — Bob was doing fine, then his wife died, and now he's one of these
people everyone steps around.*

### 7. Therapy

Of course Tarquin has a therapist. He brings her the drive-home feeling — the
first guilt of his entire life. *"What is this… feeling?"* Play it for comedy.
Her professional conclusion: **"I don't think there's any amount of therapy
that can help you. You need the most powerful psychedelic known to man."** The
ayahuasca is set up as a punchline and a prescription — not a personality
transplant. Tarquin doesn't suddenly become some cool spiritual guy.

### 8. The standoff

Tarquin arrives at Waitrose — stocking up before the retreat — and parks the
wank tank right by Bob's tent. Both men revert to type and both judgements are
**short** — one beat each, no prolonging. Tarquin: *"Get a job, you lazy
c***."* Bob: *"F***ing wank tanks — you're all pricks. I wish my wife was
here."* The crack in Tarquin's armour from scene 5 gets deliberately slapped
back shut. Cut.

### 9. The trip

(The forest-drive scene is **dropped** — it added nothing.) At the Welsh
retreat, keep the ceremony minimal: the shaman says her piece, Tarquin grabs
his knee, and we cut straight to *"F*** me, that kicked in quick"* and the
water drop. Then the narrator takes over: Tarquin has a profound moment about
the fragility of human existence — that he could even end up like that homeless
man he saw. **No mention of AI here.** The vision is not a literal preview of
the future; it's *wind your neck in, d***head*. (The newspaper does the AI
reveal later — don't spend it early.) As the audience scrolls/watches, his
**eyes close…**

### 10. The wake-up

**…and open — from exactly the perspective Bob had, inside the tent, looking
out.** But outside is chaos; a fire burning (maybe the only animated element).
The **narrator does the freak-out** — *what the f*** is going on* — so we never
have to animate Tarquin panicking (that's where AI slop lives). The gag that
carries the scene: **he still thinks he's tripping.** *"This is a bad trip. I
don't like it. I'm going to get in my car and go home."* He crawls out of the
tent — and he's in the Waitrose car park. And there's Bob.

### 11. The newspaper

Tarquin: *"I remember you — I saw you just yesterday."* Bob: *"No no no, mate.
That was like five years ago."* And Bob hands him the newspaper — *"see, look
at the date."* **One prop, two jobs:** the date proves five years have passed,
and the headline says the AI took the jobs — including his. This is also the
moment the narrator (who has been treating all of this as a very strong trip)
runs out of deniability. Then a flick of empathy, man-sized: *"Where the f***
have you been, then? You look in a much worse state than when I saw you last —
you alright?"* Shared empathy, two humans in the same situation. AI took the
last job. Drop the mic.

*(Detail licence, decided: yes, that world probably doesn't print newspapers.
Fiction wins — call it the last newspaper ever printed if anyone asks. And yes,
they'd still burn it: they're in survival mode; they don't care what it is.)*

### 12. The ending — embers into bad code

The two of them by the fire in the car park — **not** off in the woods; no new
locations this late. Their faces are different now: almost a smile, almost
happy to be together. *Well — we were both wrong, weren't we.* The mutual
apology happens the man way — cans clinked, nothing said straight, "I'm sorry I
misjudged you" squeezed in somewhere without ever being said blatantly. It's
cold; the newspaper goes on the fire. It burns. The camera pans up with the
smoke — **and the rising embers become the bad code.** End.

## How it's told (the devices)

- **The narrator does the heavy lifting.** Inner states, freak-outs, the
  U-turns — all narration over held images. Tarquin saying it himself is
  cheesy; the narrator saying it is the badass-from-the-future voice. This also
  dodges the uncanny valley: we never animate a character emoting.
- **The mirrored city shots.** NatWest Tower + "2008" ↔ the Shard + "2026". The
  skyline is the timestamp.
- **The blink.** Eyes close and reopen across the five-year jump — driven by
  the scroll in the comic, and possibly even better in video.
- **The POV rhyme.** Bob's view out of the tent (scene 6/8 world) and Tarquin's
  wake-up view (scene 10) are the same framing — the audience realises the two
  men are one position before anyone says it.
- **The newspaper.** Time-machine proof and AI reveal in one hand-off, then
  kindling. The prop does the exposition so nobody has to.
- **The audio is a parallel universe.** Narrator recorded, sound effects from
  libraries — never relying on a generation model for the audio track.

## Decisions log (from the master notes)

- **Structure locked; words tweakable.** The scene order above is the
  commitment. Narrator lines and scene details stay open until they're funny.
- **Backstory front-loaded** — the unlock that made everything line up.
- **Dropped:** the wife's on-screen death / wank-tank collision (too much); the
  forest-drive scene; moving the ending to the woods; any AI mention inside the
  trip; currency debasement.
- **Both characters humanised:** Bob vindicated by backstory; Tarquin's empathy
  kept small, sideways, and cold-based rather than guilty.
- **Spell-it-out vs subtle:** the scene-5 U-turn is a spell-it-out moment; most
  everything else talks around it.
- **The five missing years are never explained.** Bob asks *him* where he's
  been. We are a superintelligence from the future; we are allowed to warp
  space and time. The story overtakes reality and that's fine — it's fiction.
- **Day/night:** possibly Shard interior by day, drive-away by night. "Ah,
  details." Open.
