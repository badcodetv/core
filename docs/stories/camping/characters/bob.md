# Bob

**2008:** a normal, nice guy — a middle manager, and the job is deliberately
unremarkable. He and his wife set off on a holiday. The crash is shown, never
narrated; nobody on screen says who died. When we next meet Bob he is alone,
and the audience joins the dots themselves.

**2026:** he lives in a tent in the Waitrose car park, ignored by every
customer walking past. He did not fall because he was lazy — the story
front-loads his backstory precisely so the audience's reflex judgement of a
homeless man is already broken before Tarquin voices it.

He is the story's moral centre. When Tarquin — the man who sneered "get a job"
at him — crawls out of that same tent five years lost, Bob has every reason to
gloat and instead reaches for the newspaper and a bit of blunt, man-sized
kindness: *"Where the f*** have you been, then? You alright?"* His refusal to
pass the judgement that was passed on him is the whole argument.

## Visual (house style: hyper-realistic documentary photography)

Anchor character — **every scene he appears in must read as the same man.** Lock
these:

- White British man, Merseyside, around fifty but weathered to look older.
  Grey-flecked stubble going on beard; deep lines; kind, tired eyes with a wet
  shine. Broken veins across wind-chapped cheeks; a couple of missing or
  discoloured teeth when he speaks. **Name the wear — do not smooth it into a
  handsome rough-sleeper.**
- Layered against the cold: a charity-shop coat (a size wrong, zip broken) over a
  hoodie over more layers; a dark woollen beanie; fingerless gloves or bare
  chapped hands. Everything grimed and softened by being lived in.
- Body: slightly hunched; sits and rises like it hurts. Hands often round a hot
  drink or a roll-up for warmth.
- Demeanour: watchful but not aggressive; the stillness of someone used to being
  scrolled past. When grace arrives in the final scenes it is in the eyes and a
  small offered gesture, never a grand expression.
- **The 2008 variant (Scene 2):** the same face ~18 years younger and undamaged
  — early-30s, clean-shaven or tidy stubble, an unremarkable work shirt or
  weekend fleece, brighter eyes. Ordinary in the way that makes him everyman.

Lit by what is actually there — overcast car-park daylight, a sodium streetlamp,
the cold blue before dawn. No flattering rim light. The realism is the argument:
he is the man anyone has stepped around outside a supermarket.
