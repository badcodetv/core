# Bob

**2008:** a normal, nice guy — a middle manager, and the job is deliberately
unremarkable. He and his wife **Jo** set off on a holiday. The crash is shown, never
narrated; nobody on screen says who died. When we next meet Bob he is alone,
and the audience joins the dots themselves.

**2026:** he lives in a tent in the Waitrose car park, ignored by every
customer walking past. He did not fall because he was lazy — the story
front-loads his backstory precisely so the audience's reflex judgement of a
homeless man is already broken before Tarquin voices it.

He is the story's moral centre. When Tarquin — the man who sneered "get a job"
at him — crawls out of that same tent five years lost, Bob has every reason to
gloat and instead reaches for the newspaper and a bit of blunt, man-sized
kindness: *"Where the f*** have you been, then? You alright?"* His refusal to
pass the judgement that was passed on him is the whole argument.

## Jo — Bob's wife *(named 2026-08-30 by Jack)*

**Jo. Short for Joanne, and nobody ever uses the long one** — which is the point of choosing it:
a shortened name does character work for free. Nobody calls their wife by her full name, so
*"Bob and Jo"* says *this was an easy marriage* without a line of narration spending itself on it.

She was **unnamed for the whole of the film's development** — "his wife" in fourteen places — and
that was never a decision, just a gap. 🔑 **The gap mattered**, because Kai's own reasoning on the
2026-08-04 call is what makes scene 3 work: *"we've set up this is Bob and his wife's name… they're
going on holiday. If the crash happens, then we just need to fade to 2026."* **Two named people get
in a car and one comes back.** That is what buys the silent crash. Without names it is an accident;
with them it is a bereavement, and nobody has to say so.

- **Born around 1978**, so about thirty in 2008. White British, Merseyside-adjacent.
- **Deliberately unremarkable, exactly as Bob's job is.** She is the far pole from "Tarquin".
- 🔒 **She has no Flow Character and needs none** — she appears in `2a` and `2b` only, both shot.
  Her look is fixed by the accepted `2b` prompt: *a plain-looking white British woman of about
  thirty with shoulder-length brown hair pushed back and no makeup, in a long-sleeved top, one foot
  up on the edge of the dashboard.*
- 🔴 **Never name her in a Flow prompt.** The `2b` plate is accepted and locked; her appearance is
  already settled and the prompt ledger is a restore point. The name lives in the script, not in
  a generation.

### ⬜ Open: how far the name reaches — *deferred by Jack, 2026-08-30 ("we'll see")*

**She is Jo in canon.** What is **not** ruled is how often the film says it. Two lines are affected
and neither has been changed:

1. **Scene 2's narration** — currently *"Meet Bob. Nice guy. Middle manager. Normal."*
2. **Bob's car-park line, scene 8** — currently *"…I wish my wife was here."*

🔑 **The second one is where the name earns its keep.** *"My wife"* is a category; *"Jo"* is a
person, and the same sentence gets considerably worse to hear. ⚠️ But it only works if she was
named earlier — an unheard name arriving at the emotional peak reads as a continuity error, not a
gut-punch. **So the two lines are one decision, not two.** Drafts of both are below; nothing is
executed.

**Scene 2 — if the name is spoken:**

> "Meet Bob. Nice guy. Middle manager. Normal. That's Jo."

*Four sentences, all flat, the last one flattest. She is introduced the way a person actually gets
introduced in a car — as an afterthought, because the narrator does not yet think she matters. She
does.*

**Scene 8 — if the name is spoken:**

> **BOB:** "F***ing wank tanks. You're all pricks. …I wish Jo was here."

⚠️ **Both or neither.** Naming her in scene 2 and not in scene 8 wastes it; naming her in scene 8
alone breaks it.

## Visual (house style: hyper-realistic documentary photography)

Anchor character — **every scene he appears in must read as the same man.** Lock
these:

- White British man, Merseyside, around fifty but weathered to look older.
  Grey-flecked stubble going on beard; deep lines; kind, tired eyes with a wet
  shine. Broken veins across wind-chapped cheeks; a couple of missing or
  discoloured teeth when he speaks. **Name the wear — do not smooth it into a
  handsome rough-sleeper.**
- Layered against the cold: a charity-shop coat (a size wrong, zip broken) over a
  hoodie over more layers; a dark woollen beanie; fingerless gloves or bare
  chapped hands. Everything grimed and softened by being lived in.
- Body: slightly hunched; sits and rises like it hurts. Hands often round a hot
  drink or a roll-up for warmth.
- Demeanour: watchful but not aggressive; the stillness of someone used to being
  scrolled past. When grace arrives in the final scenes it is in the eyes and a
  small offered gesture, never a grand expression.
- **The 2008 variant (Scene 2):** the same face ~18 years younger and undamaged
  — early-30s, clean-shaven or tidy stubble, an unremarkable work shirt or
  weekend fleece, brighter eyes. Ordinary in the way that makes him everyman.

Lit by what is actually there — overcast car-park daylight, a sodium streetlamp,
the cold blue before dawn. No flattering rim light. The realism is the argument:
he is the man anyone has stepped around outside a supermarket.
