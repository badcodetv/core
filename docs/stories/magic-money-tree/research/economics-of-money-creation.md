## 1. Keynes: "Anything we can actually do we can afford"

**Exact wording:** *"Assuredly we can afford this and much more. **Anything we can actually do we can afford.**"*

**Source:** BBC radio talk "How Much Does Finance Matter?", part of a BBC series on post-war planning — **printed in *The Listener* on 2 April 1942** (CW XXVII pp.264–70). ⚠️ **Corrected 2026-08-25:** 2 April is the *print* date; no source gives a distinct broadcast date and BBC Genome was unreachable — say "on the BBC, spring 1942" until Genome is read. Reprinted in *Collected Writings of J.M. Keynes*, Vol. XXVII. **Kai's paraphrase "we can afford what we can create" is a close gloss but NOT the quote** — Keynes's version is about *doing* (deploying real labour and materials). Use the exact line; let a character gloss it.

Context: Keynes proposing ~£50m for theatres, concert halls, galleries, communal dining in post-war reconstruction.

> "We are immeasurably richer than our predecessors. **Is it not evident that some sophistry, some fallacy, governs our collective action if we are forced to be so much meaner than they in the embellishments of life?**"

> "**We shall, in very fact, have built our New Jerusalem out of the labour which in our former vain folly we were keeping unused and unhappy in enforced idleness.**"

**The architect exchange — the single most usable passage for a comic:**

> "**The money? But surely, Sir John, you don't build houses with money? Do you mean that there won't be enough bricks and mortar and steel and cement?**"

**And the constraint, named in the same broadcast — the MMT position eighty years early:**

> "**The first task is to make sure that there is enough demand to provide employment for everyone. The second task is to prevent a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply, which is the proper meaning of inflation.**"

> "Our building programme must be properly proportioned to the resources which are left after we have met our daily needs and produced enough exports to pay for what we require to import."

**Graham Moore, *The Wealth of Shadows* (2024):** yes, Keynes is a character — protagonist Ansel Luxford (Treasury, economic warfare on Germany) meets his idol Keynes "only to find himself in a battle of competing visions"; climaxes at Bretton Woods 1944. The real Keynes–White fight: Keynes proposed the **bancor** and an International Clearing Union with **symmetric obligations on creditors** (surplus nations charged interest for hoarding); White's plan put all adjustment on debtors. **Sequencing detail most accounts get wrong: bancor was killed *before* the conference convened — Bretton Woods was a ratification ceremony, not a debate.**

## 2. How money is actually created

### Bank of England, 2014 Q1 Quarterly Bulletin (McLeay, Radia & Thomas) — verbatim from the PDF

- "This article explains how **the majority of money in the modern economy is created by commercial banks making loans.**"
- "**Banks do not act simply as intermediaries, lending out deposits that savers place with them, and nor do they 'multiply up' central bank money to create new loans and deposits.**"
- "**Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money.**" — "**bank lending creates deposits**" — "'**fountain pen money**', created at the stroke of bankers' pens."
- **97% figure:** "Of the two types of broad money, **bank deposits make up the vast majority — 97% of the amount currently in circulation**" (as of Dec 2013). ⚠️ 97% of **broad money (M4ex)**, not "all money" loosely.
- Money multiplier: "**not an accurate description of how money is created in reality.**" "**In no way does the aggregate quantity of reserves directly constrain the amount of bank lending or deposit creation.**"
- Limits (the honest half): banks "**cannot do so freely without limit**" — profitability, prudential regulation; "**Monetary policy acts as the ultimate limit on money creation.**" And: "**Just as taking out a new loan creates money, the repayment of bank loans destroys money.**"

Sources: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy (403 to bots) · PDF mirror https://monetary.org/pdfs/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy.pdf

### QE — central-bank channel

- "The central bank purchases a quantity of assets, **financed by the creation of broad money** and a corresponding increase in central bank reserves."
- Worked example (as close to "adding a number to a spreadsheet" as the Bank puts in writing): buying £1bn of gilts from a pension fund — "One way… would be to print £1 billion of banknotes and swap these directly… But transacting in such large quantities of banknotes is impractical. These sorts of transactions are therefore carried out using electronic forms of money." "**The Bank of England finances its purchase by crediting reserves to the pension fund's bank — it gives the commercial bank an IOU.**"
- **Portfolio rebalancing / "hot potato" — QE was DESIGNED to raise asset prices:** sellers "will therefore want to rebalance their portfolios… leading to the '**hot potato**' effect… **This will raise the value of those assets.**"
- Two myths the Bank kills (both common in left commentary): reserves are not "free money" for banks; and QE doesn't work by boosting bank lending — "**QE works by circumventing the banking sector.**"

### The clean two-way split for the story

- **Commercial banks** create ~97% of broad money by lending — nobody votes on it; since the 1980s overwhelmingly **mortgages against existing housing stock**, not production.
- **The central bank** creates base money (and under QE broad money), entering **at the top of the asset market, by design**.
- Rhetorical point: we already conjure money at scale every day. The question is not *whether* — it is *who gets to conjure it and what they may point it at.*

### Deficit spending mechanically

⚠️ Verify against DMO "About Gilts" before publishing. Generally: government spends from the Consolidated Fund (a credit to recipients' accounts); the **full funding rule** makes the DMO issue gilts covering the deficit; under QE the **APF** buys gilts in the **secondary market** with new reserves, indemnified by the Treasury. **April 2020: the Ways and Means facility** (the government's literal overdraft at the Bank) temporarily extended — the closest openly acknowledged "type the number in" moment; repaid.

**Honest framing:** the "spreadsheet" description is *mechanically accurate* and *institutionally incomplete*. The self-imposed rules exist to preserve the fiction that spending and creation are unrelated. **The story is not that the constraint is fake — it's that the constraint is a rule we wrote and could rewrite, dressed up as a law of nature.**

## 3. MMT vs mainstream

**Core MMT (Kelton, *The Deficit Myth*):** a sovereign-currency issuer can't be forced to default in its own currency; "replacing an artificial revenue constraint with a real inflation constraint"; taxes don't fund spending (they regulate inflation, drive currency demand, redistribute); "**the limit is inflation.**"

**Where mainstream AGREES (exploit this):** no involuntary default in own currency (Krugman and Rogoff grant it); **inflation, not solvency, is the operative constraint** (textbook); banks create money by lending (the BoE's own position). Disagreement is magnitude, mechanism, political economy: how much headroom; rates vs fiscal stabilisation; whether a legislature can tighten into inflation; exchange rates.

**Strongest critiques:** Krugman ("indefensible", "sophistry" — substantive objection: at full employment big deficits need higher rates, which raise debt service); Rogoff ("nonsense"); Summers ("recipe for disaster"); the practical one — **no parliament has ever raised taxes promptly into an inflation**; Latin America (Argentina, Venezuela, 1980s Brazil) as the cautionary file — MMT replies they lacked genuine monetary sovereignty; unresolved.

**IGM Forum, 13 March 2019** (38 economists) — regularly abused, get it right:
- Statement A: "Countries that borrow in their own currency **should not worry about deficits** because they can always create money" → **0 of 38 agreed**.
- Statement B: "…can finance **as much real government spending as they want** by creating money" → **0 of 38 agreed**. (Duffie: "each such country could finance the purchase of all of the world's output, which is obviously impossible.")
- ⚠️ **Cuts both ways:** Kelton et al. respond that **neither statement is an MMT claim** — MMT explicitly asserts an inflation constraint; the statements are strawmen. True that 0/38 endorsed the statements; NOT true that 0/38 endorsed MMT. Using it as a knockout is a misuse; ignoring it dodges that it captures the *internet* version of MMT. A character making exactly this observation is a good beat.

## 4. QE, asset prices, wealth inequality

**Scale:** BoE peak **£895bn** ✅ (began Mar 2009; £165bn by Sept 2009; five further tranches to Nov 2020). Fed: ~$700–800bn pre-2008 → ~**$8.9tn** peak (mid-2022). ECB €60–80bn/month from 2015; **€750bn PEPP** Mar 2020. BoJ ¥60–70tn/yr under Abenomics.

**Mechanism not in dispute:** raising asset prices IS the stated transmission mechanism (§2). The distributional consequence follows from who owns assets.

**Evidence:**
- BoE's own July 2012 analysis: QE gains mainly to the wealthy; **"40% of gains to the richest 5% of households."** ⚠️ Most-quoted figure in the debate, obtained only via secondary summary — verify against "The Distributional Effects of Asset Purchases" (BoE, July 2012).
- **BIS Quarterly Review March 2016** (Domanski, Scatigna & Zabai) — careful and genuinely mixed: wealthy households' net wealth grew **2–5× faster** post-2008; "**monetary policy may have added to inequality to the extent that it has boosted equity prices**"; but low rates/bond appreciation had negligible distributional effects; **equities drove the widening, housing partly offset it** (rising house prices equalise among owners, catastrophic for non-owners). Rich-vs-poor differentials: DE/IT ~2×, US ~4×, FR ~5×. https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt1603f.htm
- **Counter-evidence (include it):** ECB July 2018 study — its QE raised poorest-fifth net wealth 2.5% vs 1.0% richest (equalising, via employment); ⚠️ contested. Draghi's defence: employment effects help those with no assets. **Strongest counter: the right comparison is QE vs a deeper recession, and depressions are regressive too.**
- **Theresa May herself, 2016:** "Monetary policy — in the form of super-low interest rates and quantitative easing — **has helped those on the property ladder at the expense of those who can't afford to own their own home.**" (Useful because it's from the right.)

⚠️ GAP: specific asset-price magnitudes (S&P troughs→peaks, UK house-price/earnings ratios) not sourced — **pull from FRED and Nationwide/ONS directly.**

**Recommended framing — the claim that survives every counter-argument:** not "QE made the rich richer" (contested) but "**the emergency proved the capacity exists.** £895bn was created at speed with no debate about affordability, pointed at asset markets by design. The constraint that binds when someone proposes £895bn for housing or care is demonstrably political, not financial."

## 5. The post-war boom, 1945–1973

### UK debt at the peak

⚠️ "259%" unverifiable — **use "around 250%"**, or 247.5% (1946, Reinhart & Sbrancia), or 249% (OBR "all-time high"), or ~270% (OBR "shortly after the war"). Per OBR the ratio then **fell for 27 consecutive years, by 206 percentage points**.

**The most quotable line (OBR):** "**In nominal terms, debt increased by 137 per cent over the 30 years, but nominal GDP increased by more than 1,200 per cent.**" *The debt was never repaid. It was outgrown.* Nominal GDP grew 8.8%/yr (2.3% real + 6.5% inflation) vs 3.6% average interest on the debt. **But:** primary surpluses averaged **1.6% of GDP/yr for 30 years** — it was not free.

### The NHS, founded 5 July 1948, mid-solvency-crisis

Britain 1945–48 was externally near-insolvent: Anglo-American Loan 15 July 1946 ($3.75bn at 2%, negotiated by Keynes; +$1.19bn Canada); convertibility crisis July–Aug 1947 (~$1bn drawn in a month, suspended in weeks); reserves $2,696m (1946) → $1,668m (1949); **1949 devaluation $4.03 → $2.80 (−30.5%)**; rationing to 1954. **The NHS opened between a suspended-convertibility crisis and a 30% devaluation, carrying ~250% debt/GDP.**

Costs: estimates £108m→£134m; **actual first-year spend £373m**; 1949/50 £447m; Bevan needed +£53m in year one, +£100m March 1950. Overruns in dentistry (3×) and ophthalmics (5×) — 8.5m dental patients, 5m+ pairs of spectacles in year one. Share of GDP peaked **3.37% (1951)**. **Guillebaud Report (1956): NHS spending had FALLEN 3.75% → 3.25% of GNP 1948–54** — the runaway-cost panic was wrong.

**Counterpoint to include:** April 1951 — Gaitskell introduced charges for spectacles and dentures to fund Korean rearmament; **Bevan resigned**. Free-at-point-of-use breached within three years.

Rest of the Attlee build: **1,016,349 homes by 1951, 806,857 council** (four in five); nationalisations ~20% of the economy (BoE 1946, coal 1946, electricity 1947, rail 1947, gas 1948, iron & steel 1949); National Insurance Act 1946; National Assistance Act 1948.

### Financial repression — the mechanism, and the honest reframe

**Reinhart & Sbrancia, NBER WP 16893:** directed lending by captive audiences, interest-rate caps, capital controls, government–bank fusion. **Annual "liquidation effect": UK 1945–80 = 3.6% of GDP/yr = 26% of tax revenues/yr** (US 3.2% / 18.9%). "Annual deficit reduction of 3 to 4 percent of GDP quickly accumulates… to a 30 to 40 percent of GDP debt reduction in the course of a decade."

**Plain English: a stealth tax on bondholders worth about a quarter of all UK tax revenue, every year, for thirty-five years.** UK real rates on government debt: below zero in 47.8% of years, **below 3% in 97.2%**; below inflation in **24 of 30 years**.

**The confession, in the government's own minutes** — Wm. McChesney Martin Jr., FOMC, March 1951, on locking savers into 29-year non-marketables: "**Some people will think the 2¾ nonmarketable bond is a trick issue. We want to meet that head on. It is.**"

Ended: UK Exchange Control Act 1947 suspended **October 1979** (32 years of capital controls); US Treasury–Fed Accord 1951, gold ownership restored 1974, Regulation Q suspended 1982.

### Growth, inequality, and the honest counterpoints

Real GDP/person growth 1950–73: Japan **8.0**, Germany **5.0**, France **4.0**, **UK 2.4**, US 2.4 (vs ~1% for 1913–50 everywhere). UK unemployment: **13.4% (1921–38) → 1.6% (1950–69)**.

⚠️ **Awkward fact:** 1950–73 was simultaneously Britain's best-ever growth AND its worst relative performance (22nd of 25) — Britain had the least war damage and grew slowest. **Catch-up/reconstruction is the academic consensus explanation for the boom**; "Keynesian demand management caused it" is the weakest claim in this file.

**Inequality:** US top 1% pre-tax income share 22.29% (1928) → **10.35% (1978, minimum)** → 18.17% (2020). UK top 1%: ~20% (1918) → ~6% (1970s) — halved 1949–74 while the US share fell only a quarter. **Wealth (best single finding):** UK top 1% wealth share fell **48.7pp between 1914 and 1979 — but the war years contributed only 10.5 points**. Alvaredo/Atkinson/Morelli: "**The large decline in top shares was very much a peacetime phenomenon.**" *It wasn't bombs. It was policy.*

Top marginal rates: US **91% held 1946–1963**; UK **83% earned / 98% unearned** to 1979. ⚠️ Effective rates far below headline — use as signals of the political settlement, not revenue evidence. Union density: UK peak ~50–52% (13.2m members, 1979) → ~22% today. UK wage share ~59% (1955–73), peak ~65% (1975), ~53% (2008).

⚠️ **THE BIG COUNTERPOINT — don't dodge:** Hall & Sargent (2011): of the US debt/GDP fall 1945–74, only ~20% came from inflating bondholders; ~40% growth, ~40% primary surpluses. **Acalin & Ball (IMF WP 2024/005):** without the rate distortions and with primary balance, US debt would have fallen only 106% → 74%, not to 23% — directly rebutting Krugman's "the debt just became irrelevant as the economy grew." **If BadCode makes the Krugman-style claim, this is the paper that gets thrown at it.**

**The honest reframe, and the better story: they did not ignore the debt. They rigged the financial system so savers and bondholders paid for the hospitals, alongside brutal choices (rationing to 1954, devaluation, prescription charges). "We chose to make rentiers pay for the NHS" is both truer and a far better line than "it was free."**

## 6. Gold standard vs productivity

### Cassel's 3% — hang the argument on this

**Gustav Cassel** (1920, via Irwin NBER WP 17597): from 1850–1910 data, gold production had to grow **~3%/yr** to keep prices stable; faster → inflation, slower → deflation. **The economy needs ~3% more money a year; geology supplies whatever it supplies; there is no mechanism connecting the two.** Cassel: "The prospect of a long period of falling prices would kill all enterprise."

Stock-vs-flow: even the 1855 peak (227t/yr) was **1.23% of the ~18,461t above-ground stock** — a sevenfold production shock moves the stock ~1%/yr.

### The fake stability

Bordo: 1880–1914 inflation averaged 0.1%/yr. True — **and it's the mean of ~−1.5%/yr (1873–96) and ~+2%/yr (1896–1914): two opposite twenty-year regimes cancelling out, with the switch thrown by South African geology and a Glaswegian chemistry patent.**

### The Long Depression, 1873–1896

US CPI **−29.8%** (−1.53%/yr); UK wholesale prices **−44.6%**. Grain 1894 at ⅓ of 1867; steel rails $71→$20/ton; 18,000 US bankruptcies 1873–79 incl. 89 railroads; **Oct 1873–Mar 1879 = 65 months, longest contraction in NBER history** (Great Depression's: 43). Revisionists (Bordo et al., NBER WP 10329) call the deflation "primarily good" (productivity-driven) — **but their own sting: "it doesn't mean that people felt good about it… reflected in labor strife and political turbulence. It is doubtful if a true deflation today would be any less unpopular."**

**Synthesis: aggregate output was fine; the distribution was not. Deflation is a wealth transfer from debtors to creditors — in 1890s America the debtors were farmers, the creditors East Coast banks. That's why it produced a revolt.**

### The California gold rush as the clean natural experiment

Money supply expanded for reasons with zero connection to productive capacity — a sawmill in Coloma, a creek in Ballarat, a reef in the Transvaal.

- California ~370t in the first five years; **cumulative US production 1792–1847 was ~37t — California's 1849 output alone exceeded it.**
- **World production 1840–44 → 1855–59: 146t → 1,011t ≈ 7×.** Laughlin: 1851–1875 (25 years) produced as much as the previous **357 years**.
- Inflation: wholesale prices +30% 1850–55 (EH.net); US CPI +14.2% 1849–57; ⚠️ pro-gold rebuttal (Lawrence White, GDP deflator): +12.4% over eight years — "surprisingly small." Both true.
- ⚠️ Safest phrasing: "world annual gold output rose roughly five- to sevenfold between the 1840s and the late 1850s."

**How the deflation ended — best narrative payload:** Witwatersrand 1886 + the **MacArthur–Forrest cyanide process (British Patent 14174, Glasgow, October 1887)**, first used on the Rand May 1890 — recovery 55% → 96%. Rand output 600k oz/yr (1890) → 3m+ oz/yr (1898). US prices flipped from −1.53%/yr to +2.08%/yr after 1896. **The free-silver movement was not defeated by argument; it was defeated by a chemistry patent.** Bryan lost 1896 (McKinley outspent him ~5:1); cyanide delivered Bryan's inflation anyway by 1900.

**Bryan, "Cross of Gold", 9 July 1896** — trickle-down refuted in 1896: "There are those who believe that, if you will only legislate to make the well-to-do prosperous, their prosperity will leak through on those below. The Democratic idea, however, has been that if you legislate to make the masses prosperous, their prosperity will find its way up through every class which rests upon them." And: "**you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.**"

### Interwar rigidity and the Depression — the most robust finding

- **Bernanke & James (1991):** "Between 1932 and 1935, growth of industrial production in countries not on gold averaged about **seven percentage points a year better** than countries remaining on gold."
- **Eichengreen & Sachs (1985):** clear negative relationship between exchange-rate height and recovery. **France stayed on gold: IP +12% (1933), −7% (1934), −4% (1935), recovering only in 1936 — the year it devalued.** Britain (off gold Sept 1931): +11% (1934), +7% (1935).
- **Bernanke (2004):** "**the longer that a country remained committed to gold, the deeper its depression and the later its recovery.**"
- ⚠️ Common misreading: E&S found devaluation as practised **was** beggar-thy-neighbour; their claim is that *coordinated* devaluation would not have been.
- **Eichengreen & Temin's ideology thesis:** "once those ships began taking on water, gold was a millstone around their necks… **only a hegemonic ideology can convince leaders to persist in such counterproductive policies. The gold standard provided just such an ideology, supported by a rhetoric of morality and rectitude.**"
- **Churchill 1925** (HC Deb 28 Apr 1925): return to gold at "the fixed price of £3 17s. 10½d. per standard ounce." ⚠️ He never said "$4.86"; the "look the dollar in the face" line is unsourced. **Lovely symmetry: Keynes had named "the outworn dogma… of £3:17:10½ per ounce" in the same 1923 sentence that called gold "already a barbarous relic." Churchill legislated that exact price two years later.**
- **Keynes, "The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill" (1925):** deflation works "in no other way than by the deliberate intensification of unemployment… a policy from which any humane or judicious person must shrink." The imaginary honest Treasury memo — practically a BadCode script already: "'We ought to warn you… it will not be safe politically to admit that you are intensifying unemployment deliberately in order to reduce wages. Thus you will have to ascribe what is happening to every conceivable cause except the true one. We estimate that about two years may elapse before it will be safe for you to utter in public one single word of truth.'" And: "The gold standard, with its dependence on pure chance, its faith in 'automatic adjustments,' and its general regardlessness of social detail, is an essential emblem and idol of those who sit in the top tier of the machine."

### Nixon, 15 August 1971

**Triffin dilemma** (JEC testimony Oct 1959; *Gold and the Dollar Crisis* 1960): run deficits and dollar claims outgrow the gold stock; stop and the world starves of liquidity. Foreign official claims exceeded US monetary gold in **1964**; by the late 1960s ~$50bn of foreign dollar holdings vs ~$10bn of gold. US gold 701.8m oz (1949) → 316.3m oz (1970). **Closed loop worth using: Triffin's proposed fix was Keynes's bancor — the thing the US killed in 1944.**

⚠️ **THE UK "$3 BILLION" STORY IS A MYTH — most important correction here.** Irwin, from the archives: it was Friday 13 August; Britain requested *cover* (a guarantee) for its dollar holdings; the request was **garbled in transmission** into "Britain wants $3bn in gold"; and "the British request did not trigger the closing of the gold window; the meeting to formalize that decision had already been set up the day before." **Volcker: "One story circulated later that the British request precipitated our decision to go off gold. That was not true."** (Narrative payload: the system ends, in folk memory, on a misheard phone call.)

**The speech, 15 Aug 1971, 9pm** (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-nation-outlining-new-economic-policy-the-challenge-peace): "The gainers are the international money speculators. Because they thrive on crises, they help to create them." / "…suspend **temporarily** the convertibility of the dollar into gold…" / "Let me lay to rest the bugaboo of what is called devaluation… **your dollar will be worth just as much tomorrow as it is today.**" Plus a 10% import surcharge and a 90-day wage-price freeze. **Nixon sold the end of gold as a jobs-and-fairness measure; devaluation appears only to be denied.** Gold $35/oz → **$850/oz by 21 January 1980** (~24× in nine years). Smithsonian Agreement (Dec 1971, "the most significant monetary agreement in the history of the world") collapsed in fourteen months; generalised float March 1973.

## 7. What actually drove inflation, 2021–23

**The story's claim "printing beyond productive capacity → inflation" is directionally defensible but NOT what the evidence primarily says. If a BadCode narrator asserts the flat monetarist story, an informed reader will bounce off it.**

- **SF Fed (Shapiro, June 2022):** of the April 2022 headline PCE gap, **supply ~52%, demand ~29%**, ambiguous ~17%. Core PCE: ~45/45. Timing: demand lit it (from March 2021), supply sustained it. https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2022/06/how-much-do-supply-and-demand-drive-inflation/
- **Profits ("greedflation") — better evidence than the sneer suggests. ECB Economic Bulletin 4/2023:** **unit profits ≈ 60% of euro-area GDP deflator growth** over the three quarters analysed, against a 42% share of nominal GDP — "price rises have exceeded the increases seen in costs." https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2023/html/ecb.ebbox202304_03~705befadac.en.html
- **The 1970s comparison — devastating for "workers caused it" (ECB EB 5/2022):** 1970s terms-of-trade loss ~4.8% of GDP, wage share ROSE, real wages ROSE; 2021–22 loss ~1.6%, wage share FELL, real wages FELL. Workers did not recoup.
- **Bernanke & Blanchard (2023):** "most of the inflation surge that began in 2021 was the result of shocks to prices given wages, including sharp increases in commodity prices and sectoral shortages" — not overheated labour markets (which matter more for persistence). Shapiro: labour costs explain ~0.1pp of the 3pp US core rise.
- **Where "money printing" fits honestly:** US M2 +24–25% y/y 2020–21 is real support at low frequencies — but the euro area got similar inflation with far less monetary expansion, and Japan got neither. **The defensible formulation is Keynes's own, not Friedman's: inflation is "a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply" — the same sentence from the same 1942 broadcast. A very nice structural rhyme.**
- ⚠️ US profit-decomposition sources (EPI/Bivens vs Kansas City Fed) disagree — the disagreement is the honest picture; fetch both directly if a US number is needed.

## 8. The 2% wage-indexation idea

**Framing fact: no country has ever indexed wages to a monetary aggregate. Every real scheme indexes to prices.**

### Italy's scala mobile (1945–1993) — the cautionary tale

Generalised 31 Jan 1975 (Agnelli–Lama). The *punto unico di contingenza* — a flat cash amount per point, identical for every worker — egalitarian by design, compressed differentials hard. Inflation 17–21%/yr through 1975–83. Dismantled: "decreto di San Valentino" 14 Feb 1984 (split the unions); **June 1985 referendum to restore it LOST 54.3/45.7**; abolished 1992, replaced by the 1993 Ciampi protocol (forward-looking target inflation, energy shocks excluded). Canonical indictment: **Modigliani & Padoa-Schioppa (1978)** — with ≥100% indexation, an adverse real shock can't cut real wages, so adjustment lands on the exchange rate, the deficit and employment. ⚠️ Counterweight (**Manacorda 2004, JoLE**): the flat point powerfully compressed earnings inequality — it fell while the scala operated, rose after. Whether it *caused* inflation or entrenched *persistence* was never settled — claim the weaker one. Dark detail: **Ezio Tarantelli**, architect of the 1984 compromise, was assassinated by the Red Brigades, 27 March 1985.

### Belgium — the living system, and the omission

Indexes wages/pensions/benefits to the **health index** (CPI minus tobacco/alcohol/petrol/diesel), 4-month smoothed; public-sector **pivot index** fires at each 2%. ~98% of private employees covered. 2022–23: JC200 **+11.08% on 1 Jan 2023** (~500k employees); Belgian real wages recovered to pre-crisis by Q1 2023 while euro-area real wages hadn't; wage growth **front-loads then falls** (9.1% Q1-23 → 2.5% Q1-24 while euro area still rising). FGTB claim (attribute): Belgian purchasing power −0.1% in 2022 vs NL −6.8%, DE −4.2%.

**⚠️ THE LOAD-BEARING OMISSION: the Law of 26 July 1996** caps wage growth **on top of** indexation, benchmarked on DE/FR/NL — since 2009 never more than 1.1% real over two years; **0% for 2023–24 and 2025–26. Belgium does not have indexation *plus* bargaining. It has indexation *instead of* bargaining.** Escape hatches: the 2015 "index jump" (permanent ~2% real cut); Luxembourg postponed a 2022 tranche; Cyprus froze its COLA 2012–13. **Every indexing country suspends indexation under stress.** IMF recommends excluding volatile energy/food — precisely what made people poorer. ECB Governing Council has formally "called for such schemes to be avoided." Only ~3% of euro-area private employees have automatic indexation.

### US analogues

Social Security COLA: automatic since **1975** (CPI-W Q3/Q3); **can never be negative** (zeros: 2010, 2011, 2016); peak 14.3% (1980), 8.7% (2023). Union escalators: born in the **May 1948 GM–UAW contract** (Wilson/Reuther), template via the 1950 "Treaty of Detroit"; coverage peaked at 61% of major-contract workers (1976) — maybe 10% of all US workers (Card); **collapsed 61% → 21% between 1980 and 1981 settlements**. Card (1986): COLA contracts carry *smaller* deferred raises (workers pay for the insurance) and flat formulas erode skill differentials.

### The trap: indexation is how hyperinflations become permanent

- **Israel:** inflation 445% (1984), ~500% annualised mid-1985; the July 1985 stabilization **required a three-month COLA suspension**; inflation fell to <20% within ~two months. **Leiderman & Livnatan: "it is hard to generate a strong anti-inflation lobby in a highly indexed economy"; "although indexation is not an independent source of inflation, it can enhance the persistence of nominal shocks"** ← the precise, defensible formulation.
- **Brazil:** indexation written into law (1964); IPCA annualised 4,922% (June 1994). **The URV trick — elegant, steal it:** March–July 1994, a pure unit of account with no notes, priced daily in cruzeiros; prices/wages restated in URVs while payment stayed in cruzeiros, so the old currency absorbed all the inflation; wages converted at 4-month average real value; 1 July 1994, 1 URV = 1 real. Inflation 916% → 22% with unemployment untouched. **De-indexed by *synchronising* indexation.**
- **Argentina:** the 1991 Convertibility Law *prohibited* indexation clauses; cost: ~33% real appreciation, unemployment 6.5% → 17%.

### The theory

**Gray (1976, JME) & Fischer (1977, Carnegie-Rochester):** indexing wages **stabilises output under nominal shocks, destabilises it under real shocks** — hence optimal *partial* indexation. **Fairest reading of Kai's proposal:** if inflation is genuinely money-driven, that IS a nominal shock and indexation is textbook-correct. **The trap: you get one rule and it fires whatever the shock.** When the oil stops coming, the country is genuinely poorer, the equilibrium real wage has fallen, indexation blocks the adjustment, and the shock lands on employment. That is the 1970s. Ball (1994): universal statutory indexation maximises the output cost of any future disinflation.

### Did a 2021–23 spiral happen? No

IMF (Oct 2022): of 22 historical wage-price-spiral candidates, only a small minority spiralled. **Bernanke & Blanchard's explanation of the 1970s contrast directly implicates indexation:** "because of automatic cost-of-living adjustments and other mechanisms, catch-up effects were presumably much stronger" — hence 1970s persistence. Scene material: **Andrew Bailey, 4 Feb 2022**, asked if he was telling people not to ask for a pay rise: "**Broadly, yes.**" (Salary: £575,000.) ⚠️ Heterodox economists object that the wage-spiral obsession lets profit-driven inflation off the hook — given unit profits were ~60% of the euro-area deflator growth, that objection has teeth.

### ⚠️ What's wrong with "money supply +2% → legal +2% pay rise", in order of severity

1. **Wrong variable, and 2020–23 is a killer counter-example both ways:** M2 +25% in 2020–21 with 1–2% inflation → the rule mandates a 25% pay rise into a supply-constrained economy; 2022–23 money growth ~zero with 8–10% inflation → the rule mandates a real pay freeze mid-cost-of-living-crisis. Exactly backwards, twice.
2. **The government doesn't control the money supply** — 97% is commercial-bank deposits; "the Bank of England does not directly control the quantity of either base or broad money." The trigger describes a lever that mostly doesn't exist. **To make the rule operable you'd first have to nationalise money creation — the most interesting beat: the pay rule secretly presupposes a far more radical monetary reform than it advertises.**
3. **Which aggregate?** M0/M1/M2/M3/Divisia diverge and give opposite signals. Whoever picks the aggregate picks everyone's pay rise. Goodhart's Law with teeth.
4. **Velocity** isn't stable; MV=PY fails exactly at annual frequency.
5. **Gray–Fischer trap** (above).
6. **Who pays in a firm with no revenue growth?** Belgium's honest answer is the 1996 wage-norm law — the proposal omits the second half of the machine.
7. **Competitiveness:** index alone and unit labour costs diverge (Belgium +7.4% vs 5.3% DE/FR/NL, 2023); remedies are all ugly.
8. **One-way ratchet:** negative money growth → legal pay cut? Every real system says no (US COLA floors at zero), so "symmetric" fails in practice.
9. **Coverage:** the self-employed, gig workers, pensioners, claimants sit outside.
10. **A percentage rise is regressive in absolute terms** — Italy and Malta both chose flat cash precisely to compress differentials. If the appeal is egalitarian, the percentage form undercuts it.

## Safe to assert vs hedge

**Rock solid:** Keynes's line + inflation definition (2 Apr 1942, BBC); banks create most money by lending / 97% of broad money / multiplier is wrong (BoE's own position); QE designed to raise asset prices (BoE's words); £895bn UK QE peak, ~$8.9tn Fed peak; NHS + 806,857 council houses + ~20% nationalised on ~250% debt/GDP while near-insolvent; debt fell 27 straight years, 206pp, debt +137% vs GDP +1,200%; UK real rates below inflation 24 of 30 years, repression ≈ 26% of tax revenue/yr; top-1% shares hit century floor, wealth compression "very much a peacetime phenomenon"; gold-exit timing vs depression depth (~7pp/yr); Nixon 15 Aug 1971 "your dollar will be worth just as much tomorrow"; gold $35 → $850 by Jan 1980; Belgium indexes today only alongside a 0%-real cap.

**Hedge / avoid:** never "259%" (say ~250%); "growth painlessly erased the debt" is contested (surpluses averaged 1.6% GDP/yr; Acalin & Ball) — better line: **"we chose to make rentiers pay for the hospitals"**; "Keynesian demand management caused the boom" is the weakest claim; the BoE "40% to richest 5%" figure needs primary verification; QE-and-inequality is genuinely mixed — the claim that survives is the political one (£895bn proved the capacity exists); "money printing caused 2021–23 inflation" is not the primary story — use Keynes's formulation; the IGM MMT survey statements are strawmen (use carefully); the UK-$3bn Nixon trigger is a myth; E&S found devaluation WAS beggar-thy-neighbour as practised.
