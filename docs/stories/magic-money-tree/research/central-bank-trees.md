# Research: Central-bank trees — the real facts under the lore

> Deep-research brief, 2026-08-07 session (the grove lore). Commissioned to test whether
> "there is a magic money tree inside every central bank" could be built on real history
> rather than invented whole. **It can.** The Bank of England's own museum blog is titled
> *"Does money grow on trees?"* and the answer it gives is, essentially, yes.
> Facts below are triangulated from secondary sources unless marked. ⚠️ = verify before print.

## 0. The headline

**The Bank of England has four magic money trees growing in a graveyard in its
courtyard, and it planted them in the early 1940s because the oldest banknote it
owns is made of tree.** We did not invent the premise of this story. We found it.

Every load-bearing element of the lore in [`../the-grove.md`](../the-grove.md) has a
citation in this brief. That is the point: when the audience Googles it, it holds.

## 1. The Bank of England's mulberries (Garden Court, Threadneedle Street)

- **Four black mulberries** (*Morus nigra*) grow in Garden Court at the Bank of
  England's Threadneedle Street head office. Source: Bank of England Museum blog,
  *"Does money grow on trees?"*; corroborated by Morus Londinium (the London
  mulberry survey), which read the Bank's own records.
- **Planted in the early 1940s** ⚠️ (exact year not published in either source) —
  when Garden Court was relocated as part of **Sir Herbert Baker's** demolition and
  rebuilding of Soane's 1780s Bank. **This is the same handful of years the story
  is set in.** The Bank planted new money trees during the Blitz.
- **Why mulberry, reason one:** the earliest banknote in the Bank's collection is
  **printed on paper made from the bark of the mulberry tree** — issued in China in
  the reign of the **Hongwu / Hung Wu emperor (1368–1398)**, Ming dynasty. The Bank
  chose the species as a nod to its own oldest note.
- **Why mulberry, reason two — and this is the good one:** mulberry roots are
  **shallow and grow horizontally**, which makes them plantable in a courtyard with
  **only about a foot of soil**. There is only a foot of soil because of §2.

## 2. Garden Court is a graveyard

- Garden Court is the former **churchyard of St Christopher le Stocks**, a 14th-century
  church partly destroyed in the **Great Fire (1666)**, rebuilt by **Wren**, then
  deconsecrated and demolished — the Bank extended into the site in **1781**, after
  protesters climbed the steeple during the **Gordon Riots (1780)** and the Directors
  decided they did not care for a tall building overlooking their vaults.
- The Bank **promised to leave the graves undisturbed** and turned the churchyard
  into its garden.
- **Long Bill Jenkins.** **William Daniel Jenkins (1767–1798)**, a Bank clerk **6 ft
  7½ in** tall, feared body snatchers would sell his skeleton (going rate for a
  frame that size in 1798: **~200 guineas**). His friends petitioned the Directors,
  who let him be buried **in Garden Court, at dawn on 1 April 1798**, before the
  working day began. In **1933** his **7 ft 6 in lead casket** was found **8½ ft
  down** at the end of the garden during Baker's rebuild and moved, with other
  remains, to the eastern catacomb at **Nunhead Cemetery** — under an Act of
  Parliament passed in **1923** specifically to govern the bodies the Bank was about
  to dig up.
- **Usable, unembellished:** the Bank of England moved its dead to make room, then
  planted four money trees over the ones it left, with roots that spread flat
  because they mustn't go deep.

## 3. The tree they cut down

- The **original** Garden Court had a single mature **European lime** as its
  centrepiece — documented in a **1906 watercolour** and photographed **c. 1920**.
- It was **cut down / the garden dug up and relocated** during the **1930s** phase of
  Baker's rebuild. The four mulberries are its **replacements**, not its companions.
- **Story value:** the Bank of England removed the one old tree that had stood there
  since the 1780s and installed four young ones. In canon, the park tree is the
  lime's child and does not know the parent is gone. See [`../the-grove.md`](../the-grove.md).

## 4. What banknotes are actually made of (the joke's factual floor)

- **Not wood pulp.** Bank of England paper notes were **cotton fibre** (cotton rag,
  with linen historically) — which is *why* they survive a washing machine.
- **Portals**, Laverstoke, Hampshire, has supplied Bank of England banknote paper
  since **1725**; for nearly a century a Bank officer ("Ye Bank Officer") was
  stationed at the mill to watch the paper being made.
- **Polymer since 2016:** the first polymer £5 issued **September 2016**, £10 in the
  second half of **2017**. The polymer is **polypropylene** — a plastic film.
- **How to use this honestly:** the "paper comes from a tree" gag is *false* for a
  modern British banknote and *true* for the oldest note the Bank owns (§1). Don't
  assert that today's notes are wood. Let the tree say it — the deadpan version
  ("they print me on plastic now") is both funnier and correct.

## 5. Threadneedle Street, 1694 — the first tree was planted to pay for a war

- The **Bank of England was created by Royal Charter on 27 July 1694**, primarily to
  **fund the war against Louis XIV's France** (the Nine Years' War).
- Trigger: after the **Battle of Beachy Head (1690)**, England needed to rebuild its
  navy and **William III had neither the money nor the credit**.
- The deal: subscribers lent the Crown **£1,200,000 at 8%**; in exchange they got a
  limited-liability corporation that would bank for the government and hold **the
  right to issue banknotes**. The £1.2m was **subscribed in 12 days**.
- **Story value, and it is the spine of the lore:** the institution that told a nurse
  in 2017 that there is no magic money tree **was itself founded as one, to pay for a
  war, in twelve days.** Keynes in 1942 is not asking the Bank to do something new. He
  is asking it to do the exact thing it was chartered for.

## 6. London, August 1914 — the Bradbury (the seed)

- **Currency and Bank Notes Act 1914** passed **6 August 1914**, the day after
  Britain declared war ⚠️ (sources give 5 and 6 August for passage; the Act is dated
  6 August). It let **the Treasury** — not the Bank — issue **£1 and 10-shilling**
  currency notes, to stop a run on gold.
- The **£1 note was issued Friday 7 August 1914**; the 10/- a week later. Known as
  the **first Bradbury issue**, after **Sir John Bradbury**, Permanent Secretary to
  the Treasury, whose signature they carried.
- **Before this there were no £1 notes in England** — the smallest Bank of England
  note was £5. The Bradbury is the first modern English pound note.
- **The detail worth the whole scene:** the first ones were rushed out on the wrong
  stock — **there was no banknote paper available, so they were printed on paper made
  for postage stamps.** A proper design (by George Eve, on real banknote paper)
  followed on **23 October 1914**.
- They were **Treasury** notes: government money, issued directly, in three days,
  because there was a war. Not bank money.
- **Story value:** the seed the clerk plants in the park is a first-issue Bradbury —
  government money, printed on the wrong paper, in a panic, in the first week of a
  war. Its child grows up to teach an economist how to pay for the next one.

## 7. Berlin, 1923 — the tree that fruited hollow (burn one)

- The Reichsbank's printing operation at the peak: **~30 paper mills, ~150 printing
  firms, ~2,000 printing presses** running day and night.
- **August 1923:** Reichsbank president **Rudolf Havenstein** reported issuing
  **20,000 milliard marks daily**, planning to reach **46,000 milliard daily** within
  the week.
- **25 October 1923:** the Reichsbank announced it had printed **120,000 million
  million marks** that day. Estimated demand for cash that day: **a quintillion marks.**
  It could not print fast enough to keep up with its own printing.
- **2 November 1923:** the first **100-trillion-mark** note issued.
- Peak exchange: **$1 = 4.2 trillion marks** (already in canon).
- **Story value:** this is not a tree failing. It is a tree being asked for something
  the country could not make, fruiting exactly as instructed, and producing notes that
  are physically identical and buy nothing. Hollow fruit. Keynes's real 1942 definition
  of inflation — *"a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply"* — is
  the mechanic, stated by the man himself.

## 8. Berlin, November 1923 — they rooted a new tree in the ground

- The **Rentenmark** was issued **15 November 1923**. With gold reserves gone,
  **agricultural and industrial land was mortgaged to the tune of 3.2 billion
  Rentenmark** to back it.
- Fixed at **1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion Papiermark**, pegged at **4.20 RM = US$1**.
  Combined with a hard stop on printing and severe austerity (Reichsbank president
  **Hjalmar Schacht**, finance minister **Hans Luther**), inflation **collapsed within
  weeks** — the *"miracle of the Rentenmark."*
- Replaced by the **Reichsmark, 30 August 1924**, under the **Dawes Plan**.
- **Story value:** they killed the old tree and rooted the new one **in the actual
  soil of Germany** — in farms and factories, in things you can stand on. It worked
  in weeks. Backing money with *productive capacity* rather than with metal is the
  story's entire thesis, and Germany proved it in 1923 by accident.

## 9. Berlin, 1934 — the fake tree in the dark (the one that bombs London)

- **Mefo bills**, devised by **Hjalmar Schacht** in **1934**: six-month promissory
  notes drawn on **Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft ("Mefo")** — a **letterbox
  company** with no operations, set up by five German blue-chip firms and staffed by
  Reichsbank and military personnel.
- Mechanism: bills passed from armaments firms → commercial banks (discount) →
  Reichsbank (rediscount), **creating base money while bypassing the Reichsbank's own
  legal limits on credit expansion, the national budget, and parliamentary scrutiny.**
- **Purpose: to finance rearmament in secret, against the terms of the Treaty of
  Versailles.** Secrecy was an explicit design goal — figures that other financing
  methods would have made public were concealed.
- **Scale: ~12 billion marks outstanding** when the programme was discontinued in
  **March 1938**.
- **Story value — the connection the whole history lesson exists to make:** the bombs
  falling on London while Keynes sits on the bench were bought with **money conjured
  out of nothing by a man running a fake company**, off the books, in the dark, by the
  same central banker who had saved the currency eleven years earlier. The enemy
  already knows how to use the tree. They used it first and they used it hidden. That
  is why Keynes has to do it **in the open, in a published pamphlet, on the radio** —
  and it is the argument that turns the history lesson into the plot.

## 10. The British answer, for symmetry

- **Keynes, *How to Pay for the War* (pamphlet, 1940)** — developed October 1939 to
  February 1940, argued in *The Times*, on the radio, in public.
- The proposal: **compulsory saving, euphemised as "deferred pay"** — a forced loan
  from the working population, repaid after the war, designed to strip excess demand
  out of the economy so that resources went to the war rather than to price rises.
- Partially legislated in **Kingsley Wood's** budget as **post-war credits**, repaid
  progressively afterwards (at reduced real value).
- **Story value:** the contrast with §9 is exact and needs no commentary. Schacht hid
  twelve billion marks in a shell company. Keynes published a pamphlet and went on the
  radio to ask people to lend him their wages. Same problem, same instrument, opposite
  ethics — and the tree can say so in one line.

## 11. 1971 — what actually changed (correcting our own canon)

The pre-2026-08-07 canon said that after Nixon *"they planted them everywhere."*
Given §5 (1694), that is now incoherent — the grove long predates 1971. The accurate
and stronger reading:

- The trees were **always** there. What ended on **15 August 1971** was the **last
  tether to gold** — the constraint that had been placed on them from outside.
- So 1971 is not a planting. It is **the chain coming off** — every tree on earth
  suddenly able to fruit as much as it is asked for, in the hands of people who had
  never once asked it what its actual limit was.
- This is also just better economics: the gold link was an external, arbitrary
  constraint; removing it exposed the *real* constraint (productive capacity), which
  nobody had learned to read because gold had been doing the thinking for them.

## 12. Tally sticks — England's money was wood for six centuries (added 2026-08-07)

- From the 12th century, the **Exchequer recorded Crown debts on tally sticks** —
  notched sticks of **willow or hazel**, split lengthwise on issue: the **stock**
  retained by the creditor (origin of the word *stock*), the **foil** by the
  debtor; payment was confirmed by marrying the halves. A split stick was a
  negotiable instrument of the English Crown.
- The system was obsolete from **1826**; in 1834 the Exchequer held two
  cart-loads of old tallies and ordered them **burned in the furnaces beneath
  the House of Lords**. The stoves overheated and on **16 October 1834** the
  fire destroyed the **Palace of Westminster**. Turner painted it from the south
  bank; Dickens later roasted the bureaucracy of it in an 1855 speech.
- The **counterfoil** on a chequebook preserves the tally's foil in modern
  English.
- **Story value:** money didn't metaphorically grow on trees in England — for six
  hundred years it *was* tree; and the one attempt to erase the wooden money
  burned down Parliament. Also: stock/foil = the same stick at two addresses —
  the medieval ancestor of the payslip/yacht image.

## 13. Berlin's limes — Unter den Linden felled 1934–35 (added 2026-08-07)

- The lindens of **Unter den Linden** — many **~250 years old** — were **felled
  in 1934–35** during the Nord-Süd S-Bahn tunnel construction, part of widening
  the boulevard into the east–west axis of the planned world capital
  **Germania**. Remaining old trees were destroyed or cut for firewood in the
  war's last days; today's lindens are postwar plantings (1950s onward).
- **Story value:** both capitals cut down their limes in the same decade, for
  the same reason — the institution wanted a grander room. ⚠️ Don't tie the
  30 Jan 1933 torchlight parade specifically to the avenue (that route ran via
  Wilhelmstraße); "the avenue the regime paraded down" is safe.

- **Bank of England notes keep face value forever** (the promise to pay never
  expires); Treasury notes were folded into BoE issuance in **1928**. A 1914 £1

- **Lime folklore**: the *Dorflinde* / **Gerichtslinde** — the village linden as

  by the lovers' blood; the mourning tree. James I's failed state silk project

## Never say

- ❌ **"Banknotes are made of wood / paper from trees."** False for modern notes
  (cotton, then polymer). Only the 14th-century Chinese mulberry-bark note licenses
  the literal claim — attribute it to that note, or let the tree make the joke against
  itself.
- ❌ **"The Weimar wheelbarrows were just before WWII."** The hyperinflation is
  **1923** — sixteen years before the war and **ten years before Hitler took power**.
  Conflating them destroys the story's actual argument, which is that the **1930–33
  austerity**, not the 1923 inflation, produced the Nazi vote (see
  [`fascism-and-the-wealth-gap.md`](./fascism-and-the-wealth-gap.md)). Keep the two
  burns separate and in order; Mefo (1934–38) is the *third*, separate episode.
- ❌ **"Central banks planted the trees after 1971."** Superseded — see §11.
- ❌ **"Schacht was a Nazi true believer"** — he was Hitler's economics minister and
  built the rearmament financing, and he was also later dismissed, imprisoned after
  the July 1944 plot, tried at Nuremberg and **acquitted**. Don't relitigate him; use
  the *mechanism* (Mefo), not the man's soul.
- ❌ Any claim that the Bank of England "admits" to magic money trees. The blog title
  is a museum pun about mulberry bark. The joke is that the facts line up — don't
  overclaim the institution's intent.
- ⚠️ **Exact planting year of the mulberries** is unpublished in the sources read.
  Write "the early 1940s" or "during the war," never a specific year. (2026-08-07:
  Kai ruled the exact year isn't needed — don't chase the archive.)
- ❌ **Never let the Bradbury become a dogwhistle.** "Bring back the Bradbury pound"
  is a live trope in monetary-crank and conspiracy circles. The clerk's Bradbury is
  a joke that grew, not a policy proposal; the story must never read as
  Bradbury-revivalism.
- ❌ **Never generate footage of a real person.** May, Nixon, the nurse, Harry
  Leslie Smith: genuine archive only, under fair dealing/licence. The generated
  registers are for the fiction (Keynes, the park, Garden Court, the ascent).
