# What Britain Can Afford When It Decides It Must

> **Research completed 11 September 2026.** Its evidence, audit and corrections inform
> [The Future He Never Saw](../storyboard.md), subsequently chosen as the single documentary
> storyboard. The proposed full-film and vertical structures below are historical recommendations,
> now superseded. Statements about canon being unchanged describe this report's research-only pass,
> not the later storyboard consolidation.

## Executive finding

The documentary's central question survives investigation, but in a more exact form:

> Why was a modest rise in a nurse's pay framed as unaffordable while governments and the Bank of
> England could redesign rules, assume risk and mobilise hundreds of billions when banks, markets,
> war production or employment were judged to be in danger?

The record supports a film about **priority, institutional speed, recipients and real capacity**. It
does not support a film which treats a guarantee, an equity purchase, quantitative easing (QE), a
wartime tax, furlough and a permanent pay award as the same act of “finding money”. In an emergency,
Britain repeatedly changed rules and mobilised finance quickly. Each intervention still transferred
risk, used or redirected real resources, created distributional consequences and left a bill or
balance-sheet position of a different kind.

The 2010 fiscal problem was real: borrowing was exceptionally high. The size of the structural
deficit was estimated, not directly observable, and the Coalition chose the pace and spending-heavy
composition of consolidation. The deficit fell from 10.1% of GDP in 2009–10 to 2.0% in 2018–19, but
debt did not get “paid down”: public-sector net debt excluding public-sector banks rose from 62.4%
to 78.4% of GDP across those endpoints.[^1] The NHS budget was not simply cut. It grew unusually
slowly against demand while pay restraint, reduced training commissions, workforce shortages and
capital pressure accumulated.[^2]

The nurse is therefore a better measure than an abstract “money tree”. Money could immediately buy
retention, more hours, lower agency dependence and training places; it could not instantly create a
qualified nurse. The film's best economic line remains true: **“You can print the money. You can't
print the nurse. She was already there.”** It should now be followed by the harder point: keeping her
was a resource decision too.

“Tax where the money comes to rest” can survive as a distributional rule, not as a complete theory
of public finance or inflation. It means taxing land and economic rents, excess profits, realised
capital gains, high incomes and wealth where publicly supported gains accumulate. It does not mean
that particular pounds can be traced and reclaimed, that every gain stays in Britain, or that a tax
imposed later can undo a supply shock now.

**Editorial ruling:** make the audience see that necessity changes the permissible instrument and
the speed of decision. Never suggest that nominal financing removes the limits imposed by people,
materials, imports, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, institutional credibility or time.

## Audit of the existing research

The 25 August documentary sweep did valuable work on the war footage, Keynes quotation bank,
Parliamentary fair dealing, debt figures and the initial £65bn/£19bn LDI distinction. This pass does
not repeat those catalogues. It adds the missing post-2008 chain, reconciles announcement amounts
with use and outturn, tests the tax and capacity hypotheses, and replaces browser snippets with
primary sources where possible.

The earlier research remains dependable on these points:

- the state is not operationally a household;
- money creation cannot substitute for real resources;
- the 1694, 1914 and 1940–45 episodes need their own mechanisms rather than a generic “printing”
  label;
- £65bn was not the amount spent in the 2022 LDI intervention;
- the May clip and Parliament footage require rights analysis, not an assumption that online means
  available;
- the Bank's mulberries are documented, but no production-ready photograph has yet been found.

The main new corrections are material, not cosmetic:

1. **£500bn after 2008 was not Treasury spending.** It combined up to £50bn recapitalisation
   capacity, a guarantee scheme initially described at up to £250bn, and at least £200bn of secured
   liquidity. Initial equity subscriptions were £37bn; the liquidity and guarantee peaks were lower
   than their envelopes.[^3]
2. **“£450bn in three months” is false.** The Bank authorised additions of £200bn on 19 March,
   £100bn on 18 June and £150bn on 5 November 2020. The £895bn target was reached late in 2021.[^4]
3. **The QE inequality claim is too absolute.** The largest cash gains accrued to households with
   large asset holdings, but the Bank's model of all monetary easing from 2008–14 estimated that
   relative wealth inequality changed little and that employment, income and debt-service channels
   benefited many non-owners. The £350,000/£3,000 comparison is not QE alone.[^5]
4. **NHS pay was not frozen identically for seven years.** Headline pay was frozen for two years
   from 2011–12, with a low-pay policy, and then constrained around 1%; increments and individual
   outcomes varied.[^6]
5. **The DUP profile in the earlier May brief is wrong.** The primary annex commits £850m across
   the first two years and £150m across five, not £910m plus £90m.[^7]
6. **The May exchange is now timecoded at subtitle level, not visually authenticated speaker by
   speaker.** A broadcast-derived transcript places the sequence at 00:26:20–00:28:12. It confirms
   the wording but does not settle whether Victoria Davey or an adjacent nurse delivered the later
   payslip line.[^8]
7. **Parliament video is not merely an unresolved rights question.** Current download terms bar
   uses including satire, entertainment, advertising, promotion and commercial exploitation. For
   this film, seek express permission or use licensable BBC material; Open Parliament Licence (OPL)
   text is the reliable fallback.[^9]
8. **“Real wages did not regain 2008 levels until 2026” is unsafe.** On the cited ONS series,
   CPIH-adjusted regular weekly pay was already only £1 below its April 2008 peak in October 2019.
   Any later date needs a different, explicitly named series.[^10]

## The distinctions the film must teach without lecturing

| Mechanism | What moves | Immediate recipient | What the headline amount means | Principal constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Departmental spending or grant | Exchequer payment | Worker, employer, supplier, council or department | Actual spending, unless it is only a budget allocation | Labour, supplies, demand, tax and borrowing framework |
| Equity or loan | Cash exchanged for a financial asset | Bank or company | Gross outlay; final cost depends on recovery, sale, dividends and financing | Credit risk, valuation, governance |
| Guarantee | State promises to meet specified losses | Lender if a borrower defaults | Maximum exposure, not cash spent | Defaults, recoveries, moral hazard |
| DMO gilt issue | Investors exchange cash for government debt | Exchequer receives cash | Borrowing used to finance the deficit | Interest cost, market demand, maturity, fiscal credibility |
| Commercial-bank lending | Bank creates a deposit and matching borrower debt | Borrower | Private credit, even when state-guaranteed | Capital, creditworthiness, repayment and demand |
| QE | Bank creates reserves and buys existing bonds, mainly in secondary markets | Market counterparty; seller receives a deposit through its bank | Gross stock of assets purchased, not public spending | Inflation mandate, market function, indemnity and exit |
| Central-bank liquidity | Reserves or liquid securities against collateral | Bank or market participant | Secured and usually temporary | Collateral, counterparty risk, market stability |
| Ways and Means | Bank overdraft to the Exchequer | Treasury | Direct short-term central-bank credit | Institutional rules and monetary-fiscal credibility |
| Wartime mobilisation | Taxes, borrowing, money, controls and allocation combined | State, contractors, workers and households | Never one instrument or one headline number | Labour, materials, imports, inflation and political consent |

The useful visual grammar is therefore not only `YEAR · WHAT FOR · HOW MUCH · HOW LONG`. Every card
also needs `INSTRUMENT · USED · FIRST RECIPIENT`. Without those three fields, unlike numbers become
propaganda by juxtaposition.

### What each receipt proves

The receipts prove that decision-makers can change acceptable risk, suspend ordinary pacing and
mobilise institutions quickly. They also show why the verb matters:

- in 2008 the state **guaranteed, swapped, insured and bought**;
- after 2009 the Bank **purchased assets** to change monetary conditions;
- from 2010 government **withheld, reduced and capped** within a fiscal programme;
- in 2017 government **committed** multi-year spending to secure parliamentary stability;
- in 2020 Treasury **spent, granted, guaranteed and borrowed**, while the Bank separately **lent
  and purchased**;
- in 2022 the Bank **backstopped and unwound**.

That verbal precision strengthens rather than weakens the political comparison. Affordability did
not disappear in emergencies. It changed from a veto into a design problem.

## Austerity, the nurse and what the economy could do

Osborne's public case repeatedly used household language: Britain should “live within its means”
and deficit reduction was compared with paying a national credit card.[^19] Yet the first OBR was
explicitly a forecaster: ministers still chose the goals, taxes and spending. The June 2010 Budget
added tightening of about 2% of GDP to Labour's inherited plan, with 74% of the total planned
tightening through spending.[^12]

The programme achieved a large reduction in annual borrowing. It missed its original debt target,
and the initial recovery forecast was far too optimistic: real GDP grew about 0.9% between 2010 Q1
and 2012 Q2 against a June 2010 forecast of 5.7%.[^20] The OBR later judged unexpectedly large
2011–12 consolidation one contributor, alongside the euro-area crisis, exports, credit conditions
and productivity. The safe charge is not “austerity alone caused stagnation”; it is that demand was
withdrawn, public investment fell early, and persistent underperformance repeatedly extended the
programme.

The NHS is a revealing mixed case. The 2010 Spending Review planned a tiny real increase in total
NHS funding across four years while cutting capital DEL in cash terms.[^2] IFS calculated only 1.3%
average annual real growth from 2009–10 to 2015–16, compared with 4.1% historically.[^21] This was
protection relative to other departments and pressure relative to demand at the same time.

More money in 2010–17 could have bought:

- better retention and morale, additional hours and less reliance on expensive agency cover;
- more training commissions and clinical placements whose payoff arrived after roughly three
  years;
- equipment, beds and estate maintenance where suppliers and construction capacity existed;
- earlier workforce planning and less avoidable scarcity.

It could not have bought instantly qualified nurses in unlimited numbers. The Migration Advisory
Committee found nurse-training commissions fell nearly one-fifth between 2010 and 2013, largely for
financial reasons, and called the later shortage substantially foreseeable.[^22] The NAO found a
reported clinical staffing shortfall of about 50,000 in 2014, while warning that vacancy data were
poor.[^23] Headcount nevertheless rose on some measures. Pay was one cause of retention pressure,
not the only cause of shortage.

The wider investment evidence has the same clock. The OBR's 2024 central scenario estimated that a
sustained public-investment increase of 1% of GDP could raise potential output by just under
0.5% after five years and about 2.5% after fifty years. It also modelled weaker cases where public
investment competes with private activity for labour and inputs.[^51] Capacity can be built; the
resource demand begins before the productive return.

This earns a precise line for the film:

> The NHS budget still rose, but unusually slowly against rising demand. Pay restraint was built
> into the savings plan while training, retention and capacity lagged.

## “Tax where the money comes to rest”

### What the phrase can responsibly mean

The phrase works when it names a distributive choice after a public intervention. QE raises bond
prices and, through portfolio rebalancing and lower yields, other asset values; government
contracts can create private profit; infrastructure can raise nearby land values; monopoly or
scarcity can turn shocks into rents. Tax can reclaim part of those gains, reduce inequality and
release real resources for public use.

Candidate instruments are not interchangeable:

| Resting place | Plausible tax | Strength | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land value created by place and infrastructure | Recurrent land-value or reformed property tax | Immobile base; reaches economic rent | Valuation and transition; does not target every asset gain |
| Realised capital gains | Reformed CGT, aligned rates, exit rules | Reaches gains concentrated among high-gain taxpayers | Timing, lock-in, migration and avoidance |
| Excess returns/rents | Rent-based corporation or sector tax | Can tax return above required investment return with less distortion | Difficult baseline; bad gross-revenue design can cut supply |
| Exceptional energy profit | Windfall or permanent rent tax | Can capture shock-driven rents | Temporary rules and allowances affect investment incentives |
| High incomes and accumulated wealth | Income, inheritance or carefully designed wealth tax | Broad distributional reach | Liquidity, valuation, avoidance and political durability |

IFS's Mirrlees work makes the clean case for taxing land rent; its 2024 CGT review distinguishes
normal returns from excess returns caused by luck, rent or disguised labour and recommends an exit
charge as part of reform.[^24] HMRC's latest statistics show the concentration: in 2024–25, gains of
£5m or more generated 45% of CGT while involving fewer than 1% of CGT taxpayers.[^25] The Wealth Tax
Commission found a one-off wealth tax administratively feasible, but judged a recurrent wealth tax
more difficult and avoidance-sensitive.[^26] IMF work supports well-designed economic-rent taxes
while warning that poorly designed windfall taxes on gross revenue can suppress supply and raise
prices.[^27]

### What it cannot mean

Pounds do not carry tags. Public spending can become wages, imports, debt repayment, pension saving,
business revenue and asset bids in the same circuit. “Rest” is a snapshot of claims and power, not a
literal terminus. Some gains leave the UK, some are unrealised, and some belong to pension funds
whose beneficiaries are widely dispersed even when management and ownership are concentrated.

Tax also acts on different clocks. An immediate energy import shock raises prices before a later tax
can withdraw demand. A tax on investment returns might reduce future supply. Strong enforcement can
shift avoidance rather than eliminate it. Taxing a gain can improve distribution without creating
the nurse, turbine or gas cargo whose absence caused inflation.

Four schools illuminate different parts:

- **Mainstream public finance:** tax raises revenue under the statutory budget system, changes
  behaviour, shares burdens and supports debt sustainability.
- **Keynesian stabilisation:** taxes and transfers alter aggregate demand; automatic stabilisers
  lean against the cycle, while discretionary changes face timing and multiplier questions.
- **Sectoral balances:** one sector's net borrowing is another's net lending. This is an accounting
  identity, not proof that any deficit is desirable or inflation-free.[^28]
- **MMT:** a floating-rate sovereign currency issuer faces real-resource and inflation constraints
  before a household-style financing constraint; taxes withdraw purchasing power and support the
  currency. Serious MMT sources also recognise bottlenecks before measured full employment and the
  importance of exchange-rate and foreign-currency exposure.[^29]

The strongest usable formulation is:

> Spend to mobilise real capacity where it exists; build capacity where it does not; tax rents and
> concentrated gains so public action does not harden private power; withdraw or redirect demand
> when it collides with supply.

That is a policy test, not a magic loop.

## Hostile-economist test

The argument was tested as if an orthodox public-finance economist, a central banker, a heterodox
critic and a sceptical voter were in the room.

### Objection 1: the montage compares assets with expenses

A bank share purchase can be sold. A collateralised loan is repaid. A guarantee may never be
called. QE swaps one public liability for another. A permanent pay award recurs and is mostly a
current cost.

**Concession earned:** never compare headline amounts as fungible “pots”. Put instrument, actual use,
recovery and duration on screen. What survives is the comparison of decision speed and declared
necessity.

### Objection 2: QE did not finance nurses

QE was a monetary-policy operation in secondary markets. During Covid, the DMO issued £485.8bn of
gilts in 2020–21 while the Bank made separately mandated secondary-market purchases. The temporary
Ways and Means extension was not materially drawn.[^16][^17]

**Concession earned:** keep Treasury and Bank in different columns. The film may show that their
actions interacted; it must not call QE an Exchequer grant.

### Objection 3: the rescue had a counterfactual beneficiary

Bank owners and creditors were protected unevenly, but allowing the payments system and credit
intermediation to collapse would have harmed workers, depositors and public finances. QE's absolute
asset gains were concentrated, but a deeper recession would also have been regressive.

**Concession earned:** show direct recipients and intended beneficiaries separately. Distribution is
not proved by identifying only the first account credited.

### Objection 4: a currency issuer can still run out of purchasing power

The UK issues sterling and has little foreign-currency public debt, a long average debt maturity and
deep markets. That gives it more room than a household or a foreign-currency borrower. It remains
exposed to imported energy and goods, exchange-rate depreciation, gilt yields, debt-interest costs
and confidence in the inflation mandate.[^30] QE has shortened the effective maturity of part of the
public balance sheet because reserve remuneration moves with Bank Rate.[^31]

**Concession earned:** say “can always make the sterling payment” only with “cannot guarantee what
sterling will buy.” Institutional independence and market response are real constraints even when
technical insolvency is not.

### Objection 5: inflation begins before full employment

Skills, electricity, housing, ports, semiconductors or gas can bottleneck while unemployment still
exists elsewhere. Both MMT's serious literature and its critics recognise this.[^29][^32] In 2022,
the Bank attributed much of the inflation rise to energy, imported goods and global supply, with
domestic wage and service pressure increasingly relevant.[^33]

**Concession earned:** replace one national “capacity gauge” with sector-specific labour, material,
import and time constraints. General demand restraint can suppress useful activity while failing to
create the scarce input.

### Objection 6: higher NHS pay is not the same as higher NHS capacity

Pay can improve retention and recruitment, but a three-year training lag, clinical placements,
housing and migration rules matter. Raising pay without expanding supply may redistribute within a
fixed service before it expands output.

**Concession earned:** frame pay as retention and fairness first, capacity support second. Pair the
pay card with training commissions and vacancy evidence.

### Objection 7: post-war Britain did pay

The NHS was founded with very high debt, but also with taxes, rationing, constrained private
consumption, capital controls, financial repression, borrowing and external assistance. The war
comparison proves capacity was politically allocated; it does not prove painless finance.

**Concession earned:** the historical card must display what households surrendered as well as what
the state built.

### Objection 8: “tax it back later” is too late

Later taxes cannot reverse today's queue, blackout or price spike. Anticipated taxes may change
behaviour now, but legislation, valuation, enforcement and avoidance all take time.

**Concession earned:** present tax as distribution, demand management and legitimacy—not an eraser
for every inflationary effect.

### Verdict under attack

The strongest hostile economist cannot erase the documented asymmetry in political permission.
They can and should erase the lazy equivalence of the sums. The defensible conclusion is:

> Emergencies reveal the state's capacity to decide and coordinate quickly. Economics still decides
> which instruments work, who bears risk, which resources exist, and when nominal demand becomes
> inflation instead of output.

### Current rights rulings

- Parliament's download terms permit fair and accurate reporting but prohibit satire, ridicule,
  denigration, entertainment, advertising, promotion, commercial sponsorship and financial gain;
  they also require out-of-context safeguards and can be changed.[^9]
- Parliament says the OPL does **not** cover live or archived audio/video.[^36]
- The commercial stock-footage route is Parliament's photo/video team; secure written terms before
  designing around the clip.[^37]
- BBC Studios directs clip licensing enquiries through its licensing process. Availability and cost
  remain open.[^35]
- GOV.UK text is normally OGL unless a page identifies exclusions. Logos, third-party images and
  quoted material are not automatically covered.
- Bank of England page text, PDFs, charts, photos and footage use Bank-specific terms; replotting
  OGL database data is safer than lifting designed graphics.[^38]
- NAO material is often available for reuse, but its notice distinguishes Crown, Parliamentary and
  NAO copyright. Check the exact work.[^39]

## Recommended full-film spine

The narrator counts. The audience supplies the verdict.

1. **Consequence:** the nurse's 2009 and 2017 payslips; no theory yet. One sentence establishes
   26 years' service. Keep the later speaker unnamed pending the visual check.
2. **The language of necessity:** Osborne's dated household/credit-card words; the freeze and 1%
   cards; waiting lists, training commissions and retention pressure.
3. **Receipt one—rescue:** `2008 · BANKING SYSTEM · £50bn CAPITAL CAPACITY / £250bn GUARANTEE /
   £200bn LIQUIDITY · USED DIFFERENTLY`. Animate three mechanisms, not one cheque.
4. **The bill Britain said it inherited:** separate rescue assets from recession borrowing and the
   estimated structural deficit. Show deficit down, debt ratio up, investment down then recovering.
5. **The old emergency memory:** three brief receipts—1694 credit mobilisation, 1914 emergency
   currency, 1940–45 finance plus rationing. No heroic montage without the cost.
6. **The thing built under debt:** NHS foundation as the use of capacity after mobilisation, not a
   free lunch. Keynes may answer only in verified words under the existing guardrails.
7. **The shake:** Question Time, only the amount necessary for criticism; May's full hard-choice
   answer, not an isolated catchphrase.
8. **Receipt two—24 days:** the £1bn NI agreement appears as six allocations and two time horizons.
   Do not say the DUP personally received it.
9. **Receipt three—Covid:** split-screen Treasury and Bank. Grants, guarantees, gilt issuance and QE
   arrive on different rails. The numbers stop looking like one pile.
10. **Where it rested:** QE asset-price transmission, bank-recovery receipts, furlough beneficiaries
    and fraud/counterfactuals. Each first recipient is paired with intended beneficiary and observed
    consequence.
11. **The limit:** energy, imports, vacancies and training time. The narrator says only: “You can
    print the money. You can't print the nurse. She was already there.”
12. **The tax question:** land, gains and rents appear as possible collection points; imports,
    avoidance and time punch holes in a literal recycling pipe.
13. **The 2022 proof of reflex:** £65bn capacity becomes £19.3bn used, then unwound. Institutions can
    move quickly and still count precisely.
14. **Return to the payslip:** `WHAT FOR · WHO FIRST · HOW LONG`. No stated moral. The viewer has
    enough receipts to ask why one necessity had to prove affordability before another did.
15. **Mulberries/song:** use the documented trees only with cleared image access; `shake / starve /
    plant` remains metaphor, not mechanism.

**Offshoring ruling:** exclude it. Dyson's 2002 Malaysia move offers a named chooser, supplier/labour
cost rationale and roughly 1,000 announced Malmesbury job losses, but it predates and does not cause
the austerity/QE sequence.[^40] A general deindustrialisation montage would import the ownership
argument from *Git Push Origin Master* and overload this film. Retain the case for a later film or a
single footnote only if an editor needs a concrete example of capacity being allowed to leave.

## 60–90 second vertical proof

**0–08s — consequence.** Tight frame on two anonymised payslip totals: 2009 / 2017. On-screen:
`NURSE · 26 YEARS`. Archive audio, if cleared: “How is that fair?”

**08–20s — answer.** May's answer runs long enough to include “hard choices”, pay restraint and the
magic-money-tree line. Card: `2 JUNE 2017 · NHS PAY · 1%`.

**20–32s — changed priority.** Six DUP allocations click into a total. Card:
`26 JUNE · NORTHERN IRELAND · £1bn COMMITTED · £850m/2 YEARS + £150m/5 YEARS`.

**32–48s — instruments.** Three 2008 columns arrive separately:
`CAPITAL · GUARANTEE · LIQUIDITY`. Voice: “Not one cheque. Not the same thing. All authorised when
the threat was named an emergency.”

**48–65s — speed.** Split screen: `TREASURY`—furlough grants and £485.8bn gilt issuance;
`BANK OF ENGLAND`—£200bn QE decision, purchases beginning next day. The split never collapses.

**65–77s — limit.** Empty training place, occupied ward, three-year calendar. Voice: “You can print
the money. You can't print the nurse. She was already there.”

**77–90s — question.** Return to payslip. Cards cycle: `WHAT FOR? · WHO FIRST? · HOW LONG?`. Cut
before answering. If no BBC licence, use the verified transcript as text and spend the saved seconds
on the 24-day interval.

## Never say

- “The banks got a £500bn cheque.”
- “The bailout caused the whole deficit.”
- “The structural deficit was a measured fact.”
- “Austerity paid off the debt.”
- “The NHS budget was cut” without naming the budget, years and real/cash measure.
- “Nurses' pay was frozen for seven years.”
- “Every nurse lost exactly 14%.”
- “The DUP got £1bn that afternoon for ten votes.”
- “The Bank printed £450bn in three months.”
- “QE money was lent into the economy by the banks.”
- “QE made every homeowner richer and everyone else poorer.”
- “The bottom got £3,000 and the top got £350,000 from QE.”
- “Treasury gave business £330bn.”
- “Furlough paid £70bn straight into workers' accounts in nine days.”
- “The Bank gave pension funds £65bn.”
- “Covid proved deficits do not matter.”
- “A country that issues its currency cannot go broke” without defining nominal, real and external
  constraints.
- “Taxing the money back prevents inflation.”
- “Sectoral balances prove this deficit is good.”
- “The 1694 subscription was money printing.”
- “Bradbury notes paid for the First World War.”
- “Britain built the NHS at 250% debt, so it cost nothing.”
- “The Bank planted its mulberries during the Blitz.” Say “in the early 1940s” or “during the war”
  unless the Bank supplies an exact planting date.
- “Parliament footage is public domain.”
