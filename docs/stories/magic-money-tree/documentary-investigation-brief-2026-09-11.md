# Magic Money Tree documentary — austerity, emergency and the question of affordability

**Status:** completed historical research commission, captured 2026-09-11 from Kai's direction.
The [resulting investigation](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md)
is complete. Kai subsequently chose [The Future He Never Saw](./storyboard.md) as the single
canonical documentary storyboard. The wider scope and reusable research prompt below are retained
as provenance, not instructions to restart the investigation or restore its earlier film structure.

## The thought to preserve

The documentary should leave one question active in the viewer's head:

> **Why is a modest pay rise for a nurse described as something Britain cannot afford, while a
> war, a bank rescue or a national emergency is treated as something for which money can be found?**

The immediate British sequence is the financial crisis, the Cameron–Osborne austerity programme
from 2010, years of constrained public-sector pay, the nurse's 2017 Question Time challenge and
Theresa May's “magic money tree” answer, followed by the scale and speed of fiscal and monetary
action during Covid. The comparison is not meant to prove that wars, bank rescues, pay rises and
pandemic support have identical economic effects. It asks who is allowed to define necessity,
which real resources can be mobilised, who receives the money first, where its gains collect, and
why “affordability” changes register when the political priority changes.

The desired experience is recognition rather than instruction. Dates, decisions, sums, recipients
and consequences should accumulate until the viewer asks the question without the narrator
delivering a general theory at them.

## The working hypothesis — to test, not repeat as fact

A currency-issuing government is not constrained in the same way as a household. It can mobilise
idle labour and real resources through public spending, borrowing and coordination with monetary
institutions. The meaningful limits include available people, materials, productive capacity,
inflation, distribution, exchange-rate exposure, institutional rules and political consent.

Taxation is part of that system: it creates fiscal space, changes behaviour and distribution,
supports demand for the currency, and can reclaim some gains after public spending circulates.
The proposed phrase **“tax where the money comes to rest”** is a vivid distributional hypothesis,
not yet an adequate account of tax, inflation or public finance. The investigation must establish
what it can responsibly mean and where it stops being true.

Public investment can expand useful capacity when it buys available labour, skills, equipment and
materials. It cannot conjure a nurse, house, energy supply or supply chain that does not exist.
This is why the documentary's existing line remains valuable:

> **You can print the money. You can't print the nurse. She was already there.**

## Corrections and distinctions to carry from the start

- The Cameron–Clegg coalition took office in **May 2010**; “2020-10” in the originating thought
  was a spoken slip for the period from 2010 onward.
- Government spending, gilt issuance, commercial-bank lending, central-bank reserve creation,
  quantitative easing, emergency liquidity and equity/capital support are related but different.
  The film must not collapse them into “the government printed money.”
- A headline facility is not necessarily money used. Record announced ceilings, actual outturns,
  guarantees, loans, asset purchases and grants separately.
- QE's distributional effects, bank-rescue beneficiaries and the fiscal costs of austerity require
  direct evidence. Do not infer a single causal chain from their chronological order.
- Offshoring may belong as a named decision with beneficiaries and consequences. It is not assumed
  to explain why QE raised asset prices, and it must not become unaimed decline nostalgia.
- Covid proved that institutions can act at great speed when government defines a crisis. It did
  not prove that spending has no limit or cost. Compare the decisions and real constraints, not
  merely the size of the numbers.
- The strongest version of the war comparison asks how Britain mobilised people and materials
  when financial orthodoxy said it was broke. It does not romanticise war or imply that wartime
  controls are a peacetime programme.

## The investigation

### 1. What Britain said it had to repay after 2008

Build a dated record of the Cameron and Osborne case for austerity: speeches, Budgets, Spending
Reviews, fiscal rules and the household/debt language used with the public. Separate the bank
rescue, recession borrowing and structural-deficit argument. Test what was prediction, what was
policy choice and what happened to debt, growth, investment, wages and public services.

### 2. The nurse as the human measure

Trace public-sector and NHS pay policy from 2010 to the 2017 Question Time exchange: freeze, cap,
inflation-adjusted pay and staffing/capacity. Establish the nurse's exact words, May's complete
answer and the subsequent £1bn DUP agreement without implying the two expenditures were
economically identical. The dramatic comparison is priority and speed.

### 3. When money was found

Create comparable receipt cards for:

- the 2008 financial rescue and its actual forms and recipients;
- QE rounds and the Bank of England's stated transmission mechanism;
- the 2017 DUP agreement;
- Covid fiscal support, furlough, guarantees and the Bank's 2020 asset purchases;
- the 2022 LDI intervention, distinguishing the facility ceiling from actual use;
- British war-finance examples already present in canon: 1694, 1914 and 1940–45.

Each card should record: **date · decision-maker · purpose · instrument · announced amount · amount
used · time taken · direct recipient · intended beneficiary · observed distributional consequence ·
source · footage/status**. Nominal sums across centuries should never be compared without context.

### 4. What the economy could actually do

Test the real-resource account through cases where spending mobilised spare capacity and cases
where supply was constrained. Distinguish demand-driven inflation from energy, import, supply-chain
and margin effects. Ask what additional NHS spending could buy in 2010–17 given staffing and
training lags, and which constraints were themselves consequences of earlier investment choices.

### 5. “Tax where the money comes to rest”

Test this phrase against mainstream public-finance, Keynesian, sectoral-balances and MMT accounts.
Specify which taxes could reach asset gains, rents, excess profits, wealth or high incomes; when
tax acts too late to prevent inflation; and how avoidance, capital mobility, imports and monetary
policy complicate the recycling metaphor. Seek the strongest serious objection and retain any
concession it earns.

### 6. Offshoring and ownership

Investigate offshoring as a separate supporting thread: the decisions, sectors, dates, labour and
regional consequences, and who gained. Test whether it illuminates this film's affordability
question or imports GPOM's ownership argument and overloads the documentary. Default editorial
position: exclude unless one short, well-evidenced chooser/beneficiary beat earns its place.

### 7. Footage and documents

For every candidate beat, identify the exact source asset, rights holder, date, duration needed,
licence/fair-dealing rationale, attribution, download route and fallback. Prefer primary records,
public-domain archive and official documents. Treat broadcaster footage, Parliament video,
commercial newsreels and Bank photographs as rights questions requiring verification, not as
available merely because they can be viewed online.

## Story constraints

- The question is the engine. The narrator counts and compares; it does not state the moral.
- Consequence precedes explanation. The nurse supplies the human scale.
- Name each decision-maker and beneficiary. “The system” is not a character.
- Honour the inflation objection early and accurately; never mock the viewer for having it.
- Keep the Bank and Treasury distinct.
- Use the existing **shake / starve / plant** grammar only if the evidence still supports each card.
- Give the best opposing economist enough space to be recognisable, then let the historical record
  determine what survives.
- Real people remain archive-only. Keynes may be dramatised under the story's existing guardrails.
- Do not use contemporary far-right imagery as shorthand for the conclusion. Keep the historical
  two-burn distinction exact.
