## The story in one line

**John Maynard Keynes talking to a tree. That's it.** The friendship and
collaboration between Keynes and the Magic Money Tree — a sweet, patient thing
operating against the most difficult problems in the world — is the vehicle that
teaches the viewer how money actually works.

## Format

**A narrated YouTube video.** Bernard Cornwell-style historically *inspired*
fiction (not a documentary of Keynes) for the park scenes, intercut with
documentary/news-register era sections that use **real archive footage** (May's
Question Time clip, Nixon's address) under BadCode narration. UK footage usage is
**fair dealing** (criticism/review/quotation): keep clips short and always
surrounded by commentary. Narration-led; **the song enters for the final montage
and the video ends on the track's hard cut — "There is no magic money tree."**

**Production mode (locked 2026-08-07):** the fiction registers are **AI-generated
film** — the park across the decades, the opening ascent, Garden Court, the ghost.
**Real people are archive-only**: May, the nurse, Nixon and Harry Leslie Smith
appear exclusively as genuine footage under fair dealing or licence, never
generated and never voice-cloned. Keynes is the single exception (80 years dead,
historical-fiction register, economics verbatim-anchored). The film's credibility
rests on the *facts* being checkable — see The twist → the third turn — not on the
footage being documentary, so generating the fiction costs us nothing.

## Key concept

Money is created out of thin air, and **that is fine — wise, even — when it
builds real things.** The magic money tree is real, and it is good. The law it
teaches (Keynes, verbatim, BBC radio, 2 April 1942): **"Anything we can actually
do we can afford"** — and its counterpart from the same broadcast: inflation is
*"a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply."* The crime is not
printing; the crime is **where the money comes to rest**. Poured into wards,
houses, bridges, it flows and everyone eats. Parked in assets, it freezes — the
nurse's flat payslip and the yacht in Monaco are the same banknote at two
addresses.

**The mechanism that makes this showable (added 2026-08-07 — full lore in
[`the-grove.md`](./the-grove.md)):** a money tree **fruits** money on command —
nothing is chopped, nothing used up. It has exactly one limit, and it isn't
gold: **it can only fruit as much as there is real work to buy.** Ask for more
than the country can physically make and it still fruits, because it cannot
refuse — but the notes come out **hollow**: identical, correctly numbered,
worth nothing. That is inflation, and it is Keynes's own definition rendered as
physics rather than opinion. So the story answers the question everyone
actually has — *if printing is fine, why isn't infinite printing fine?* —
without a lecture and without conceding the point. **Two distinct failures:
hollow** (printed past capacity — Weimar 1923) and **frozen** (printed
correctly, then parked — 2020 onward). The story has always been about the
second; being able to *show* the first is what earns us the right to defend
printing at all.

**And the stakes are not abstract (the 2026-08-06 conclusion):** get the money
wrong and fascism comes back. Weimar Germany burned twice — once by printing
without limit, once by refusing to print at all — and starved people went
looking for someone to blame. The postwar settlement (the NHS, full employment,
homes) was built *deliberately* as the machine that makes fascism unnecessary.
Misusing the tree — money pooling in assets while working people can't eat —
dismantles that machine and re-arms the despair. **The wealth gap is undoing
what the WWII generation fought and died for.** That is the story's gut-punch,
and it is a unification of left and right: the left gets the NHS and
anti-austerity; the right gets the veterans and what they fought for; both get
handed the same enemy — the misuse of the money system, not each other.

## Background (real grounding — full detail in [`research/`](./research/))

- **The grove is real and citable** (✅ verified 2026-08-07 — see
  [`research/central-bank-trees.md`](./research/central-bank-trees.md), whose
  *Never say* list is binding). **Four black mulberries grow in the Bank of
  England's Garden Court at Threadneedle Street.** The Bank planted them **in
  the early 1940s** — during the war this story is set in — because the oldest
  banknote in its collection is **printed on mulberry bark** (China, Hongwu,
  1368–98), and because mulberry roots grow shallow and sideways, which is what
  you need when there is **only a foot of soil over a graveyard**: Garden Court
  is the former churchyard of St Christopher le Stocks. The Bank's own museum
  blog is titled *"Does money grow on trees?"* **We did not invent this
  premise; we found it.** Also load-bearing: the Bank was chartered **27 July
  1694 to fund a war against France** (£1.2m at 8%, subscribed in 12 days) —
  *the institution that told a nurse there is no magic money tree was founded
  as one, to fight a war, in a fortnight*; the original Garden Court's single
  **European lime was cut down in the 1930s rebuild**; and the first English £1
  note, the **Treasury "Bradbury" of 7 August 1914**, was rushed out on
  **postage-stamp paper** because no banknote paper was ready.
- **Keynes, "How Much Does Finance Matter?", BBC, 2 April 1942** — the exact
  quote, the architect exchange ("you don't build houses with money… won't there
  be enough bricks?"), and inflation defined as the real constraint. See
  [`research/economics-of-money-creation.md`](./research/economics-of-money-creation.md).
- **1945–48:** UK debt ~250% of GDP, near-insolvent, rationed — and the NHS
  opens 5 July 1948; 806,857 council houses by 1951; ~20% of the economy
  nationalised. The debt was never repaid, it was outgrown (debt +137% over 30
  years, GDP +1,200%) — with real rates held below inflation for 24 of 30 years:
  **"they made the rentiers pay for the hospitals."** Keynes dies 21 April
  1946 — two years before the NHS proves him right.
- **Nixon, 15 August 1971** — off gold entirely; "your dollar will be worth just
  as much tomorrow as it is today." (The "Britain demanded $3bn" trigger story
  is a myth — don't use it.)
- **2008** — bailouts and QE begin; QE is *designed* to raise asset prices
  (BoE's own "hot potato" mechanism).
- **2 June 2017** — a nurse of 26 years (real, unnamed; her 2009 payslip matched
  her 2017 payslip) asks Theresa May about pay; May: *"there isn't a magic money
  tree that we can shake…"* The line was Cameron's from 2013 and Amber Rudd's
  scripted attack line two days earlier. 24 days later, £1bn for the DUP.
  See [`research/theresa-may-question-time.md`](./research/theresa-may-question-time.md).
- **2020–21** — £450bn conjured in ~3 months (vs ~£445bn for the whole
  2009–2017 era); the VIP lane; March 2021, the same nurses offered 1%; the
  £122m gowns judgment; the yacht bought May 2021.
  **Guardrails in [`research/michelle-mone-ppe-medpro.md`](./research/michelle-mone-ppe-medpro.md)
  are mandatory** (real figures — never "£270m"; civil judgment against the
  company; no criminal conviction; her own BBC admission is the safe material).
- **Flow vs freeze** (Ken Honda, verbatim): hoarded money isn't dammed, it
  **freezes** — "when beliefs are too stuck… the flow of money can easily
  freeze." Healthy money is a lake fed by streams; unhealthy money a stagnant
  pond. Visual system, not lecture.
  See [`research/ken-honda-happy-money.md`](./research/ken-honda-happy-money.md).
- **2021–23 inflation guardrail:** the narrator must NOT claim printing caused
  it (supply shocks + margins did most of it). Keynes's formulation is the one
  that survives.
- **The fascism-economics thread** (✅ verified 2026-08-06 — see
  [`research/fascism-and-the-wealth-gap.md`](./research/fascism-and-the-wealth-gap.md),
  whose safe-to-assert list is binding): Keynes predicted it all in *The
  Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) — *"vengeance, I dare predict,
  will not limp"*; *"Men will not always die quietly."* Weimar's **double
  burn**: the 1923 hyperinflation destroyed savings ($1 = 4.2 trillion marks
  at peak); but the Nazi *votes* came in the 1930–33 austerity-deflation years
  (**2.63% in 1928 → 37.27% July 1932**, 6m+ unemployed) — approved line:
  *"Germany burned twice — once by printing without limit, once by refusing to
  print at all. The first burn took their savings. The second took their
  republic."* Anchored on Galofré-Vilà et al. ("Austerity and the Rise of the
  Nazi Party" — austerity econometrically tied to Nazi vote share; Hitler's own
  pamphlet: *"this emergency decree will help my party to victory"*) and
  Funke/Schularick/Trebesch ("Going to Extremes" — far-right vote share rises
  **~30% relative** (a third of what they had, NOT 30 points) after *financial*
  crises specifically, not ordinary recessions). The explicit
  anti-fascism-by-economics quote belongs to **FDR, 11 Jan 1944**: *"People who
  are hungry and out of a job are the stuff of which dictatorships are made"*
  (no equivalent British quote exists in Hansard — don't invent one; Beveridge
  gives us the five giants and *"a time for revolutions, not for patching"*).
  **Modern guardrails:** the UK is NOT in hyperinflation (2022 peak: 11.1%,
  ONS) — the UK's parallel is to the *austerity* burn; and **UK income
  inequality is NOT at a century high** (ONS shows the opposite) — the honest
  UK line is *"the 1980s dismantled the postwar compression and it was never
  restored,"* and the strong UK number is **wealth** (top 10% own ~49% of it).
  The US is where top-1% income really is back at Roaring-Twenties levels.
- **The third German episode — Mefo, 1934–38** (new 2026-08-07; the plot
  connector the story was missing). **Hjalmar Schacht**, the man who *saved* the
  currency in 1923 with the Rentenmark, later built a **counterfeit** money
  tree: **Mefo bills**, drawn on a letterbox company with no employees and no
  operations, conjuring **~12bn marks off the books** — invisible to the budget,
  to the Reichstag, and to the Treaty of Versailles, with **secrecy as an
  explicit design goal**. It financed rearmament. **The bombs falling on London
  while Keynes sits on the bench were bought with imaginary money by a man
  running a fake company.** The mirror is exact and needs no commentary: Schacht
  hid twelve billion in a shell; Keynes published a pamphlet (*How to Pay for
  the War*, 1940) and went on the radio to ask the country to lend him its
  wages. Same instrument, same physics, opposite ethics — **the sunlight is the
  whole difference.** Guardrail: use the mechanism, not the man's soul (Schacht
  was tried at Nuremberg and acquitted; don't relitigate him).
- **Veteran material:** built on **one real voice, not an invented chorus** —
  **Harry Leslie Smith** (RAF 1941–48, 1923–2018, *Harry's Last Stand*,
  *Don't Let My Past Be Your Future*), the near-unique on-record veteran who
  made exactly this argument. "The last of them said this" is true; "they all
  say this" is not. (Harry Billinge stays out — a remember-the-dead voice,
  never a political one.) **Footage strategy:** US public-domain WWII archive
  (Signal Corps/OWI via NARA/archive.org — free, adaptable, monetisable) as
  the workhorse; IWM only under paid licence (its free licence excludes
  monetised YouTube); BBC/Pathé licence-or-don't-touch; UK fair dealing
  reserved for short clips we explicitly comment on (the May clip) — archive
  as mood-wallpaper under music is NOT fair dealing.

## Beats

1. **Cold open — the war, black and white.** 1940–42 montage: Dunkirk, the
   Battle of Britain, the Blitz. America not yet in the war. Churchill in real
   trouble. It's fucked, and everyone knows it. Register: gritty, desperate,
   the strain a nation unites around. **Plant, without labouring it, what the
   war is *against*: fascism** — the flags, the marches, the machine we are
   fighting. The ending will collect this.
2. **The bench.** St James's Park, 1942 (park locked 2026-08-07). **The
   opening ascent, now canon:** we enter beat 2 from below — the Cabinet War
   Rooms, the map table, the strain of beat 1 compressed into one room — and
   the camera rises: through the ceiling, through concrete, through earth,
   **through roots**, up into the grass of St James's Park, to an empty bench,
   as Keynes walks the path towards it. The geography is real and load-bearing:
   the War Rooms sit beneath the Treasury's wartime building on the park's
   edge — Keynes's employer directly above Churchill's bunker, the bench across
   Horse Guards Road. The war is being run under the grass; the answer grows
   over it. *(Hidden edge for the narrator, one line max: the man under the
   grass put the pound back on gold in 1925; the man walking to the bench wrote
   "The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill" telling him not to. It's 1942,
   and they're both still cleaning up.)* Keynes sits wrestling with the
   impossible sum: how does a broke country fight a total war? The tree speaks:
   *"Mate. You need to speak to me."* — *"…Who the hell are you?"* — *"I'm the magic
   money tree. Nobody knows about me. But you seem to be having a problem."*
   **Its origin, told light and early** (lore: [`the-grove.md`](./the-grove.md)):
   in **August 1914** a clerk at the Bank of England pocketed a first-issue
   Bradbury — the first £1 note England ever had, printed in three days on
   postage-stamp paper because there was a war on — walked west out of the City,
   and put it in the ground as a joke. The notes are the seed. Twenty-eight years
   later the joke is the only one of its kind that anybody has ever *talked* to.
   And the tease that sets up beat 3: **it is not the only one, and it knows the
   others.**
3. **The collaboration — and the scar.** Keynes carries a wound the tree
   recognises: **he predicted all of this in 1919** (*The Economic Consequences
   of the Peace*) — starve a country economically and it goes mad. Nobody
   listened. So the stakes on the bench are double: *win the war, and build a
   world where it can never restart.*
   **The history lesson is how this beat is now structured** — not Keynes
   explaining economics to the audience, but **the tree testifying** to what its
   own family has been made to do. It has relatives in every central bank on
   earth, and family talks. In order:
   - **1694, Threadneedle Street** — *"You're not asking me anything new, mate.
     It's what I was planted for."* The Bank was chartered to fund a war, in
     twelve days. Keynes's request isn't radical; it's the job description,
     forgotten.
   - **1923, Berlin** — the wheelbarrows. Asked for reparations the country
     could not physically produce, the Reichsbank tree fruited **hollow**:
     2,000 presses, 30 paper mills, and on 25 October it printed 120,000
     million million marks against a demand for a quintillion — *it could not
     print fast enough to keep up with its own printing.* **The tree was not
     failing. It was screaming.** This is where the mechanism's limit lands, and
     where the audience's "…but Zimbabwe" objection gets answered and buried.
   - **15 November 1923** — they killed it and rooted a new one in **land**: the
     Rentenmark, backed by mortgaged German farms and factories. Inflation
     collapsed in weeks. **Germany accidentally proved our thesis** — back money
     with what a country can *make*, not with metal, and it holds.
   - **1930–33** — burn two: the tree standing in full fruit, ordered not to
     drop. Six million unemployed; the Nazi vote 2.63% → 37.27%. *(The fascism
     brief's guardrails are binding: the votes came from the deflation, not the
     inflation.)*
   - **1934–38 — the fake tree, and the turn.** Schacht doesn't use the
     Reichsbank tree; he builds a counterfeit one out of a letterbox company.
     **The bombs falling on London tonight were bought with imaginary money by a
     man running a fake company.** The enemy already knows how to do this. They
     did it first and they did it in the dark. **So Keynes has to do it in the
     light** — which is the argument that turns a history lesson into the plot,
     and the reason the next thing he does is publish and broadcast rather than
     conceal.

   Then the principles, now *earned* rather than asserted: balance tax,
   inflation, wages and the money supply; focus on productivity; government
   liquidity for *productive* ends is fine when coordinated. Not
   gold-in-the-ground; capacity. The law, spoken: **"Anything we can actually do
   we can afford."** Keynes takes it to the country in the open — *How to Pay
   for the War* (1940), then the real 1942 broadcast and the architect exchange.

   **The private grief, six lines, threaded through this beat or just after it:**
   the tree mentions its mother — the old European lime at Threadneedle Street,
   *"she'd know"* — and Keynes, who has the run of the Treasury and can simply
   ask, finds out. Cut down in the '30s for a better lobby. Garden dug up, the
   graves moved. **Four young mulberries planted over her last year**, roots
   deliberately shallow, who will never be asked a question, only shaken. And he
   has to come back to the bench and tell it.
4. **It works — the build.** Documentary register, the numbers playing over
   archive: victory; then, at ~250% debt and near-insolvency, the NHS opens,
   the houses go up, National Insurance arrives. In our darkest hour the tree
   came through — because the money chased real work. And the honest dark
   line: the rentiers paid for the hospitals. **Frame it as what it was: the
   anti-fascism machine** — jobs, homes, health, dignity; the deliberate
   construction of a country where the recruiters of despair find no one to
   recruit.
5. **The death — and the empty bench.** 21 April 1946: Keynes dies, worn out,
   two years before the NHS opens and proves him right. Then 5 July 1948:
   archive of the first NHS patients — and **the vindication scene plays to an
   empty bench.** The tree in full leaf, nobody to tell. Then the ghost sits
   down, unhurried — like he's late rather than dead. *"Did it work?"* —
   *"Mate. You should've seen it."* The friendship resumes mid-sentence.
6. **The ghost at the bench.** From here the friendship continues — the ghost
   of Keynes and the tree, same bench, watching history arrive:
   - **1971, Nixon.** Real footage / news register: off gold entirely ("your
     dollar will be worth just as much tomorrow"). Back to the park — the
     bancor in exactly one exchange: *"I told them in '44 this would happen.
     They preferred to win the argument."* — *"How'd that go?"* — *"They're
     about to find out."* No Bretton Woods scene; that line carries it. **The
     grove image lands here — but corrected (2026-08-07):** the old line was
     "after '71 they planted them everywhere," which is incoherent now that the
     first tree dates to 1694. The true version is stronger: **the trees were
     always there, and they were chained.** Gold was a collar — an arbitrary
     constraint from outside that had the convenient side effect of doing
     everyone's thinking for them. Nixon took the last one off. So the montage
     is not planting, it's **collars coming off, worldwide, at once** — every
     tree on earth suddenly free to fruit as much as it's asked for, in the
     hands of a profession that had never once established what the real limit
     was, because gold had been standing in for it. *They spent the next fifty
     years finding out. Badly.*
   - **2008.** They finally learned to shake the tree at scale — for the
     people who broke the machine, with the proceeds pointed at asset prices
     by design.
7. **The forgetting.** The 2017 May clip plays (real Question Time footage —
   perhaps on a phone someone left on the bench); the tree: *"…I'm literally
   right here."* But the clip is no longer the climax — **it's the nod: they
   have forgotten.** Then the proof, blow by blow: 2020, £450bn in three
   months — the tree *working*, furlough, the thing it was born for — then
   the VIP-lane door held open, the gowns, the 1% for the nurses in 2021, and
   the yacht: the money it grew, frozen solid on a deck in the Med. The gap
   numbers land here, honestly told: America's top-1% share back at
   Roaring-Twenties levels; Britain's postwar compression dismantled in the
   1980s and never restored — and the top 10% holding roughly half of all UK
   wealth.
8. **The consequence — what grows in the cold.** Real footage: the flags and
   the marches are back — here, across Europe, the same choreography the cold
   open showed us in black and white. Intercut with real WWII archive (US
   public-domain as the workhorse — see background) and **one real veteran's
   voice: Harry Leslie Smith** — the RAF veteran who spent his last years
   making exactly this argument (*Don't Let My Past Be Your Future*). One
   voice, honestly framed: *the last of them said this.* The register is not
   mockery; it is grief. The story's claim, made by juxtaposition, never
   lectured: *starve people of a future and someone will sell them someone to
   blame — we have seen this film.* The despair is manufactured by the misuse
   of the tree; the marchers are the recruited, not the enemy.
9. **The bench, in winter — cold ending.** The park. The tree bare out of
   season — its grief. The ghost of Keynes beside it, head in his hands.
   Everything they built — built precisely so this could never happen
   again — being unbuilt by people who say the tree isn't real. A final
   exchange (script to land it; candidate register: *"We didn't beat it,
   mate. We just gave it a long winter."*).
   **The last image (2026-08-07).** Cut from the bare tree in the park to
   **Garden Court, Threadneedle Street: four black mulberries in full leaf**,
   planted during the war, standing in a graveyard, roots spread deliberately
   shallow so they never reach the dead — *being shaken.* The four that were
   never asked a question, thriving; the one that was, bare. **All those people
   died for this.** Generated like the park scenes — the credibility play lives
   in the *facts* being checkable, not in the footage being documentary (see
   locked decisions: real people are archive-only, never generated). Then the
   song slams in over the closing montage and the video ends on the track's
   hard cut: **"There is no magic money tree."**

## The device

- **The friendship is the argument.** Every economic idea is delivered as
  conversation between a dying genius (then his ghost) and a patient tree —
  never as lecture. The viewer eavesdrops on a century.
- **Register split:** park scenes = warm historically-inspired fiction; era
  sections = documentary/news with real footage and BadCode narration; the two
  registers alternate and finally collide when the song takes over.
- **Flow vs freeze** as the visual grammar for money throughout (moving/warm
  vs pooled/frozen).
- **Emperor's New Coin: cameo only** — a background/easter-egg presence; the
  real linkage is social (posts linking story ↔ coin), not plot.

## The twist

The title is the lie told twice. The politicians say the tree doesn't exist —
while standing in a forest of them. And the audience discovers the tree was
never the printer gag they expected: it's the wisest character in the story,
and the only one nobody in power ever listens to.

**The third turn (new 2026-08-07): the joke is real.** The audience spends the
whole film treating "money grows on trees" as our conceit — and then finds out
that the Bank of England has **four mulberries growing in its courtyard**, that
it planted them **during the Blitz**, that it chose the species because **the
oldest banknote it owns is made of mulberry bark**, and that they stand in **a
graveyard** with roots kept shallow so as not to disturb the bodies. Every word
of that is on the Bank's own website, under the headline *"Does money grow on
trees?"* **We didn't write a fable and dress it as history. We found the history
and it was already a fable.** This is what makes the piece survive contact with
a sceptical viewer: the one thing they're sure we made up is the one thing they
can check.

**The deeper turn (the conclusion):** the story the viewer thought was about
economics is revealed to be about the war. The cold open wasn't scene-setting —
it was the stakes. What the tree and Keynes built in 1948 was the anti-fascism
machine, and the wealth gap is dismantling it. *All of those people died for
this — and we are giving it back.* Left and right walk out of the video with
the same enemy.
