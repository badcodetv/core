# The Grove — how magic money trees actually work

> **Added 2026-08-07 (Kai's lore session).** The worldbuilding layer under the story:
> what a magic money tree *is*, where they live, what the park tree's parentage is,
> and the history the tree can teach Keynes from the bench. Every load-bearing fact
> is cited in [`research/central-bank-trees.md`](./research/central-bank-trees.md).
>
> **This does not change the spine.** Keynes still talks to a tree; the tree is still
> a sage, not a gag. What this adds is a *mechanism* (so the economics can be shown
> instead of explained) and a *family* (so the tree can testify to history it
> personally remembers). See [`story.md`](./story.md) for how it lands in the beats.

## The one-line version

**Every central bank on earth has a magic money tree growing inside it. The Bank of
England has four, they are mulberries, they stand in a graveyard in the courtyard,
and it planted them during the Blitz.** That last sentence is not fiction.

## 1. The mechanism

**A magic money tree fruits money.** It is not chopped, milled or pulped — nothing
is consumed and nothing is used up. A central bank *asks*, and the tree drops. This
is the printer going brrr, and it is the oldest technology in finance: the first
paper money in human history — Song dynasty Sichuan, and the Yuan *chao* that
astonished Marco Polo — was printed on **paper made from mulberry bark**. Money
began as a tree. Everything since is an abstraction of that.

**The tree has exactly one limit, and it is not gold.**

> A tree can only fruit as much as there is real work to buy.

Ask for more than the country can actually make, and the tree still fruits — it
cannot refuse — but the notes come out **hollow**. Identical to look at. Correct
serial numbers. They buy nothing. That is inflation, and it is Keynes's own real
definition, verbatim from the 1942 broadcast: *"a demand in excess of the physical
possibilities of supply."*

This is the whole economic argument of the story, expressed as physics rather than
opinion:

| The question | The wrong answer | The tree's answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How much money should exist? | However much gold is in a hole | However much we can actually build |
| Can we afford it? | Only if we've saved up | *Anything we can actually do we can afford* |
| Why is printing dangerous? | Printing is always dangerous | Only when you outrun your own hands |
| What went wrong in 2020? | We printed too much | We printed fine — then let it settle in the wrong place |

**Hollow fruit is the visual.** Wheelbarrows of perfect, worthless notes. And its
opposite — the good fruit — is a note that gets *spent*, over and over, warm from
being handled. This plugs straight into the existing flow-vs-freeze grammar
([`research/ken-honda-happy-money.md`](./research/ken-honda-happy-money.md)):
hollow = printed past capacity; frozen = printed correctly and then parked.
**Two different failures.** The story has always been about the second. The lore now
lets us *show* the first and dismiss it, which is exactly the move that earns us the
right to defend printing at all.

## 2. Where they live

**Inside the central bank. Always.** A money tree is not wild. It is an institution
with bark. The building goes up around it.

- **Threadneedle Street, from 1694** — the first. The Bank of England was chartered
  on 27 July 1694 **to pay for a war against France**, after Beachy Head left William
  III with no navy and no credit. £1.2m at 8%, subscribed in twelve days. *The
  institution that told a nurse there is no magic money tree was founded as one, to
  fight a war, in a fortnight.*
- **A tree in every central bank since** — the model was copied the way every
  institution gets copied: badly, and by people who only wanted the fruit.
- **They are shaken, never spoken to.** This is the tragedy the story turns on. In
  three hundred years of central banking, exactly one human being has ever asked a
  money tree a question, and he only did it because he was too tired to be
  frightened.

## 3. The family tree (literally)

This is the bit that lets the tree testify.

### The Lime — the elder, Bank of England, c.1781–1930s

The original Garden Court at the Bank of England was the **churchyard of St
Christopher le Stocks**, taken over in 1781 after the Gordon Riots, on a promise not
to disturb the graves. At its centre stood **a single European lime** — painted in
1906, photographed around 1920. It stood there through the Napoleonic wars, the
Bradbury, the Great War, the Gold Standard, the crash.

**In the 1930s, during Herbert Baker's rebuild, they dug up the garden and cut it
down.** They also exhumed the dead — including Long Bill Jenkins, the 6'7½" clerk
buried in the courtyard at dawn on 1 April 1798 so the body snatchers couldn't sell
his skeleton — and shipped them to Nunhead.

**The Bank of England killed the only tree that remembered anything, to make room for
a better lobby.**

### The Four Mulberries — the replacements, early 1940s

In their place the Bank planted **four black mulberries**. Chosen because the oldest
banknote in the Bank's collection is printed on mulberry bark (China, Hongwu,
1368–98), and because **mulberry roots grow shallow and sideways**, which is what you
need when there is **only a foot of soil over a graveyard.**

They were planted **in the early 1940s** — during the war, while the story is
happening.

They are young. They are obedient. They have never been asked a question. Four
saplings with their roots spread flat across the coffins, doing as they are told.

> **The image that ends the story (canon — see story.md beat 9):** the four
> mulberries in leaf, being shaken, in a graveyard, roots deliberately too
> shallow to reach the dead. *All those people died for this.*

### The Park Tree — our tree, from 1914

**A clerk at the Bank of England planted a pound note in a London park, and it grew.**

The note was a **first-issue Bradbury**: a Treasury £1, 7 August 1914, three days
after Britain declared war — the **first £1 note England ever had** (before it, the
smallest Bank of England note was £5), rushed out to stop a run on gold, and printed
on **paper meant for postage stamps** because nobody had banknote paper ready.

Government money. Wrong paper. Panic. First week of a war.

The clerk — unnamed, and he should stay unnamed — took one, walked west out of the
City to **St James's Park** (locked 2026-08-07), and put it in the ground. The
distance is a feature: he was hiding it — and he chose, without knowing it, the
ground across the road from the Treasury, above where the next war would be run
from a bunker. Canon does not say why he did it. **Best available reading:
he did it as a joke, and it is the single most consequential thing anyone at the Bank
of England did that century.**

Because the notes *are* the seed. So the park tree is **the lime's child** — a young
lime in a London park, feral, unlicensed, growing where no institution can shake it,
and in 1942 it is **twenty-eight years old**.

### The scene this parentage exists to produce

**The tree does not know its mother is dead.** It has been in the park since 1914.
Nobody visits. Nobody tells a tree anything.

At some point in the collaboration it mentions her — *"there's an old lime at
Threadneedle Street, she'd know"* — and Keynes, who has the run of the Treasury and
can simply ask, finds out. And then has to come back to the bench and tell it.

Cut down in the '30s. Garden dug up. Four new ones planted last year.

That is the story's private grief, sitting underneath the public one, and it costs
about six lines.

## 4. The history the tree can testify to

The tree has family in every central bank, and family talks. This is the device that
turns beat 3 from a lecture into **a conversation with a witness** — and it is how we
get Weimar into a story set in 1942 without a flashback narrator.

Chronological, and the order matters:

1. **1694, Threadneedle Street — "it's what I was planted for."** The first tree,
   chartered to fund a war. Keynes's request is not radical; it is the Bank's
   original job description, forgotten.
2. **1914, London — the Bradbury.** The Treasury conjures a new pound note in three
   days on stamp paper. The tree's own birth. Told lightly, from the inside.
3. **1923, Berlin — burn one, the hollow fruit.** The Reichsbank tree asked for
   reparations the country could not physically produce. ~2,000 presses, ~30 paper
   mills, ~150 printing firms. On 25 October 1923 it printed 120,000 million million
   marks against a demand for a quintillion — *it could not print fast enough to keep
   up with its own printing.* The wheelbarrows. **The tree was not failing. It was
   screaming.**
4. **15 November 1923, Berlin — the root in the ground.** They killed it and rooted a
   new one in **land**: the Rentenmark, backed by 3.2bn of mortgaged German farms and
   factories. Inflation collapsed in weeks. **Germany accidentally proved the
   story's thesis** — back money with what a country can *make*, not with metal, and
   it holds.
5. **1930–33, Berlin — burn two, the tree forbidden to drop.** Austerity. Six million
   unemployed. The tree standing in full fruit, ordered not to shake. Nazi vote
   2.63% → 37.27%. *(Already canon, and the fascism brief's guardrails are binding
   here — the votes came from the deflation, not the inflation.)*
6. **1934–38, Berlin — the fake tree.** Schacht doesn't use the Reichsbank tree; he
   builds a **counterfeit** one. **Mefo bills**: a letterbox company with no
   employees and no operations, ~12 billion marks conjured off the books, invisible
   to the budget, to Parliament, and to the Treaty of Versailles. Explicitly designed
   to be secret. Rearmament, paid for with money that was never admitted to exist.

**And that is the point of the whole history lesson:**

> The bombs falling on London while Keynes sits on that bench were bought with
> imaginary money by a man running a fake company.

The enemy already knows how to do this. They did it first and they did it in the
dark. Which is precisely why Keynes has to do it **in the light** — a published
pamphlet (*How to Pay for the War*, 1940), an argument in *The Times*, a broadcast on
the BBC asking the country to lend him its wages.

Same instrument. Same physics. Opposite ethics. **The tree is morally neutral; the
sunlight is the whole difference** — and the tree can say that in one line.

## 5. 1971 — the chain, not the planting

**Correction to prior canon.** The old spine said that after Nixon *"they planted them
everywhere."* Given 1694, that's incoherent — the grove is three centuries older than
Bretton Woods.

The true version is better: **the trees were always there, and they were chained.**
Gold was a collar — an arbitrary constraint imposed from outside, which had the
convenient side effect of doing everyone's thinking for them. On 15 August 1971 Nixon
took the last chain off.

So the grove reveal at the '71 stop is not a montage of planting. It is a montage of
**collars coming off**, worldwide, simultaneously — every tree on earth suddenly free
to fruit as much as it is asked for, in the hands of a profession that had never once
established what the real limit was, because gold had been standing in for it.

They spent the next fifty years finding out. Badly.

## 6. Rules for anyone writing this

- **The tree is still a sage, not a gag.** The grove is *mechanism and family*, not
  comedy. The tree gets one or two dry jokes about being a tree, total.
- **Never a face. Never roots that walk.** Documentary realism holds
  ([`characters/the-tree.md`](./characters/the-tree.md)). Performance is wind, light,
  leaf-fall, and going bare out of season.
- **The Garden Court mulberries are shown, never voiced.** They're too young to have
  anything to say, and that's the point. If we ever see them, it's footage of a
  courtyard.
- **The clerk stays unnamed and never appears in dialogue.** He is a thing the tree
  remembers, in one sentence.
- **Don't explain the mechanism twice.** It lands once, in beat 3, from the tree, in
  under a minute. After that it's assumed.
- **Hollow fruit ≠ frozen money.** Two distinct failures (§1). Muddling them hands
  the story's argument to the other side.
- **The literal "paper comes from a tree" claim is false for modern notes** and the
  tree should be the one to say so — cotton until 2016, polypropylene since. Only the
  14th-century Chinese note licenses the literal version. See the brief's *Never say*.
- **Keep 1923, 1930–33 and 1934–38 as three separate episodes, in order.** Collapsing
  them into "German hyperinflation caused Hitler" is the single most common version of
  this story and it is wrong — and our own fascism brief already commits us to the
  correct one.
