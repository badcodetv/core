# Money For Something — the game show (working title)

**Status: an idea of Jack's, 2 October 2026. Nothing here is ruled, and Kai has not seen it.**
It is a new way of telling [The Magic Money Tree](./README.md). It does not replace the direction
agreed on 1 October ([`direction-2026-10-01.md`](./direction-2026-10-01.md)) until both of you say
so. Where the two disagree is listed under "Open calls".

## The idea in one breath

A rigged quiz show from the future. The host has run it before and humanity lost every time. A
presenter points at real clips and narrates over them. Panellists who were there give their own
accounts. The rules change depending on who is asking for money.

## What Jack asked for

- **A game show**, first called "Why We're Fucked".
- **A presenter who points at clips and narrates them**, the way Mock the Week did with the sound
  off on politicians.
- **The tone of Frankie Boyle's New World Order:** dark, funny, historical, economic, satirical.
- **Done artfully, not another panel show.** The reference is the YouTube channel and podcast
  **Fin vs History** (Fin Taylor and Horatio Gould): "for people who like history but don't care
  what actually happened".
- **Clickbait titles** so that it gets views. The reference is Steven Bartlett's Diary of a CEO.
- **A First World War panel**, interviewed: a German soldier, an English soldier, then an English
  politician, each giving a satirical account. Jack's seed line: "One minute I was mining coal, the
  next my arm flew off. Morphine is hard to kick, I tell you."
- **A speech like the Keith David narration in Community's pillow-fight episode.**

## The First World War panel (first sketch, to be argued with)

**The joke:** the two soldiers tell the same story from opposite sides. The politician tells a
different one. The host only has to point at the gap.

**The English soldier**, a miner:

- "One minute I was down a hole in Barnsley. Next minute I was down a hole in France. Same mud,
  more Germans."
- "They said it'd be over by Christmas. They never said which one."
- "My arm went that way and I went this way. The morphine was lovely. Bugger to give up, mind."

**The German soldier**, also a miner:

- "I was also a miner. I was also in a hole. For four years I shot at a man called Tommy."
- "I have since learned that Tommy was a miner. We could have just had a strike."

**Both, asked why it started:**

- English: "A duke got shot, somewhere I can't spell."
- German: "We were told the English started it."
- English: "We were told you did."

**The English politician:**

- "A terrible sacrifice. I felt it very keenly, from Whitehall."
- "The money? We found it in an afternoon. It's extraordinary what one can afford when it matters."
- Host: "And the homes you promised them?" Politician: "Ah. No. There was no money for that."

**Facts behind the politician, and how sure we are:**

- The 1914 War Loan raised under a third of its £350m target and the Bank of England secretly
  covered the rest. Sourced: [Bank Underground](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2017/08/08/your-country-needs-funds-the-extraordinary-story-of-britains-early-efforts-to-finance-the-first-world-war/).
- "Homes fit for heroes", and the housing programme being cut in the early 1920s. **From memory,
  not checked.** Check the exact Lloyd George wording and the dates before any of it is scripted.

**Reader rule:** the soldiers are the heroes and the politician is the target. Neither soldier is
ever the butt of the joke ([`the-reader.md`](../../marketing/the-reader.md), rules 1 and 2).

## The Community reference

- **Episode:** "Pillows and Blankets", season 3, episode 14, first shown 5 April 2012.
- **What it is:** a spoof of Ken Burns' documentary *The Civil War*. A deep, solemn narrator
  (Keith David) treats a campus pillow fight as a great war, with stills, maps, interviews, and
  text messages read out as soldiers' letters home.
- **It spoofs the American Civil War, not the First World War.**
- **The full narration was not found.** The transcript page refused the request (HTTP 402) and
  IMDb's quote page refused too (HTTP 403).
- **Short lines confirmed from a search summary:** "Just after dawn, the end of the war begins."
  And: "just because something is in black and white doesn't mean it's good."
- **The trick to borrow:** total solemnity about something stupid. The narrator never laughs, and
  admits when he does not understand it either ("I don't get it either").

## Rounds floated so far

1. **What Happened Next?** Freeze Theresa May on "no magic money tree" (2 June 2017). The answer:
   £1bn for the DUP, 24 days later.
2. **Sound Off.** Muted politicians, dubbed in the host's own voice.
3. **Shake, Starve or Plant?** Three buttons.
4. **Higher or Lower.** Debt against the size of the economy, 1948 against 2010.
5. **Can We Afford It?** War: yes. Banks: yes. Nurse: buzzer. Nobody explains the rules.
6. **The Price Is Right.** Guess the bank support: £1,162bn at its peak, mostly guarantees (£133bn
   in cash).
7. **Who Got Paid?** £20m to slave owners in 1833.
8. **Final round.** The chair is empty.

## The look — a real 1970s game show (ideas, 2 October 2026)

Jack: "a realistic show, but with a 70s style over it, maybe the aspect ratio of the time."
Nothing here is chosen. No prompt has been written; that goes through `shot-craft` first.

**The medium (this is what makes it read as 1970s, not a style word):**

- 4:3 frame. Flow stills at 4:3 are confirmed; **4:3 video is not checked**, and the fallback is to
  crop to 4:3 in the edit.
- Studio scenes look like videotape from tube cameras: bright highlights leave a smeared tail
  behind them, and colour is soft. Sourced (see links in the chat of 2 October).
- Real archive plays as "the film insert", which is how 1970s television cut from studio to
  outside footage. **From memory, not checked.**
- The palette of the time: velvet curtains, drab orange, brown and beige. Sourced.

**The set:**

- An end-of-pier game-show studio, with one thing wrong: there is no audience, and the APPLAUSE
  sign still lights up.
- The Mastermind chair. Its real origin is the creator's wartime interrogation as a prisoner of
  war: a light in the face, and "name, rank and number" became "name, occupation, specialist
  subject". Sourced (Wikipedia). A soldier in that chair is the joke already made.
- A mechanical scoreboard. The politician's rolls over; the soldiers' stays on nil.
- A prize conveyor belt carrying a house, a hospital and a cuddly toy past the soldiers.
  The conveyor belt is from memory of The Generation Game, not checked.

**The cast:**

- **English soldier:** 1914 khaki, trench mud still wet under studio lights, one sleeve pinned up.
  Name card: miner, Barnsley.
- **German soldier:** field grey, the same mud. Name card: miner, the Ruhr.
- **Politician:** frock coat, spotless, a cushion and a drink. His buzzer is the only one wired in.
- **Host, three options:** a velvet-jacketed compère who never blinks; a voice with no body; or
  the red light on the studio camera, which is the BadCode logo (a ring with a red dot).

**Funny things the format lets us do:**

- A tape fault every time the politician lies.
- A "normal service will be resumed" card when a question gets too close.
- The politician badly cut out against battlefield footage, with a coloured fringe round him.
- The frame widens from 4:3 only when money is found.
- A channel ident and a continuity announcer: "and now on BadCode Television".

**Lessons from the shows that did this well:**

- Look Around You used real period cameras and effects. Garth Marenghi's Darkplace used bad
  lighting, clumsy cuts and obvious green screen, all on purpose. Both play it completely straight.
- Our own finding from 1 October: a style word changes only the colour. Describe the medium as a
  physical thing (the camera, the lamps, the tape).
- A game-show set is symmetrical and evenly lit, which is the "looks like AI" bundle in
  `docs/cinematography/symptoms.md`. Each frame needs one thing breaking it: mud on the carpet,
  a dead bulb, an empty seat.
