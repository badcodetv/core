# The Magic Money Tree — The Future He Never Saw

> **The single canonical storyboard for the Magic Money Tree documentary.** Chosen by Kai on
> 11 September 2026. This replaces the talking-tree fable, the century-long debt-ledger treatment,
> their alternative endings and the earlier comic narrative as sources for new video work.
> [Superseded treatments are archived](./archive/2026-09-11-superseded/README.md).
>
> This is a detailed **story plan with a continuous working narration**, not a shot list or a
> finished recording script. Scene order, focus and ending are the current creative decision;
> individual sentences remain editable. No images, voices or footage have been generated or cleared
> by writing this document.

## 1. The film we are making

**Logline:** Britain rescues an army at Dunkirk, then faces a harder question about the peace:
what should people come home to? Keynes helps challenge the economics of enforced idleness, but
dies before the NHS opens. The country builds a future he never sees—and later calls the work
needed to sustain it unaffordable.

The title refers specifically to the NHS's opening and the subsequent reconstruction, not to a
claim that Keynes saw none of the post-war settlement. He lived through victory in 1945 and worked
on arrangements for the peace. This is **a story of collective achievement threaded through one
unfinished human life**, not a biography claiming that one genius invented the NHS.

**The audience question:** How did Britain emerge from war with national debt around two and a
half times annual output, yet begin universal healthcare and rebuild homes? What does that history
allow us to ask when useful work is ruled out as unaffordable?

**The emotional movement:** rescue → an uncertain homecoming → possibility → a public commitment
→ loss → achievement → a broken promise → a practical question about the future.

The opening question is **“What are they coming home to?”** The ending answers it with an ordinary
person able to receive treatment and return home. The repeated human action is one person helping
another to safety. That is an editorial rhyme, not a claim that military evacuation and healthcare
are economically identical.

### What this story is really arguing

Writer's controlling idea, not a speech for the narrator: **when democratic government treats
useful work and shared security as responsibilities, it can organise finance and real resources
to extend them; treating financial restraint as the whole objective can leave those capacities
unbuilt or neglected.**

The persuasive opposing case gets real time: Britain was poor in things it needed, heavily indebted,
dependent on imports and unable to produce skilled workers by decree. Tax, borrowing, interest,
inflation and competing uses of resources mattered. They still do. The film wins only if it shows
people making choices *within* those difficulties, not making the difficulties disappear.

The distinction to carry into the conversation afterwards:

> **Debt was a burden. It wasn't a veto.**

This is not a promise that today's feasible budget can be read from a 1945 debt ratio. It is a
challenge to treating that ratio, or a household analogy, as the end of a public decision.

### The people and the thing at stake

- **The collective protagonist:** people trying to get through the war and build a decent civilian
  life; later, the nurse trying to keep doing the work that promise requires. They have practical
  knowledge, not a deficiency the narrator needs to correct.
- **The human thread:** Keynes, a contributor to the intellectual and financial work, whose life
  ends before one of the settlement's defining institutions begins. His absence changes who carries
  the story; no ghost returns to certify the argument.
- **The choosers:** the wartime coalition accepting an employment responsibility; voters electing
  Labour in 1945; Attlee's government and Bevan establishing the NHS; councils commissioning homes;
  the later Coalition choosing pay restraint, which May defends in 2017.
- **The beneficiaries:** people obtaining treatment without a bill at the bedside, families living
  in council homes, and workers whose abilities become useful paid work. Nobody needs to be
  secretly enriched for this film's particular argument to work.
- **The concrete transaction:** an NHS nurse's paid working time becoming someone's care. A building
  and an announcement are insufficient; the shift needs staff, wages, training, supplies and continued
  funding. We do not invent a historical shift price. The pay-restraint sequence supplies the
  documented change in that transaction, rather than a made-up household or payslip.

### Scope and length

Make one complete short film, provisionally **about nine minutes**. The scene allowances below total
9:15 and are planning estimates, not locked timecodes or claims about audience retention. Read the
working narration aloud before assigning final durations. Shorten repetition before accelerating
delivery; protect the quiet moments and the economic qualifications that make the argument honest.

The story spends most of its time between 1940 and the post-war rebuilding. The 2010–17 section is
the pressure test of what the audience has just seen, not a second documentary about everything
since the financial crisis. There is one current scene order and one ending below.

## 2. How it should feel

An intelligent, occasionally funny talk that keeps finding human consequences inside apparently
dry decisions. The BadCode narrator is dry, exact and interested in people surviving their systems.
Its authority comes from distinguishable evidence and argument—not from pretending the fictional
future-AI frame is historical knowledge. No Storyverse explanation is needed to understand this film.

The humour is aimed at procedural absurdity and misplaced certainty. No jokes over evacuation
casualties, Keynes's death, frightened patients or a nurse's distress. Let the opening be sincerely
patriotic. We do not need to wink at viewers who love this history or reveal that their patriotism
was a trap. Allied participation belongs in the rescue from the beginning.

**The cross-party bridge must be history, not flattery.** Churchill's coalition, Labour's delivery,
and Conservative continuation of large-scale council building have distinct parts. They did not
agree on every policy. We can give credit accurately without pretending that the parties were
interchangeable, that every serviceman wanted the NHS, or that the post-war country treated everyone
equally. Patriotism here is an obligation to one another, not an ethnic qualification for care.

**Keep the economic explanation attached to work:** an available builder, a brick, a trained nurse,
an import that must be paid for. One brief hypothetical demonstrates a mechanism and is openly
introduced as hypothetical. It must not masquerade as a discovered historical incident.

Real war footage, documents, original illustrations, AI-generated comics and a narrator can coexist.
Their appearance is deliberately undecided. Archive establishes events; illustration can carry an
analogy or a joke. Generated imagery never supplies counterfeit evidence, testimony or a newly
discovered scene from Keynes's private life. An illustration of the historical Keynes need not speak.

## 3. Scene-by-scene storyboard

Read only the **Working narration** blocks in sequence to hear the current film. Other prose is
for the writer/editor. Narration is original draft copy unless expressly identified as a quotation.
Historical source notes support facts, not our jokes or editorial interpretation.

### Scene 01 — Get them off the beach

**Period / allowance:** Dunkirk, May–June 1940 · 35 seconds.

**Story:** Begin inside an urgent human problem, not with a politician or an economics definition.
An army is trapped. A coordinated evacuation gets hundreds of thousands to safety. The achievement
belongs to people acting together: defending troops, naval organisation, merchant crews and civilian
craft, with Allied participation. Establish that the people we will discuss as workers and patients
are the same kind of people a country already knows how to value.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** escape destruction / advancing German forces and the Channel / a military
defeat contains a rescue. Survival makes a future possible; it does not yet provide one.

**Working narration:**

> Dunkirk. 1940.
>
> More than 338,000 British and Allied troops evacuated from the beaches and harbour.
>
> Naval ships. Merchant crews. Little boats. People helping people get out alive.
>
> A defeat, and an extraordinary rescue.
>
> Britain remembers the getting-home part.
>
> This is about what came after.

**Into the next scene:** getting someone safely across the Channel leaves the question of the
country they will return to when the war is finally over.

**Evidence / restraint:** Operation Dynamo ran 26 May–4 June 1940. Do not imply that little ships
alone evacuated the army, that everyone escaped, or that Dunkirk ended the war. The film's “home”
question is our framing, not testimony attributed to every person in the archive. [RAF Museum
timeline](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/research-enquiries/history-of-aviation-timeline/british-military-aviation/1940-2/);
[IWM's Dunkirk memorial record](https://memorials.iwm.org.uk/memorial/71491).

**Only necessary material note:** genuine Dunkirk footage is the preferred opening. No scenes from
a feature film passed off as actuality; no generated casualty close-ups. Existing candidates are
listed in the [research footage ledger](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#archive-footage-candidates-retained-from-the-prior-sweep).

### Scene 02 — Home to what?

**Period / allowance:** the coming peace, previewed from 1940 · 40 seconds.

**Story:** Establish the film's apparent contradiction early. Britain will win, but victory will
not settle the housing question or abolish illness. Preview the eventual NHS and the huge war debt,
then introduce the man who will not live to see its doors open. The title arrives as a promise about
an absence, not as a surprise death to spring later.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** a worthwhile peace / destruction and financial strain / the country
undertakes something that the opening balance sheet seems to forbid.

**Working narration:**

> When the war ends, there will still be homes to rebuild, work to find, people getting ill.
>
> Britain's national debt will be roughly two and a half times a year's economic output.
>
> Yet in 1948 it will launch the NHS.
>
> How?
>
> One economist helped change the argument. He wouldn't live to see the service open.
>
> John Maynard Keynes. This is the future he never saw.

**Into the next scene:** the audience knows the outcome; the mystery is how a country makes such
a choice possible, and what Keynes contributed.

**Evidence / restraint:** use **“about 250% of GDP at the end of the war”**, with 249% and the OBR
source in the eventual source caption. Do not date that number to 5 July 1948, call it the deficit,
or suggest that all the debt was due immediately. [OBR, *300 years of public finance data*](https://obr.uk/docs/dlm_uploads/300-Years-of-public-finances-Accessible-PDF.pdf).

### Scene 03 — The unemployed builder and the unbuilt house

**Period / allowance:** the interwar problem informing Keynes's work · 40 seconds.

**Story:** Introduce Keynes through a contradiction anyone can understand, not through his education
or a list of famous books. Someone can build; someone needs a home; nothing happens. Explain why
need does not automatically become a paying order. This is the first deliberately funny piece of
economic explanation, and it must remain recognisable as an example.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** useful work and a decent home / insufficient spending to employ available
capacity / unemployment becomes a problem that policy can act upon, not a natural verdict on workers.

**Working narration:**

> Start with a puzzle. An unemployed builder. A family needing a house.
>
> For this example, the materials are available too.
>
> The builder can build. The family needs a home. But need isn't a paying order.
>
> Keynes challenged the idea that an economy would reliably put everyone to work by itself.
>
> When private spending falls short, government can commission useful work.
>
> The builder hasn't suddenly acquired moral character. He's acquired a customer.

**Into the next scene:** the obvious objection is now unavoidable: does creating the paying order
solve everything? The film invites that objection before a hostile viewer has to supply it.

**Evidence / restraint:** this is a simplified illustration of deficient demand, not a particular
builder's biography, a description of all unemployment or a claim that post-war Britain had spare
capacity everywhere. Keynes's discussion explicitly couples sufficient demand with supply limits;
see [the existing quotation/context research](./research/economics-of-money-creation.md#1-keynes-anything-we-can-actually-do-we-can-afford).

### Scene 04 — The word “actually”

**Period / allowance:** Keynes's wartime argument · 45 seconds.

**Story:** Let the resource objection win against the easy version of our own argument. The house
needs people, materials and time. Government can employ people directly and commission work through
others; this isn't a choice between unemployment and everybody becoming a civil servant. Spending
can activate idle capacity, while training and investment can build missing capacity. Neither is
instantaneous, and pushing demand into a bottleneck can raise prices.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** turn an idea into useful output / real scarcity / the seemingly magical
claim becomes a demanding practical programme.

**Working narration:**

> That doesn't mean ordering a million houses makes them appear.
>
> If the bricks are missing, make bricks. If the skills are missing, train people. Those things
> take resources and time too.
>
> If everyone is already busy, more spending can bid up prices instead of building more homes.
>
> Keynes put it like this: “Anything we can actually do we can afford.”
>
> “Actually” is carrying quite a lot of the sentence.

**Into the next scene:** war puts this distinction under the harshest possible test: mobilising
money is necessary, but it cannot eliminate the sacrifices required to mobilise things.

**Evidence / quotation status:** the ten-word Keynes quotation comes from “How Much Does Finance
Matter?”, printed in *The Listener*, **2 April 1942**, reprinted in *Collected Writings*, XXVII,
pp. 264–71. The earlier investigation verified the wording through quoted reproductions, not a
page-inspected primary edition. [Publisher's volume index](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811076/51562/index/9781107651562_index.pdf)
confirms the essay's placement; [a university-hosted facsimile lead](https://gandalf.fee.urv.cat/professors/AntonioQuesada/Curs2324/Keynes_How_Much_Does_Finance_Matter.pdf)
was found but could not be retrieved in this pass. **Collate the page before recording the quotation.**
Do not invent a precise BBC broadcast date. If the quotation cannot be collated, the surrounding
plain-language explanation carries the scene without an attributed quotation.

**Only necessary material note:** read the quotation as an attributed quotation in the narrator's
voice. No fake archival recording or invented Keynes conversation is required.

### Scene 05 — Britain did pay for the war

**Period / allowance:** 1940–45 · 40 seconds.

**Story:** The state has already demonstrated immense coordinating capacity, but the evidence is
not a printer gag. People worked, paid taxes, saved, borrowed, rationed and went without. Imports
and overseas assistance mattered. Keynes worked on war finance inside that real struggle. This
scene earns our right to call financial policy a choice by showing the costs of that choice.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** survive the war / finite production and imported supplies / the country
mobilises resources at enormous cost. The achievement is organisation, not exemption from economics.

**Working narration:**

> Britain didn't fight the war by discovering a cupboard of free money.
>
> It taxed. Borrowed. Directed production. Rationed goods. Relied on overseas supplies and support.
>
> People paid in work, in foregone comforts, and in losses no budget can measure.
>
> Keynes worked on the finances. Money had to be organised around what the country needed—and
> what it could actually produce.
>
> Winning would leave another job: organising the peace.

**Into the next scene:** demobilisation cannot mean allowing useful people to become surplus to
requirements. The wartime commitment to employment supplies a documented bridge.

**Evidence / restraint:** Keynes's *How to Pay for the War* argues about restraining excess civilian
purchasing power, not free wartime spending. His proposals and the government's eventual measures
must not be treated as identical. [Keynes's 1940 text, Federal Reserve archival copy](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/pay-war-6021/fulltext);
[Bank of England's wartime history](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/archive/bank-of-england-1939-45-ww2).
War destroyed lives and wealth; it is not the film's recommended economic stimulus.

### Scene 06 — A job after the uniform

**Period / allowance:** wartime planning, 1942–44 · 40 seconds.

**Story:** Keynes's insight meets public responsibility. The 1944 Employment Policy White Paper
provides an actual promise about work after the war. The coalition also develops proposals for a
national health service. Different people supply different parts: economic thinking, social-insurance
proposals, legislation and administration. Do not turn this into four miniature biographies.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** civilian security after service / the danger of a return to unemployment
and fragmented provision / employment and health become declared government responsibilities.

**Working narration:**

> Before victory, Churchill's coalition accepted responsibility for maintaining “a high and stable
> level of employment” after the war.
>
> Not just finding people something to do in uniform. Making civilian work a public responsibility.
>
> Beveridge's proposals and plans for a national health service were part of the argument about
> the peace too.
>
> Keynes wasn't building this future alone.
>
> And a promise on paper still needed a government to carry it out.

**Into the next scene:** the audience now needs to see democratic agency doing something concrete:
the electorate chooses a government with a reconstruction programme.

**Evidence / restraint:** [Employment Policy debate, 21 June 1944](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1944/jun/21/employment-policy)
records Ernest **Bevin**, Minister of Labour, presenting the commitment under Churchill's coalition.
Do not confuse him with Aneurin **Bevan**, the later Health Minister. The commitment is to high and
stable employment—not a statutory individual job guarantee or evidence that zero unemployment was
achieved. [Beveridge and the coalition's 1944 health proposals, TNA](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/the-foundation-of-the-nhs/).

### Scene 07 — Victory does not come with a roof

**Period / allowance:** 1945 and the reconstruction task · 50 seconds.

**Story:** Victory is real. So are the bomb damage, housing shortage and obligations. Voters elect
Attlee's Labour government. Make the burden credible and the decision legible: the government must
decide how to use resources released from war production, maintain finance and pay for imports.
The debt figure returns now with an answer, not simply a bigger font.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** convert victory into a liveable peace / debt and shortages / voters
authorise a programme, and government begins reallocating effort to civilian needs.

**Working narration:**

> In 1945 Britain wins. Voters elect Attlee's Labour government.
>
> The celebration does not come with a roof.
>
> Debt is enormous. Supplies are short. Rationing continues.
>
> But the war's end releases people and production for civilian work. Taxes and borrowing
> remain part of the answer; so do external finance and the difficult business of paying for imports.
>
> Keynes negotiates an American loan, then defends it in Parliament. Still finding time to tell
> a fellow peer: “I have never heard statistics so funny.”
>
> The country doesn't wait to clear its entire debt before rebuilding.
>
> Debt is a burden. It isn't a veto.

**Into the next scene:** the programme is under way, but the man who helped make its economic
possibility intelligible will not accompany us to its most recognisable achievement.

**Evidence / restraint:** the peace dividend is genuine, not an implication that every soldier can
become a doctor or every munitions plant a hospital. Post-war limits included skilled labour, housing
materials and foreign exchange. [OBR's warfare-to-welfare account](https://obr.uk/docs/dlm_uploads/300-Years-of-public-finances-Accessible-PDF.pdf);
[Keynes's explanation of the Anglo-American financial arrangements, 18 December 1945](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1945/dec/18/anglo-american-financial-arrangements).
The short statistics quotation is Keynes's documented reply to Lord Beaverbrook in that debate,
not invented dialogue. It gives him a moment of his own argumentative humour before the handover;
do not portray the loan terms as painless or wholly of his choosing. Do not say recovery was wholly
financed domestically or that the debt simply vanished through growth.

### Scene 08 — 21 April 1946

**Period / allowance:** Keynes's death · 25 seconds, including room for silence.

**Story:** Pay the title's promise plainly. Do not fabricate a deathbed, last thought, exhausted
speech or family reaction. We have followed a living person's work into the peace; now that life
ends. His words can remain in the film, but he no longer gets to be its actor.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** see the work become a lived future / death / the story passes from
an economist's contribution to the collective work that will outlast him.

**Working narration:**

> Keynes died on 21 April 1946.
>
> The NHS would open more than two years later.
>
> He had seen victory. He would not see this.
>
> We don't know what he would have made of everything that followed.
>
> Other people had the work to do.

**Pause:** let the absence register without commentary filling it. This is not yet the film's final
warmth; the delivery stays restrained. We do not return later to a grieving ghost.

**Into the next scene:** an idea surviving its author is not enough. Someone must negotiate,
legislate, staff and operate the service.

**Evidence / restraint:** [King's College's Keynes biography](https://www.kings.cam.ac.uk/biographies)
records his wartime Treasury work and death. Do not say he “died saving the NHS”, that overwork
caused his death, or that we know his judgement of modern policy. The title is about what he did
not live to witness; it is not a claim to speak for him.

### Scene 09 — The promise gets a working day

**Period / allowance:** implementation, then 5 July 1948 · 50 seconds.

**Story:** This is the achievement, and it needs more than a date. Attlee's government and Bevan
turn policy into an institution through legislation, negotiation and practical organisation. Existing
health services are brought into a new publicly funded settlement. People do the work. Our concrete
transaction arrives: care given because it is needed, without charging the patient at the point of
use, with staff and suppliers still paid.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** treatment available by need / fragmented provision, professional
opposition and limited capacity / universal entitlement becomes something people can actually use.

**Working narration:**

> Attlee's government, with Aneurin Bevan as Health Minister, established the NHS.
>
> It took legislation, negotiation and people turning up to work.
>
> On 5 July 1948, existing hospitals and services became part of a new settlement: care available
> to everyone, free at the point of use.
>
> Not free to provide. The staff and suppliers still had to be paid.
>
> Public funding changed who faced the bill.
>
> A nurse could get on with the work. A patient could get on with getting better.

**Let the benefit breathe:** do not rush straight from opening day to modern failure. The viewer
needs to experience this as a genuine achievement, not a rhetorical stepping stone. An ordinary
act of care is sufficient; there is no need to invent a named first patient or a cure.

**Into the next scene:** a service is one answer to the homecoming question. Housing makes the
same constructive ambition tangible beyond the hospital and beyond a single party.

**Evidence / restraint:** [NHS launch leaflet, TNA INF 2/66](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/significant-events/birth-of-the-nhs-1948/)
states access and public funding. [TNA's NHS records guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-topic/health-and-welfare/nhs-posters-and-leaflets/)
explains the consolidation of existing provision. Do not imply that all hospitals were newly built,
that no healthcare existed before 1948, that every provider became a salaried state employee, or that
launch removed waiting, shortages or discrimination.

### Scene 10 — The key in the door

**Period / allowance:** post-war council building, extending into the 1950s · 35 seconds.

**Story:** Give rebuilding a domestic consequence. A completed council house is not an argument
about GDP; someone can shut the door and live there. The work is real, sustained and crosses a change
of government. Conservative continuation expands ownership of the achievement without erasing
Labour's decisions or the remaining housing shortage.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** a stable home / a building programme that takes labour and years /
policy becomes homes, and the ambition proves larger than one party's term in office.

**Working narration:**

> Councils commissioned homes. Builders built them. Families moved in.
>
> Not everyone got a home. Not overnight.
>
> But the building continued. Under the Conservatives, Harold Macmillan made housing a major
> priority too.
>
> They disagreed about plenty. Large-scale council building wasn't confined to one party.
>
> A key in a front door is a fairly practical sort of patriotism.

**Into the next scene:** these institutions have to keep employing people and maintaining capacity.
Inheritance is not maintenance. Move to the later decision that tests that obligation.

**Evidence / restraint:** [Commons Housing Committee, history of social housebuilding](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5801/cmselect/cmcomloc/173/17305.htm).
No exact building total is needed in the spoken script. If a number is added later, specify place,
period, tenure and whether it counts starts or completions. This is not evidence of unanimous
post-war politics or a claim that the 1950s offered everyone equal security.

### Scene 11 — The promise requires another shift

**Period / allowance:** 2010–17, explicitly dated · 50 seconds.

**Story:** The modern nurse is not a new fable character and does not stand for an idle worker.
She is already delivering the service we have just celebrated. The Coalition's fiscal problem is
real, but its pay policy is a choice. Give the pay freeze/cap accurately, acknowledge the NHS budget's
slow growth rather than claim it was simply cut, then let the nurse's own question interrupt the
language of national restraint. May's answer enters with its context intact.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** keep doing valued work on sustainable terms / prolonged pay restraint /
the public promise meets the person whose paid time sustains it.

**Working narration:**

> In 2010, borrowing was exceptionally high. The Coalition chose a programme of tax rises and
> spending restraint.
>
> The NHS budget grew slowly. Public-sector basic pay was largely frozen for two years from
> 2011, then increases were held around one per cent. Individual pay could vary.
>
> In 2017, nurses challenged Theresa May about pay on Question Time.
>
> She defended the difficult choices, and said there was no magic money tree.
>
> The nurse was already doing the work.

**Human voice:** preserve room for a short, authenticated portion of the nurse's question before
May's response. Receive it without a joke or narration disputing her experience. No exact excerpt
is locked here: the existing transcript gives **00:26:20–00:28:12** as the sequence, but attribution
of the later payslip remark needs visual checking. The working narration is complete without a clip;
do not make the first film dependent on buying footage.

**Into the next scene:** May's warning about magic has a surprisingly close antecedent inside
the NHS's own first year. The answer to the modern phrase is a contemporary historical document,
not a bailout-number montage.

**Evidence / restraint:** [June 2010 Budget](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-june-2010);
[Nuffield Trust's real-pay analysis](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/resource/chart-of-the-week-real-terms-nhs-staff-pay-from-2010-to-2021);
[broadcast-derived Question Time subtitles](https://subsaga.com/bbc/news/question-time/2017/06/02/leaders-special-with-theresa-may-and-jeremy-corbyn.html).
The [completed investigation](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#austerity-the-nurse-and-what-the-economy-could-do)
holds the policy detail and primary pay-report references. Keep the date visible: this is not a
claim that a 2017 freeze is the current 2026 settlement. Do not allege secret intent or put the
payslip statement into the wrong nurse's mouth.

### Scene 12 — They knew about the magic

**Period / allowance:** return to September 1948, then the present question · 40 seconds.

**Story:** The first-year NHS pamphlet supplies the reversal. Its authors explicitly warned the
public that launching a service did not instantly create buildings or skilled staff. This is not an
embarrassing qualification bolted onto our case: the builders of the institution already understood
it. Distinguish today's staffing/pay dispute from 1948 without implying that the warning proves a
particular pay award affordable.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** answer the strongest objection / neither money nor legislation can
conjure capacity / the historical achievement includes its own realistic warning.

**Working narration:**

> Here's the awkward bit. In 1948, an NHS pamphlet warned that “no fairy wand was waved on July 5th”.
>
> New hospitals, doctors and nurses had not appeared overnight.
>
> They knew. They established the service anyway—and had to keep finding the people and supplies
> to make it work.
>
> The absence of magic wasn't the end of the discussion.
>
> It was the beginning of a job list.

**Into the next scene:** bring the audience from “then they could” to “what exactly needs doing
now?” The film must not conclude that repeating 1948's financial arrangements answers that question.

**Evidence / restraint:** the quotation is from a **September 1948** pamphlet, TNA **MH 55/965**,
not the original launch announcement. [TNA transcript and document](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/stories/the-foundation-of-the-nhs/).
There were limits then and there are limits now. The contemporary-policy inference belongs to us:
an honest limit should lead to a plan and a choice, not automatically to either spending or refusal.

### Scene 13 — What is actually missing?

**Period / allowance:** the question carried forward · 40 seconds.

**Story:** Give democratic agency a practical object. A government can commission, employ, train
and maintain; each choice needs people, money, materials, time and scrutiny. No legislative spell
employs everyone instantly. Return to the shift: wages cannot generate a trained nurse overnight,
but pay and working conditions can help keep one. This is a usable question for a voter, not a
full manifesto smuggled into an ending.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** sustain useful work / real shortages plus choices about funding and
priority / an abstract refusal becomes a programme the public can ask decision-makers to justify.

**Working narration:**

> So ask what is actually missing.
>
> Staff? Train and retain them. Buildings? Plan and commission them. Supplies? Secure them.
>
> Then show the costs, the funding, the timetable, and what has to give elsewhere.
>
> Government can create useful jobs. It cannot make every skill or resource appear by wanting it.
>
> Nor does promising a service finish the work.
>
> The nurse still needs to be there tomorrow.

**Into the next scene:** stop talking about the plan and let its purpose resolve the opening.
We began with getting people to safety; we end with what their safety is for.

**Evidence / restraint:** these are editorial questions informed by the historical mechanisms and
the [adversarial economic test](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#hostile-economist-test),
not estimates of today's spare capacity. “Train and retain” can require better pay, conditions,
supervision and years; it does not prove any one pay policy's return. Employment can be supported
through direct hiring, procurement, public investment and stabilising demand; a formal job guarantee
would be a separate proposal with eligibility, administration and inflation questions.

### Scene 14 — A life to get back to

**Period / allowance:** the historical achievement and its continuing human purpose · 25 seconds.

**Story:** Resolve the Keynes thread before resolving the homecoming thread. His absence is no
longer the last word: other people lived and worked in the future he did not see. End on an ordinary
act of help, then someone able to return to their life. No speculative modern hospital opening, no
perfect waiting list, no suggestion that an economic explanation has already fixed anything.

**Working narration:**

> Keynes never saw the NHS open.
>
> Other people did. And every day after that, other people made it work.
>
> We began with getting people home.
>
> A country worth coming home to still needs building.
>
> [Quieter.] There should be a life to get back to.

**Last beat:** the act of care resolves; the person has somewhere beyond the hospital to go. Allow
a wordless beat. This can be an explicitly illustrative action, not a falsely identified historical
patient. The film ends here. Credits/sources may follow; no extra thesis, politician's final word,
coin promotion, dead-tree reveal, fascism forecast or obligatory full song reverses this ending.

**What the audience carries away:** creating public provision was neither free nor impossible.
People chose it, organised it and kept doing the work. The future remains a public responsibility,
not a verdict already delivered by a number.

## 4. Why this order holds together

The opening rescue creates a future, **but** not a worthwhile peace. Keynes offers an explanation
of unused capacity, **but** money alone cannot supply what is missing. War demonstrates coordination
**and its costs**, **therefore** peace requires a deliberate transition rather than mere demobilisation.
The coalition makes commitments and voters elect a rebuilding government, **but** Keynes dies before
the service opens. **Therefore** other people's implementation becomes the answer to his absence.
Care and homes become real, **but** achievements need continued work and resources. The later pay
dispute exposes that dependence. The 1948 pamphlet then complicates the modern slogan: the people
who founded the NHS understood the limits too. **Therefore** the useful democratic question is what
is missing, what it would take to supply it, and who will commit to doing so.

The **central reversal** is scenes 07–09: apparent national victory and an authorised programme
meet personal loss; the story then shows collective achievement outliving the individual. The
**second reversal**, scenes 11–12, prevents a simple golden-age lament: the old government had no
magic wand either. Reality is not a surprise that only modern politicians discovered.

The builder example plants capacity; war tests it; the service makes it concrete; the modern nurse
shows why capacity needs maintenance. Debt is introduced once and answered once. Keynes's absence
is promised early, felt halfway through and resolved before the final homecoming. No planted
metaphor needs a second film to pay it off.

## 5. Economic and historical boundaries of the canonical argument

These are writing constraints, not an additional narrated appendix.

| Tempting shorthand | What this film actually says |
| --- | --- |
| “We built the NHS at 250% debt.” | Debt was about 249% of GDP at the war's end; the NHS opened in July 1948 while Britain still carried huge war debts. The dated measure is not an exact launch-day ratio. |
| “Keynes's genius rebuilt Britain.” | Keynes made important intellectual and practical contributions. Coalition planning, Beveridge, voters, Attlee, Bevan, councils, workers and overseas resources had distinct roles. This is not a clean causal experiment proving one school of economics. |
| “We built hospitals from nothing.” | The founding act reorganised and publicly funded much existing provision; new building and expanded staffing were continuing tasks. |
| “Everyone came home to a house and a job.” | Employment became a policy responsibility and large-scale housing was delivered over years. Shortages, exclusions and unequal treatment persisted. |
| “The war proved money is unlimited.” | War finance combined instruments and real sacrifices. Civilian provision required a peace dividend, taxes, borrowing, imports and priorities—not simply printing. |
| “Just spend on something productive and it pays for itself.” | Useful public work can raise capacity and wellbeing. Projects can also fail, run late or require continuing net funding. Care is valuable without a guaranteed fiscal profit. |
| “Willpower means everybody can have a government job.” | Government can directly create useful jobs and support high employment throughout the economy. Skills, geography, health, matching, supplies and administration prevent a promise of instantaneous universal placement. |
| “Taxes don't matter because government creates money.” | This film does not make that claim. Taxes and borrowing matter to funding, distribution and the management of demand; monetary institutions and credibility matter too. |
| “Money belongs to us, so we can vote away any limit.” | Democratic institutions can set public purposes, authorise spending and reshape rules. They cannot vote labour, energy or imports into existence. Much money is created by commercial-bank lending; citizens do not directly vote every loan into being. |
| “The debt was never repaid; growth solved everything.” | Individual debts were serviced, refinanced and repaid; debt/GDP fell over decades through several interacting forces. No pure-growth miracle is claimed. |
| “Austerity was entirely imaginary.” | The fiscal problem was real. The pace, composition and distribution of consolidation were political choices. The NHS budget grew slowly; it was not simply cut across the period. |
| “May's nurse proves a pay rise has no opportunity cost.” | She demonstrates that the existing service depends on paid skilled work. Her pay can affect retention and recruitment, but the level, funding and effects of an award need assessment. |
| “2010–17 tells us exactly what is affordable in 2026.” | It supplies a historical case about priorities. This storyboard offers no current fiscal-headroom estimate and no current pay-policy claim. |

Money is a set of institutional claims and arrangements with real consequences. “Money is something
we control in a democracy” is best made legible here through an election, authorised spending, actual
employment and public accountability. It does not require a false claim that the Treasury has a
costless, unconstrained printing button, or that changing banking law alone builds a house.

## 7. Story-first route to a first cut

This is the next workflow, not a second treatment or a request to invent the film again.

1. **Read this narration as one talk.** Time a scratch read, include the intended pauses, and cut
   anything that sounds like a qualification repeated rather than a story advancing. Keep the
   resource objection, the collective handover, the achieved benefit and the modern worker.
2. **Make a text-first assembly.** One numbered scene card per section above and the scratch voice
   are enough to test comprehension. No aesthetic system, detailed prompts or finished AI assets
   are prerequisites. Test whether the film still holds a listener without attractive images.
3. **Resolve the few evidential dependencies.** Collate the 1942 quotation; authenticate the chosen
   Question Time words and speakers; identify genuinely relevant Dunkirk material. Do not let an
   attractive but unidentified clip rewrite the history.
4. **Gather the smallest useful set of material.** Start with Dunkirk, wartime work/rationing,
   a verified Keynes image, the 1944 commitment, the NHS launch leaflet and resource warning,
   post-war homes, and the dated pay-policy evidence. Cleared real footage can ground these;
   original illustration can carry the builder example and the comic commentary. Detailed footage
   candidates and rights cautions already exist in the research.
5. **Test the argument with listeners who do not share all our politics.** Ask what happened,
   what Keynes contributed, what the country paid, and what the ending permits us to ask. If the
   answer is “print money and everything is free”, revise the causal passages, not just the captions.
   If the answer is “the Tories are stupid”, revise the treatment of choice and the shared history.
6. **Then design and make the scenes.** Derive any later shot list or asset records from these scene
   IDs. They record implementation, not alternative canon. Do not generate fake archival testimony.

**Completion threshold for the first film:** one understandable story with attributable facts,
an audible human stake, an honest resource constraint and an earned ending. We do not need a
complete history of money, a fictional Keynes performance or a finished companion music video.

**Material/rights status:** no footage is cleared merely because a catalogue or Internet Archive
page calls a compilation public domain. Check the actual excerpt and embedded source/soundtrack.
Use free, reusable documents and original work first; no purchase is assumed. Rights clearance is
a production task, not a claim made by this storyboard. Existing [rights findings and fallbacks](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#footage-and-rights-ledger)
remain relevant, especially the difference between Parliamentary text and Parliamentary video.

nor inflated into sole authorship. The payoff belongs to living people, not the narrator's superiority.
