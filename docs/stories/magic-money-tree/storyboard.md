# The Magic Money Tree — The Future He Never Saw

> **The single canonical storyboard for the Magic Money Tree documentary.** Chosen by Kai on
> 11 September 2026; revised 20 September 2026 (Germany 1923, the banking crisis, Covid); revised
> again on **28 September 2026** after Kai agreed the film's point and ruled its ending. That
> revision restores the three verbs (**shake, starve, plant**), arranges the scenes into five
> movements, names who paid and who got paid, replaces the closing question with a statement and
> adds a short coda. The 20 September text is
> [archived verbatim](./archive/2026-09-28-superseded/storyboard-2026-09-20.md); earlier treatments
> are in the [11 September archive](./archive/2026-09-11-superseded/README.md).
>
> This is a detailed **story plan with a continuous working narration**, not a shot list or a
> finished recording script. Scene order, focus and ending are the current creative decision;
> individual sentences remain editable. No images, voices or footage have been generated or cleared
> by writing this document.

## 1. The film we are making

**Logline:** Britain gets its army home from Dunkirk, then builds a country worth coming home to
while owing more than it ever had. Keynes explains how, and dies before the NHS opens. Almost
seventy years on, a nurse asks about her pay and is told there is no magic money tree, in the same
decade the Bank of England creates hundreds of billions of pounds. The film shows the three things
a country can do with its money: shake it, starve it, or plant it.

The title refers specifically to the NHS's opening and the subsequent reconstruction, not to a
claim that Keynes saw none of the post-war settlement. He lived through victory in 1945 and worked
on arrangements for the peace. This is **a story of collective achievement threaded through one
unfinished human life**, not a biography claiming that one genius invented the NHS.

### The point (agreed by Kai, 28 September 2026)

> **“We can't afford it” is a choice. The real limit is the work, not the money.**

The film must also beat the objection that arrives at once: *pay the nurse, print the money, get
inflation.* Its answer is Keynes's own, from the same 1942 talk as the headline quotation:
inflation is demand beyond what the country can physically supply. Money planted in work that can
actually be done adds to what there is to buy. Money shaken over nothing does not. That is why
Germany in 1923 and Britain in 1948 are in the same film.

- **Writer's theme sentence (never spoken in the film):** a country that plants its money in work
  it can actually do outgrows its debt; a country that shakes it or starves it gets the bill anyway.
- **The counter-idea, which gets real time:** a country is a household, and its debt is a veto.
- **The costed object:** one nurse's payslip, the same in 2017 as it was in 2009.
- **The line for the pub:** *You can print the money. You can't print the nurse. She was already
  there.*

**The emotional movement:** rescue → an uncertain homecoming → how the tool breaks and how it
works → sacrifice → loss → achievement → the debt outgrown → the same country doing it wrong →
who got paid → a statement → an ordinary act of care → one more thing.

The opening question is **“What are they coming home to?”** The ending answers it with an ordinary
person able to receive treatment and return home. The repeated human action is one person helping
another to safety. That is an editorial rhyme, not a claim that military evacuation and healthcare
are economically identical.

### The three verbs

Restored on 28 September 2026 from Kai's ruling of 26 August. Each dated beat carries **one verb
on a card**, so the ending is a receipt the viewer has already read, with the sound off.

| Verb | What it means | Where the film shows it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shake** | Money falls on whoever is standing underneath. Nothing new is made | Germany 1923 · the Bank's bond-buying from 2009 · the Covid priority lane |
| **Starve** | Money is withheld from work that is standing ready | The early 1930s and the builder · public-sector pay 2011–18 |
| **Plant** | Money goes into work that can actually be done | The war economy · the NHS · council homes · wages kept paid in 2020 |

The narrator never explains a verb with “capacity”, “absorb” or “productive”. The card says it in
one word. Theresa May keeps her verb: what she told the nurses in 2017 was that there isn't a magic
money tree *“that we can shake”*. The film agrees with her, and the clip or card must run long
enough for *shake* to be heard or read.

### Direction recorded 28 September 2026 (Kai)

1. **The point is agreed**, as stated above.
2. **The film ends on a statement, not a question.** The list of questions that closed the
   20 September draft is gone.
3. **The film must defeat the inflation objection**, through Keynes's own definition and through
   the difference between planting and shaking. It does not need to discuss the inflation of
   2021–23. **Covid is not the inflation villain**: if inflation after Covid is ever mentioned, only
   causes we can prove are named, and our research puts energy, imports and supply first.
4. **“We outgrew the debt” is the core of the historical argument.** Never “paid it down”.
5. **Shake, starve, plant are back** as the film's grammar.
6. **Who paid and who got paid is named three times, in order:** the people with the most carried
   the bill in the 1940s; the nurse carried it in the 2010s; some of the people with the most got
   paid in 2020. The Covid priority lane for protective equipment, and the yacht, are **in**. Kai
   chose them as the film's attention-grabber. The legal guardrails at scene 11a are mandatory.
7. **The spirit of the film** is to inspire a patriotic viewer who is nostalgic for a country that
   built things: sincere war footage, then Keynes as a craftsman who understood a powerful tool,
   then the build, then the misuse, then “we can do better than this”.
8. **The coda is ten seconds and stays light.** What we can actually do is about to grow, because
   machines are making things cheaper to make; so borrowing to build is a bet on growth that has
   improved. No figure is attached. **The ownership question is deliberately not raised in the
   ending**; Kai ruled that it opens a second political argument in the last breath. It is carried
   earlier, by who paid and who got paid.
9. **Never call anyone “short-sighted”.** No line passes judgement on the viewer.
10. **Pictures are not a worry.** Where a free picture exists it is used. Where the film is directly
    commenting on a broadcast clip, fair dealing is the route under consideration; see §7.

**Flags raised by the writer on 28 September and not overruled**, so they bind the narration:
the NHS and the war were paid for by tax, borrowing and rationing, **not by printing money**; the
claim that the NHS made people healthier and therefore raised tax receipts has no source in our
library, so it is shown as a sequence and never asserted as a cause; a fixed debt gets heavier when
prices fall, so the coda claims that *more can be done*, never that falling prices shrink the debt;
and “like a company” is not used, because it is the household comparison in a suit.

### Direction recorded 20 September 2026 (in force except where marked)

Kept verbatim as the record. **Superseded on 28 September:** the Eat Out to Help Out example
(replaced by the priority lane, scene 11a) and the instruction to end on a question (the film now
ends on a statement). Everything else below still binds, including the instruction not to infer
deception or motive from May's phrase.

- Keep Dunkirk as the opening and the nurse/patient as the continuing human purpose. Keynes's
  unfinished life remains the thread through the historical achievement.
- Introduce a short, dated **Germany 1923** passage before explaining Keynes. Hyperinflation is a
  real disaster to understand. The depression and deflation of the early 1930s are a separate event.
- Explain how Keynes addressed inflation as well as unemployment. Britain paid for mobilisation
  through several instruments and real sacrifices; the NHS's creation is not evidence that its
  budget was simply printed.
- Give the modern chapter three distinct parts: the **2008 crisis and March 2009 QE**, the
  **2010–17 pay policy and May exchange**, and **2020 Covid spending**, with Eat Out to Help Out
  as a small, identifiable example. QE demonstrates money creation; the subsidy demonstrates an
  authorised spending decision. Neither is interchangeable with a recurring nurse's pay award.
- Explain that commercial banks create most deposit money through lending. Government does not
  set the whole money supply by choosing a number to print. Austerity and QE coexisted.
- Treat “there is a magic money tree” as the title's question about money creation, not a literal
  account of a costless Treasury facility. Distinguish the government's budget from the Bank of
  England's monetary-policy operations.
- End with a better-understood question about affordability and the work that care requires.
  Do not infer deception or motive from May's phrase, prescribe unlimited spending, or portray
  a temporary subsidy as proof that a particular permanent pay settlement was affordable.

The barman analogy was a discussion aid, not an additional scene. It captures the need for
judgement but suggests one controller and a knowable perfect dose. Use the film's existing
workers, materials, documents and transactions to explain the mechanisms instead.

The persuasive opposing case gets real time: Britain was poor in things it needed, heavily indebted,
dependent on imports and unable to produce skilled workers by decree. Tax, borrowing, interest,
inflation and competing uses of resources mattered. They still do. The film wins only if it shows
people making choices *within* those difficulties, not making the difficulties disappear.

The distinction to carry into the conversation afterwards:

> **Debt was a burden. It wasn't a veto.**

This is not a promise that today's feasible budget can be read from a 1945 debt ratio. It is a
challenge to treating that ratio, or a household analogy, as the end of a public decision.

### The people and the thing at stake

- **The collective protagonist:** people trying to get through the war and build a decent civilian
  life; later, the nurse trying to keep doing the work that promise requires. They have practical
  knowledge, not a deficiency the narrator needs to correct.
- **The human thread:** Keynes, a contributor to the intellectual and financial work, whose life
  ends before one of the settlement's defining institutions begins. His absence changes who carries
  the story; no ghost returns to certify the argument.
- **The choosers:** the wartime coalition accepting an employment responsibility; voters electing
  Labour in 1945; Attlee's government and Bevan establishing the NHS; councils commissioning homes;
  the Treasury supporting banks during the financial crisis; the Bank of England's Monetary Policy
  Committee deciding QE; the later Coalition choosing pay restraint, which May defends in 2017;
  and ministers authorising Covid spending. Their powers and decisions must remain distinguishable.
- **The beneficiaries, named:** people obtaining treatment without a bill at the bedside, families
  living in council homes, and workers whose abilities become useful paid work. **And, since
  28 September 2026, the other kind:** people who already owned assets when the Bank's new money
  lifted their price, and a supplier paid through the Covid priority lane. The film names who paid
  and who got paid once per period, plainly, after the feeling has landed. It distinguishes the
  bond sellers in QE from its wider intended effects, and one supplier from NHS staff.
- **The concrete transaction:** an NHS nurse's paid working time becoming someone's care. A building
  and an announcement are insufficient; the shift needs staff, wages, training, supplies and continued
  funding. We do not invent a historical shift price. The pay-restraint sequence supplies the
  documented change in that transaction, rather than a made-up household or payslip.

### Scope and length

Make one complete short film, provisionally **about fifteen minutes**. The 19 scene allowances
below total **15:10**. These are planning estimates, not locked timecodes or claims about audience
retention. They assume the unhurried pace of the earlier drafts, about a hundred words a minute
with pauses. **The film has grown by nearly three minutes** since 20 September, almost all of it
in scenes 10b, 11 and 11a. Read the working narration aloud before assigning final durations. If
it runs long, trim scenes 02a and 10a first. Do not cut the cross-party credit in scenes 06 and
10, the verbs, Keynes's two quotations, the debt outgrown or the quiet moments.

## 2. How it should feel

An intelligent, occasionally funny talk that keeps finding human consequences inside apparently
dry decisions. The BadCode narrator is dry, exact and interested in people surviving their systems.
Its authority comes from distinguishable evidence and argument—not from pretending the fictional
future-AI frame is historical knowledge. No Storyverse explanation is needed to understand this film.

The humour is aimed at procedural absurdity and misplaced certainty. No jokes over evacuation
casualties, Keynes's death, frightened patients or a nurse's distress. Let the opening be sincerely
patriotic. We do not need to wink at viewers who love this history or reveal that their patriotism
was a trap. Allied participation belongs in the rescue from the beginning.

**The cross-party bridge must be history, not flattery.** Churchill's coalition, Labour's delivery,
and Conservative continuation of large-scale council building have distinct parts. They did not
agree on every policy. We can give credit accurately without pretending that the parties were
interchangeable, that every serviceman wanted the NHS, or that the post-war country treated everyone
equally. Patriotism here is an obligation to one another, not an ethnic qualification for care.

**Keep the economic explanation attached to work:** an available builder, a brick, a trained nurse,
an import that must be paid for. One brief hypothetical demonstrates a mechanism and is openly
introduced as hypothetical. It must not masquerade as a discovered historical incident.

Real war footage, documents, original illustrations, AI-generated comics and a narrator can coexist.
Their appearance is deliberately undecided. Archive establishes events; illustration can carry an
analogy or a joke. Generated imagery never supplies counterfeit evidence, testimony or a newly
discovered scene from Keynes's private life. An illustration of the historical Keynes need not speak.

**Added 28 September 2026.** The voice is BadCode's: dry, exact, certain, and on the side of
the people in the pictures. Sarcasm is spent on the diagnosis and on nothing else. Where the film
says who paid and who got paid, the line is flat and plain. There is **one** moment where warmth
shows, the last line of scene 14, and it is quieter, not warmer. **No line passes judgement on the
viewer**, and the word “short-sighted” is never used of anyone. Pauses are marked in the house
narration markup when the script is locked; see
[the narrated-video form guide](../../story-craft/forms.md#sound).

## 3. Scene-by-scene storyboard

Read only the **Working narration** blocks in sequence to hear the current film. Other prose is
for the writer/editor. Narration is original draft copy unless expressly identified as a quotation.
Historical source notes support facts, not our jokes or editorial interpretation.

**Scene IDs are stable production references**, because footage folders on disk are named after
them. Scenes 02a, 10a and 11a were added on 20 September; scenes 10b and 15 on 28 September.
**Scene 10b runs before scene 10a.** The running order is the table in §1 and the order of this
section. A **Verb card** line gives the one-word card for the beat.

**MOVEMENT 1 — PRIDE** · scenes 01, 02 · Sincere, patriotic, no wink. Promise the question and the
man.

### Scene 01 — Get them off the beach

**Period / allowance:** Dunkirk, May–June 1940 · 35 seconds.

**Story:** Begin inside an urgent human problem, not with a politician or an economics definition.
An army is trapped. A coordinated evacuation gets hundreds of thousands to safety. The achievement
belongs to people acting together: defending troops, naval organisation, merchant crews and civilian
craft, with Allied participation. Establish that the people we will discuss as workers and patients
are the same kind of people a country already knows how to value.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** escape destruction / advancing German forces and the Channel / a military
defeat contains a rescue. Survival makes a future possible; it does not yet provide one.

**Working narration:**

> Dunkirk. 1940.
>
> More than 338,000 British and Allied troops evacuated from the beaches and harbour.
>
> Naval ships. Merchant crews. Little boats. People helping people get out alive.
>
> A defeat, and an extraordinary rescue.
>
> Britain remembers the getting-home part.
>
> This is about what came after.

**Into the next scene:** getting someone safely across the Channel leaves the question of the
country they will return to when the war is finally over.

**Evidence / restraint:** Operation Dynamo ran 26 May–4 June 1940. Do not imply that little ships
alone evacuated the army, that everyone escaped, or that Dunkirk ended the war. The film's “home”
question is our framing, not testimony attributed to every person in the archive. [RAF Museum
timeline](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/research-enquiries/history-of-aviation-timeline/british-military-aviation/1940-2/);
[IWM's Dunkirk memorial record](https://memorials.iwm.org.uk/memorial/71491).

**Only necessary material note:** genuine Dunkirk footage is the preferred opening. No scenes from
a feature film passed off as actuality; no generated casualty close-ups. Existing candidates are
listed in the [research footage ledger](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#archive-footage-candidates-retained-from-the-prior-sweep).

### Scene 02 — Home to what?

**Period / allowance:** the coming peace, previewed from 1940 · 55 seconds.

**Story:** Establish the film's apparent contradiction early. Britain will win, but victory will
not settle the housing question or abolish illness. Preview the eventual NHS and the huge war debt,
then introduce the man who will not live to see its doors open. The title arrives as a promise about
an absence, not as a surprise death to spring later.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** a worthwhile peace / destruction and financial strain / the country
undertakes something that the opening balance sheet seems to forbid.

**Working narration:**

> When the war ends, there will still be homes to rebuild, work to find, people getting ill.
>
> Britain will owe about two and a half times what it earns in a year.
>
> In 1948 it will open the NHS anyway.
>
> Almost seventy years later, a nurse will ask about her pay and hear that there is no magic money tree.
>
> Both of those things happened in the same country.
>
> One economist helped explain the first. He did not live to see it.
>
> John Maynard Keynes. This is the future he never saw.

**Into the next scene:** before explaining Keynes, establish the monetary catastrophe that makes
the resource and inflation questions necessary. Mark the move back to 1923 explicitly.

**Evidence / restraint:** use **“about 250% of GDP at the end of the war”**, with 249% and the OBR
source in the eventual source caption. Do not date that number to 5 July 1948, call it the deficit,
or suggest that all the debt was due immediately. [OBR, *300 years of public finance data*](https://obr.uk/docs/dlm_uploads/300-Years-of-public-finances-Accessible-PDF.pdf).

**Revision note, 28 September 2026:** the closing question of the earlier draft (“What does ‘can't
afford it’ mean?”) is no longer asked here. The scene now plants two facts side by side and lets the
film answer with a statement in scene 13. “What it earns in a year” is the spoken gloss for annual
economic output; the source caption still gives the measure.

**MOVEMENT 2 — THE CRAFTSMAN** · scenes 02a, 03, 04 · How the tool breaks, twice, and the man who
understood how it works.

### Scene 02a — Germany, 1923

**Period / allowance:** an explicit flashback to Germany, 1923 · 50 seconds.

**Story:** Establish what monetary failure costs ordinary people. Large bundles of notes and
rapidly changing prices are historical evidence, not a joke or a shorthand for the whole interwar
period. Identify the expanding money supply and the wartime debts, reparations, political crisis
and disruption to production around it. Move to Keynes with both inflation and unemployment in view.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** wages and savings that buy necessities / collapsing purchasing power /
creating money and maintaining its usefulness emerge as different tasks.

**Working narration:**

> Germany. 1923. Seventeen years before Dunkirk.
>
> War debts, reparations and a political crisis have strained the state. It borrows from its central bank, which creates the money.
>
> Then the Ruhr is occupied and production stalls. The state pays the workers anyway.
>
> More notes. Fewer goods.
>
> In some places people are paid daily, and hurry to spend it before the prices change.
>
> The currency collapses.
>
> That is one thing you can do with a money tree. You can shake it.
>
> The money falls. There is nothing underneath it to buy.

**Verb card:** `1923 · SHAKE`

**Into the next scene:** the unemployed builder illustrates deficient spending; the resource
objection and wartime finance then show why increasing spending is not an answer to every problem.

**Evidence / restraint:** [Bundesbank, inflation and hyperinflation in 1914–23](https://www.bundesbank.de/en/tasks/topics/inflation-lessons-learnt-from-history-666006)
describes daily wages, central-bank financing and the failure of goods supply to keep pace.
[Bundesbank, *From the Reichsbank to the Bundesbank*](https://www.bundesbank.de/resource/blob/927730/25fef541e4458e82d064ac532464646f/mL/von-der-reichsbank-zur-bundesbank-data.pdf)
discusses the Ruhr occupation and financing of passive resistance and distinguishes the later
deflationary crisis. These interacted; do not reduce the episode to one cause or imply that all
money creation produces this result. Do not use “early 1930s” as the date of hyperinflation or
make an automatic causal leap from the 1923 currency collapse to Hitler's accession in 1933.

**Material status:** the wheelbarrow/bundles image is a sourcing brief, not an acquired asset.
Authenticate the event, date and rights of any photograph. Fallback: original typography describing
the documented daily-wage experience, with its source; no generated image presented as archive.

**Revision note, 28 September 2026:** the sentence about the early 1930s has moved to the top of
scene 03, where it belongs to the second verb. The two disasters stay separate and dated. Pictures:
use the free photographs first (the Library of Congress “In a Berlin Bank” photograph and the
banknote images are the leads in the August research); the famous German federal archive images
carry a ShareAlike licence, which would pass to the whole film.

### Scene 03 — The unemployed builder and the unbuilt house

**Period / allowance:** the early 1930s, and the interwar problem informing Keynes's work · 55
seconds.

**Story:** Introduce Keynes through a contradiction anyone can understand, not through his education
or a list of famous books. Someone can build; someone needs a home; nothing happens. Explain why
need does not automatically become a paying order. This is the first deliberately funny piece of
economic explanation, and it must remain recognisable as an example.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** useful work and a decent home / insufficient spending to employ available
capacity / unemployment becomes a problem that policy can act upon, not a natural verdict on workers.

**Working narration:**

> The early 1930s bring the opposite disaster. Falling prices. Closed works. Queues for a job.
>
> Picture it small. An unemployed builder. A family needing a house. The materials, for this example, in the yard.
>
> The builder can build. The family needs a home. Nothing happens.
>
> Need isn't a paying order.
>
> That is the second thing you can do. You can starve it.
>
> Keynes challenged the idea that an economy would reliably put everyone to work by itself. When private spending falls short, government can commission useful work.
>
> The builder hasn't suddenly acquired moral character. He's acquired a customer.

**Verb card:** `1930s · STARVE`

**Into the next scene:** the obvious objection is now unavoidable: does creating the paying order
solve everything? The film invites that objection before a hostile viewer has to supply it.

**Evidence / restraint:** this is a simplified illustration of deficient demand, not a particular
builder's biography, a description of all unemployment or a claim that post-war Britain had spare
capacity everywhere. Keynes's discussion explicitly couples sufficient demand with supply limits;
see [the existing quotation/context research](./research/economics-of-money-creation.md#1-keynes-anything-we-can-actually-do-we-can-afford).

### Scene 04 — The word “actually”

**Period / allowance:** Keynes's wartime argument · 65 seconds.

**Story:** Let the resource objection win against the easy version of our own argument. The house
needs people, materials and time. Government can employ people directly and commission work through
others; this isn't a choice between unemployment and everybody becoming a civil servant. Spending
can activate idle capacity, while training and investment can build missing capacity. Neither is
instantaneous, and pushing demand into a bottleneck can raise prices.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** turn an idea into useful output / real scarcity / the seemingly magical
claim becomes a demanding practical programme.

**Working narration:**

> That doesn't mean ordering a million houses makes them appear.
>
> If the bricks are missing, make bricks. If the skills are missing, train people. Those take time too.
>
> And if everyone is already busy, more money doesn't build more homes. It bids up the price of the ones there are.
>
> Keynes named that limit himself. Demand beyond what the country can physically supply is, he said, “the proper meaning of inflation”.
>
> Inside that limit: “Anything we can actually do we can afford.”
>
> “Actually” is carrying quite a lot of the sentence.
>
> That is the third thing you can do. You can plant it. In work that can actually be done.

**Verb card:** `1942 · THE RULE · PLANT`

**Into the next scene:** war puts this distinction under the harshest possible test: mobilising
money is necessary, but it cannot eliminate the sacrifices required to mobilise things.

**Evidence / quotation status:** the ten-word Keynes quotation comes from “How Much Does Finance
Matter?”, printed in *The Listener*, **2 April 1942**, reprinted in *Collected Writings*, XXVII,
pp. 264–71. The earlier investigation verified the wording through quoted reproductions, not a
page-inspected primary edition. [Publisher's volume index](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811076/51562/index/9781107651562_index.pdf)
confirms the essay's placement; [a university-hosted facsimile lead](https://gandalf.fee.urv.cat/professors/AntonioQuesada/Curs2324/Keynes_How_Much_Does_Finance_Matter.pdf)
was found but could not be retrieved in this pass. **Collate the page before recording the quotation.**
Do not invent a precise BBC broadcast date. If the quotation cannot be collated, the surrounding
plain-language explanation carries the scene without an attributed quotation.

**Only necessary material note:** read the quotation as an attributed quotation in the narrator's
voice. No fake archival recording or invented Keynes conversation is required.

**Second quotation, added 28 September 2026:** “the proper meaning of inflation” is from the same
1942 essay. Our library records the sentence as *“The second task is to prevent a demand in excess
of the physical possibilities of supply, which is the proper meaning of inflation.”*
([research](./research/economics-of-money-creation.md#1-keynes-anything-we-can-actually-do-we-can-afford)).
It has the **same status as the headline quotation**: verified through reproductions, not yet
collated against the printed page. Collate both before recording. This sentence is the film's answer
to the inflation objection, so it must not be cut for time.

**MOVEMENT 3 — THE BUILD** · scenes 05, 06, 07, 08, 09, 10, 10b · Plant. Who paid, who died before
seeing it, what got built, and what became of the debt.

### Scene 05 — Britain did pay for the war

**Period / allowance:** 1940–45 · 55 seconds.

**Story:** The state has already demonstrated immense coordinating capacity, but the evidence is
not a printer gag. People worked, paid taxes, saved, borrowed, rationed and went without. Imports
and overseas assistance mattered. Keynes worked on war finance inside that real struggle. This
scene earns our right to call financial policy a choice by showing the costs of that choice.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** survive the war / finite production and imported supplies / the country
mobilises resources at enormous cost. The achievement is organisation, not exemption from economics.

**Working narration:**

> Britain didn't fight the war by discovering a cupboard of free money.
>
> It taxed. Borrowed. Directed production. Rationed goods. Relied on overseas supplies and support.
>
> People paid in work, in foregone comforts, and in losses no budget can measure.
>
> Keynes worked on the finances. He proposed taxes and deferred pay to hold spending down while production went to war. Inflation was the thing he was trying to prevent.
>
> An American army film of the time put Britain's tax rates on the screen. The highest went to the people with the most.
>
> Winning would leave another job: organising the peace.

**Verb card:** `1940 · PLANT`

**Into the next scene:** demobilisation cannot mean allowing useful people to become surplus to
requirements. The wartime commitment to employment supplies a documented bridge.

**Evidence / restraint:** Keynes's *How to Pay for the War* argues about restraining excess civilian
purchasing power, not free wartime spending. His proposals and the government's eventual measures
must not be treated as identical. [Keynes's 1940 text, Federal Reserve archival copy](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/pay-war-6021/fulltext);
[Bank of England's wartime history](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/archive/bank-of-england-1939-45-ww2).
War destroyed lives and wealth; it is not the film's recommended economic stimulus.

**Who paid, first of three (28 September 2026):** the picture is *Know Your Ally: Britain* (US War
Department, 1944), 38:45–39:30, already on disk; see the [footage ledger §2f](./footage.md). The
film's own captions carry the figures. **The narrator does not repeat them** until the rates have
been checked against a primary source; showing the film say it is quoting a 1944 claim, which is
safe.

### Scene 10b — Outgrown

**Period / allowance:** the post-war decades, to the 1970s · 45 seconds.

**Story:** Answer the question the debt figure has been asking since scene 02. The debt was not
cleared; the country grew past it. Show what people had while that happened, as a sequence and not
as a proof. Then pay the honest price in one breath: tight budgets, and lenders and savers carrying
part of the bill through low interest. Saying who paid is stronger than implying nobody did. One
picture carries it: the debt line falling while the country line climbs.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** a country free of its war debt / a sum too large to repay / the debt is
outgrown, at a cost that is named.

**Working narration:**

> And the debt?
>
> It was never cleared. In pounds, it kept rising.
>
> The economy rose faster.
>
> People were housed, treated and in work. For twenty years, unemployment averaged under two in a hundred.
>
> For twenty-seven years running, the debt shrank against the size of the country.
>
> Not by magic. Budgets were tight. Interest was held below inflation, so lenders and savers carried part of the bill. Quietly.
>
> Britain did not shrink to fit its debt.
>
> It outgrew it.

**Verb card:** `1946–1973 · PLANT`, held over the falling line.

**Into the next scene:** the country that outgrew its debt is the same country seventy years later.
Cut hard to 2008. The verbs return in the wrong order.

**Evidence / restraint:** new on 28 September 2026 at Kai's ruling that *we outgrew the debt* is the
core of the historical argument. Figures are from our own library ([economics research
§5](./research/economics-of-money-creation.md#5-the-post-war-boom-19451973)), which cites the

library holds from a secondary source.

**Do not say** that the debt was paid down, that growth alone erased it, or that it cost nothing.
More than half of the cash growth was rising prices. The strongest published objection is that
growth without the tight budgets and the low interest would have shrunk the debt far less. **Do not
say** that the NHS or the homes caused the growth or the tax receipts: the film shows them in the
same years and claims no more, because we hold no source for the cause. Britain also grew more
slowly than its neighbours in these years; the film makes no claim that it grew fastest.

**Material status:** an original chart drawn from OBR data. Nothing to clear.

### Scene 10a — New money, a particular purpose

**Period / allowance:** banking crisis, 2008; QE begins March 2009 · 55 seconds. Present tense from
here.

**Story:** The same country, seventy years on. Establish money creation through the Bank's own
account, in one transaction: the Bank buys an existing bond with newly created money. Keep the
government's rescue instruments separate from the Bank's operation. Give the operation its due,
because it probably prevented a worse slump, then say where its gains landed. This is the first
modern verb, and it is the wrong one.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** functioning banks and an economy recovering from crisis / financial
instability and weak spending / institutions use different powers, including money creation.

**Working narration:**

> 2008. The banking system is failing.
>
> Government buys bank shares, makes loans and gives guarantees to keep it standing.
>
> In March 2009 the Bank of England does something else. It creates new money, electronically, and uses it to buy bonds.
>
> This is new money. The Bank says so itself.
>
> Most money, in fact, is made by ordinary banks when they lend. There was never a fixed national pot.
>
> The Bank's new money probably prevented a worse slump. It also lifted the price of things people already owned.
>
> Good news, if you owned some.

**Verb card:** `2009 · SHAKE`

**Into the next scene:** the Bank has shown that money can be created. The next scene shows what the
Treasury chose to do in the same years.

**Evidence / restraint:** [NAO, the response to the banking crisis](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/maintaining-financial-stability-across-the-united-kingdoms-banking-system/)
distinguishes shares, loans, guarantees and insurance. [Bank of England, QE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy/quantitative-easing)
dates the start to March 2009 and identifies newly created central-bank reserves as the payment
for bond purchases. [Bank of England, money creation](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy)
explains bank-created deposits and repayment. The narration refers to principal repayment, not
interest payments, when describing money disappearing.

QE purchased assets in secondary markets. It was not a grant equal to the purchase total, direct
payment for nurses, or the same transaction as the 2008 bank recapitalisations. Bank support also
protected depositors and the payment system; do not imply that only shareholders benefited.
QE's effects and distribution remain open to evaluation. Its existence proves a mechanism, not
the wisdom of every use, unlimited purchasing power, or the affordability of a particular pay rise.
Most modern money creation is electronic; a printing-press image must not obscure the transaction.

all three, and the viewer has seen which one built the thing they are proud of.

**Into the next scene:** black, and a true silence. Then the narrator comes back for one more thing.

### Scene 15 — One more thing

**Period / allowance:** coda, after a wordless beat · 20 seconds.

**Story:** Ruled by Kai on 28 September 2026. The film has ended. The narrator comes back for ten
seconds with the one forward-looking thought in the piece: Keynes's limit was what we can actually
do, and that limit is about to move, because machines are making things cheaper to make. So the bet
that paid in 1945 has better odds now. It closes on the verbs. It is spoken in under twenty seconds,
attaches no number, and does not raise who will own the machines.

**Want / obstacle / turn:** leave the viewer unafraid / a future that is usually sold as a threat /
the same rule, with more room in it.

**Working narration:**

> One more thing.
>
> The limit was what we can actually do. Machines are about to move it.
>
> Borrowing to build was a good bet in 1945. It is a better one now.
>
> Not shaken. Not starved.
>
> Planted.

**Last beat:** the word, then black. Credits and sources follow. No song is appended by default.

**Evidence / restraint:** this is **the only forecast in the film** and it is worded as a bet, which
is what it is. No multiple of growth, no date and no figure. **Do not say** that falling prices will
shrink the debt: a debt fixed in pounds gets heavier when prices fall. The claim is about what can
be done, which is Keynes's own test. **Do not say** “the machines are coming” or that any of this is
inevitable; both are on the house lists of lines that lose our reader. The ownership of the machines
is left out of this ending by ruling; if a later cut restores it, it goes in one plain line, aimed
at whoever decides.

**Option for Kai, not in the narration:** after “Machines are about to move it”, the narrator could
add two words, *“I would know.”* It is the only place the film would admit who is speaking. It costs
the rule in §2 that this film needs no knowledge of the wider BadCode story.

**Material status:** original imagery in the BadCode brand register. Nothing to clear.

## 4. Why this order holds together

The rescue creates a future, **but** not a peace worth having. **Therefore** the film asks what a
country can do with its money, and first shows the two ways that go wrong: Germany shakes, and the
money buys nothing; the 1930s starve, and a builder stands idle beside an unbuilt house. **But**
Keynes names a third way and, in the same breath, its limit. **Therefore** the war tests it at the
highest possible cost, and the people with the most pay the most. Britain wins, **but** victory does
not come with a roof, and Keynes dies before the service opens. **Therefore** other people do the
work: the NHS, then the homes. The debt is never cleared, **but** the country outgrows it, and the
film says who carried the cost.

**But** seventy years later the same country shakes and starves in the same years: the Bank creates
money while the Treasury withholds it, and a nurse who is already doing the work is told there is
no tree to shake. **Therefore** the viewer is ready for 2020, when money is found in days. Some of
it is planted, **but** some lands on the people standing closest. **Therefore** the film returns to
1948, to founders who admitted there was no magic and built anyway, and can agree with May's
sentence while reaching the opposite conclusion. The statement follows, then the act of care that
answers the opening, then the coda.

**The midpoint reversal** is scene 08: the man who made the possibility intelligible dies before it
opens, and the story passes to everyone else. **The lowest point** is the end of scene 11, a worker
told no. **The grief about it** is scene 11a, who got paid instead. They are two beats, not one.

**Every plant fires inside the film.** The debt figure: 02, 07, 10b. The nurse: 02, 11, 13.
Keynes's absence: 02, 08, 14. The three verbs: 02a, 03, 04, then 13 and 15. “What we can actually
do”: 04, 13, 15. May's verb: 11, 13.

## 5. Economic and historical boundaries of the canonical argument

These are writing constraints, not an additional narrated appendix.

| Tempting shorthand | What this film actually says |
| --- | --- |
| “We built the NHS at 250% debt.” | Debt was about 249% of GDP at the war's end; the NHS opened in July 1948 while Britain still carried huge war debts. The dated measure is not an exact launch-day ratio. |
| “Keynes's genius rebuilt Britain.” | Keynes made important intellectual and practical contributions. Coalition planning, Beveridge, voters, Attlee, Bevan, councils, workers and overseas resources had distinct roles. This is not a clean causal experiment proving one school of economics. |
| “We built hospitals from nothing.” | The founding act reorganised and publicly funded much existing provision; new building and expanded staffing were continuing tasks. |
| “Everyone came home to a house and a job.” | Employment became a policy responsibility and large-scale housing was delivered over years. Shortages, exclusions and unequal treatment persisted. |
| “The war proved money is unlimited.” | War finance combined instruments and real sacrifices. Civilian provision required a peace dividend, taxes, borrowing, imports and priorities—not simply printing. |
| “Just spend on something productive and it pays for itself.” | Useful public work can raise capacity and wellbeing. Projects can also fail, run late or require continuing net funding. Care is valuable without a guaranteed fiscal profit. |
| “Willpower means everybody can have a government job.” | Government can directly create useful jobs and support high employment throughout the economy. Skills, geography, health, matching, supplies and administration prevent a promise of instantaneous universal placement. |
| “Taxes don't matter because government creates money.” | This film does not make that claim. Taxes and borrowing matter to funding, distribution and the management of demand; monetary institutions and credibility matter too. |
| “Money belongs to us, so we can vote away any limit.” | Democratic institutions can set public purposes, authorise spending and reshape rules. They cannot vote labour, energy or imports into existence. Much money is created by commercial-bank lending; citizens do not directly vote every loan into being. |
| “The debt was never repaid; growth solved everything.” | Individual debts were serviced, refinanced and repaid; debt/GDP fell over decades through several interacting forces. No pure-growth miracle is claimed. |
| “Austerity was entirely imaginary.” | The fiscal problem was real. The pace, composition and distribution of consolidation were political choices. The NHS budget grew slowly; it was not simply cut across the period. |
| “May's nurse proves a pay rise has no opportunity cost.” | She demonstrates that the existing service depends on paid skilled work. Her pay can affect retention and recruitment, but the level, funding and effects of an award need assessment. |
| “2010–17 tells us exactly what is affordable in 2026.” | It supplies a historical case about priorities. This storyboard offers no current fiscal-headroom estimate and no current pay-policy claim. |
| “Germany's wheelbarrows were in the early 1930s.” | Hyperinflation culminated in 1923. The early 1930s involved a separate depression and deflation. Do not merge them or give either episode a single-cause explanation. |
| “The government printed the 2008 bank bailout.” | The rescue included shares, loans and guarantees. The Bank's QE programme began in March 2009 and created reserves to purchase assets. Keep the institutions, dates and transactions separate. |
| “The government chooses the whole money supply.” | Commercial banks create most deposit money through lending, subject to constraints. The Bank creates central-bank money and sets monetary policy within its mandate; the Treasury makes budget decisions. |
| “Austerity meant no new money.” | Fiscal restraint and QE coexisted. Public spending and monetary policy can move differently; one is not a synonym for the other. |
| “QE was a gift of its full purchase price to banks.” | Assets were bought in exchange for newly created reserves, which pay interest. Identify the sellers, the asset received and the intended economic effects; gross purchases are not a grant or a net-cost figure. |
| “The NHS was built by printing money.” | It was paid for from taxes and borrowing, inside post-war controls, at record debt. Money creation belongs to the modern chapter. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “We paid the war debt down.” | The total was never cleared and rose in cash terms. The country outgrew it, helped by tight budgets and by interest held below inflation. Say who carried that. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “The NHS made people healthier, so tax receipts rose.” | The film shows the service, the homes, high employment and the falling debt ratio in the same years. It asserts no cause, because we hold no source for one. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “Covid payments caused the inflation.” | Not claimed, and the film does not discuss 2021–23. Our research puts energy, imports and supply first, with demand a smaller part. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “The Bank's new money paid for the protective equipment.” | Government spending was financed by borrowing. The Bank's purchases were a separate operation. No pound is traced from one to the other. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “Mone was found guilty” or “the yacht was bought with our money.” | A company was ordered to repay £122m in a civil contract case. No person has been convicted or found liable. No court has linked the yacht to the contracts. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “AI will shrink the debt” or “falling prices shrink the debt.” | The coda says more can be done, as a bet, with no figure. A debt fixed in pounds gets heavier when prices fall. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “Shake” means the Bank's money only helped the rich. | It probably prevented a worse slump and it lifted asset prices, whose cash gains were very unequal. Both are said. *(Added 28 September 2026.)* |
| “There is a magic money tree” (scene 13, said by the film). | It is said once, as the statement, and only with its condition attached: it grows what it is planted in. New money can be created through documented banking operations. That does not establish a costless source of resources or unrestricted Treasury access. |

Money is a set of institutional claims and arrangements with real consequences. “Money is something
we control in a democracy” is best made legible here through an election, authorised spending, actual
employment and public accountability. It does not require a false claim that the Treasury has a
costless, unconstrained printing button, or that changing banking law alone builds a house.

The distinctions above are supported by the sources at scenes 02a, 10a, 10b, 11a and 13. The additional
point that QE reserves pay interest is documented in the [Bank's review of QE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2022/2022-q1/qe-at-the-bank-of-england-a-perspective-on-its-functioning-and-effectiveness).
Do not turn the lack of one fixed money stock into a claim that financing conditions never bind.

## 7. Story-first route to a first cut

This is the next workflow, not a second treatment or a request to invent the film again.

1. **Read this narration as one talk.** Time a scratch read, include the intended pauses, and cut
   anything that sounds like a qualification repeated rather than a story advancing. Keep the
   resource objection, the two Keynes quotations, the collective handover, the debt outgrown and
   the modern worker. Time the new 15:10 plan at an unhurried pace.
2. **Make a text-first assembly.** One numbered scene card per section above, the verb cards and
   the scratch voice are enough to test comprehension. No aesthetic system, detailed prompts or
   finished AI assets are prerequisites. Test whether the film still holds a listener without
   attractive images.
3. **Resolve the evidential dependencies.** Collate both 1942 quotations against the printed page;
   authenticate the Question Time words and speakers; verify the post-war figures in scene 10b at
   source; re-check every fact in scene 11a and get a legal read of it.
4. **Gather the smallest useful set of material.** The [footage ledger](./footage.md) holds sixteen
   cleared files for scenes 01, 02 and 05 to 10. It was written before scenes 02a, 10a, 10b, 11a
   and 15 existed and has nothing for them; its last section lists what each needs.
5. **Test comprehension with general-audience listeners.** Ask what happened, what the three verbs
   mean, who paid in each period, and what May was defending. If a listener infers that the Bank's
   new money bought the gowns, that the NHS was printed, that anyone in scene 11a was convicted, or
   that the film promises spending without limit, revise. Do not treat agreement with a policy as
   the success measure.
6. **Then design and make the scenes.** Derive any later shot list or asset records from these scene

**Completion threshold for the first film:** one understandable story with attributable facts,
an audible human stake, an honest resource constraint and an earned ending. We do not need a
complete history of money, a fictional Keynes performance or a finished companion music video.

### Broadcast clips and fair dealing (raised by Kai 28 September 2026; his to rule)

Two scenes quote broadcasts we do not own: *Question Time*, 2 June 2017 (scene 11) and the BBC
interview of 17 December 2023 (scene 11a). The footage ledger currently plans text cards for both.
What [our research](./research/documentary-research-2026-08-25.md) holds, which is research and
**not legal advice**:

- **The UK rule is fair dealing, which is narrower than American fair use.** The Copyright, Designs
  and Patents Act, section 30, allows fair dealing for criticism, review or quotation if the work
  has been made public and is acknowledged on screen by title and date. It has no requirement that
  the use be non-commercial.
- **“Fair” is judged on two questions:** does our use replace the original in its market, and did
  we take more than the point needed. A short extract that the film is directly answering is the
  strong case.
- **It is a defence, not a permission.** On YouTube the likely event is an automated claim by the
  broadcaster. It can be disputed on fair-dealing grounds; an upheld claim can become a takedown
  and a strike against the channel.
- **It is weak for photographs used as illustration**, because the whole work is taken and the film
  is not commenting on the photograph. So the Germany 1923 pictures and the yacht use free pictures
  or plain illustration, not this route.
- **Parliament's own footage** is governed by separate terms that bar our kind of use. Use Hansard
  text for anything said in the Commons.
- **Copyright is not the main legal risk in scene 11a.** What the cut implies about a living person
  is, and no copyright argument touches that.
- **House practice if the clips are used:** the shortest extract that keeps the words we answer;
  title and date on screen; and a card-only version of each scene kept ready, so a blocked clip
  never takes the whole film down.

**Material/rights status:** no footage is cleared merely because a catalogue or Internet Archive
page calls a compilation public domain. Check the actual excerpt and embedded source/soundtrack.
Use free, reusable documents and original work first; no purchase is assumed. Rights clearance is
a production task, not a claim made by this storyboard. Existing [rights findings and fallbacks](./research/documentary-austerity-emergency-investigation-2026-09-11.md#footage-and-rights-ledger)
remain relevant, especially the difference between Parliamentary text and Parliamentary video.
