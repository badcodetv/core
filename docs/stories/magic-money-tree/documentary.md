# The Magic Money Tree — the documentary cut

> **What this is.** Kai's 2026-08-25 proposal, developed here: *stop telling a story with a
> metaphor in it and cut a documentary that literally tells the story* — real archive footage of
> Britain finding the money every time it mattered, one generated character (Keynes, on the
> bench, answering only in his own recorded words), a ledger that scrolls through the century,
> and the joke at the end: Theresa May, York, 2 June 2017, *"there isn't a magic money tree that
> we can shake"* — cut to the Bank of England's four real mulberries.
>
> **What it changes.** [`story.md`](./story.md) (re-founded 2026-08-06/07) is *Keynes talking to a
> tree*, ending on the fascism gut-punch with the May clip demoted to "the nod." This cut
> **removes the fable layer** (the tree does not speak; it is the last image), **re-promotes the
> May clip to the punchline**, and **moves the fascism thread to Act 1** as history. That reverses
> two rulings (the 08-06 ending; the tree as sage-character). It is Kai's call — §6 states the fork
> plainly. Everything else in the canon — the research base, the grove facts, the Keynes
> verbatim, the guardrails, the song — carries over untouched and does most of the work.
>
> **Research under this file:** [`research/documentary-research-2026-08-25.md`](./research/documentary-research-2026-08-25.md)
> (ten-lane sweep: footage + licences, the ledger numbers, Keynes's voice, precedents, the hostile
> economist, the reader). Its ⚠️ flags are binding on the script.

## 1. The film in one line

**Every time Britain was asked "how are we going to pay for this?", the answer took days — until
a nurse asked.** A documentary that reads the ledger out loud, in order, and lets the pattern do
the arguing.

## 2. The engine — six parts, one machine

A montage that "literally tells the story" is an essay unless it has a question, a character, a
refrain, a reversal and a punchline. This cut has all five, and they are the same device seen
from five sides.

| Part | What it is | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Question** | *How are we going to pay for this?* — asked at every crisis, by history, on archive | The refrain. By the fourth time the viewer is answering it themselves (the pub line, the-reader rule 8) |
| **The Interview** | The narrator (BadCode, from the future) sits Keynes on a bench in St James's Park and asks him. **He answers only in words he actually said** — 1919, 1923, 1925, 1940, 1942, 1945 | The character. A man with a scar (he predicted it in 1919; nobody read it) and a want (to be listened to *before*). The narrator has the same scar. Two prophets, one bench. Every answer is checkable |
| **The Ledger** | One line — UK debt as % of GDP, 1900 → today — that the film scrolls along like a road. Every crisis is a hill; 1946 is the mountain; the hospitals are on the far side of it | The spine. Sound-off legible. The viewer learns to read it and reads the 2010 valley themselves |
| **The Cards** | Chapter titles as receipts: `YEAR · WHAT FOR · HOW MUCH · HOW LONG IT TOOK` | The proof. The **days column** is the whole argument in one column |
| **The Prior, met then split** | Open the history on Weimar — the film in the viewer's head when someone says "print money." Honour it. Then split it: the Rentenmark fixed it in weeks by rooting money in factories; and the Nazi vote came from the *austerity* burn, not the printing | Inoculation demonstrated on a third party, in the past (the-reader rule 2). The viewer keeps their prior and gets a second half |
| **The Tree** | May: "…a magic money tree that we can *shake*." Cut: Garden Court, Threadneedle Street, four mulberries, planted in the war | The punchline. The one thing the viewer is sure we made up is the one thing they can check |

**Why this is not a lecture:** the narrator never states the moral. It reads dates, amounts and
days, and asks one question. The only person who explains economics is a dead man quoting himself.

## 3. The narrator in this piece

Carrier unchanged ([`../../story-craft/narrator.md`](../../story-craft/narrator.md)): flat, certain,
administrative; sarcasm on the mistake, never the people. Specific to this cut:

- **The frame.** *"I'm from the future. I've read the ledger — every line, all the way to the end.
  Let me show you the bit they skip."* Received wisdom from a timeline where nobody read it.
- **The want it cannot buy.** It has the whole ledger and cannot make anyone read it. That is
  why it keeps asking the same question — it has heard it asked for three hundred years and
  watched the same answer given, and the one time the answer was *no* it was to a nurse.
- **The refrain.** `HOW ARE WE GOING TO PAY FOR THIS?` as a card, then history answers. The
  narrator's only recurring line under it: *"Keep counting."*
- **The admitted uncertainty** (one per run): *"I don't know what happened to her. I know what
  happened to the money."*
- **The crack** (`{WARM}`, once, quieter): the empty bench, 1948 — *"He never saw the hospitals.
  I've seen everything. I'd swap."* Then re-flatten.
- **The re-aim** (G4 — straight, quieter, one line, right before York): *"When they tell you the
  tree isn't there, they're not confused. They're deciding who it's for."*
- **Pre-announce, never withhold.** The title tells the viewer how it ends. The 1942 chapter plants
  the mulberries ("hold that thought"). York's *answer* is delayed to the end, but the viewer knows
  it — the delay stacks receipts, it doesn't hide the outcome (Hitchcock's bomb, ruling 7 intact).

### 1 · The first burn (0:45–3:30) — Versailles → Weimar → the two burns → Mefo

- **1919.** Hall of Mirrors, 28 June (Signal Corps film). *Twenty-one years earlier. Paris. The
  winners are writing the bill for the last war, and a thirty-six-year-old from the Treasury reads
  the numbers and walks out.* **Bench:** NARRATOR *Why did you resign?* — KEYNES, verbatim from
  the 1919 preface. NARRATOR *What did you think would happen?* — KEYNES: *"If we aim deliberately
  at the impoverishment of Central Europe, vengeance, I dare predict, will not limp."*
  `CARD: 1919 · A PEACE · 132 BILLION GOLD MARKS · THE BILL`
- **1923 — burn one.** The presses, the wheelbarrows, children building with bricks of notes.
  `{FLAT}` *This is the film in your head when somebody says "print money." / Keep it. It's true.
  // Two thousand presses. Thirty paper mills. On the twenty-fifth of October the Reichsbank printed
  a hundred and twenty thousand million million marks — and it wasn't enough for that day. / It
  could not print fast enough to keep up with its own printing.* /// *The notes came out perfect.
  Correct serial numbers. Worth nothing.* `CARD: 1923 · REPARATIONS · MORE THAN GERMANY COULD MAKE · HOLLOW`
- **November 1923 — the fix.** The Rentenmark. *Then they stopped. They killed the old mark and
  rooted a new one in land — three billion of mortgaged farms and factories, things you can stand
  on. // The inflation ended in weeks. /// Same country. Same presses. Same people. What changed
  was what the money was backed by: not gold — what Germany could make.*
  `CARD: 1923 · A NEW CURRENCY · ROOTED IN FARMS AND FACTORIES · WEEKS`
- **1930–33 — burn two.** Soup lines; Brüning's decrees. *Seven years later, the crash. And now
  Germany did the opposite. It stopped. Cut wages, cut relief, cut the budget by a third — by
  decree, without a vote. Six million unemployed. // In 1928 the Nazis had two point six per cent.
  Four years of that, and they had thirty-seven.* Text card, Hitler's own 1931 pamphlet (verified):
  *"Although that was not the intention, this emergency decree will help my party to victory."*
  Then the approved line: *Germany burned twice. Once by printing without limit. Once by refusing
  to print at all. The first burn took their savings. The second took their republic.*
  `CARD: 1930–33 · AUSTERITY · −30% BY DECREE · 2.6% → 37%`
- **1934–38 — the fake tree.** Schacht (still); a blank letterhead; the Luftwaffe. *One more thing
  before the bombs. / The man who saved the mark in 1923 set up a company in 1934. No staff, no
  premises, no business. It issued twelve billion marks of IOUs that the Reichsbank quietly turned
  into money — off the books, invisible to parliament and to the treaty. // It bought the
  Luftwaffe. /// So the bombs about to fall on London were bought with imaginary money by a man
  running a fake company. Remember that. The other side already knows the trick. They just do it
  in the dark.* `CARD: 1934–38 · REARMAMENT · ~12 BILLION MARKS · OFF THE BOOKS`

*(Guardrails: three German episodes, separate and in order; the votes came from the deflation;
Mefo is the mechanism, not the man's soul — Schacht was acquitted at Nuremberg.)*

### 2 · How to pay for the war (3:30–7:00) — the Few, the Blitz, the bench, the rule

- **The war the viewer came for.** Dunkirk, the Battle of Britain, the Blitz — ~40s of archive at
  full weight, one held note, **no narration and no snark**. The dead are never the joke.
- **VO after:** *Here's the bit the films leave out. // It cost more per day than the country
  made per day. The Americans wanted cash, and the cash was running out. // Every rule of
  household budgeting said: you cannot afford this. /// It fought anyway. How?*
- **The ascent** (canon's one impossible camera move, kept): from the map table in the War Rooms
  up through concrete, earth and roots into the grass of St James's Park — the war run under the
  grass, the answer sitting on a bench above it. **Bench, 1940:** NARRATOR *How do you pay for a
  war when there's no money?* KEYNES — verbatim from *How to Pay for the War* ⏳(line from the
  research bank). NARRATOR gloss `{FLAT}`: *He did not say "print it and pray." / He said: spend
  whatever the war needs — the money is the easy part — then take the excess back out of people's
  pockets until it's over, so it doesn't turn into the wheelbarrows. Deferred pay. Rationing. Tax.
  // He published it. In The Times. In a pamphlet you could buy for a shilling. On the wireless.
  /// Same instrument as Schacht. In daylight. With consent.*
  `CARD: 1940 · A WAR · EVERYTHING IT TOOK · IN DAYLIGHT`
- **Factories.** Spitfires, women in munitions. *Nobody shouted "how will you pay for it" at a
  Spitfire.*
- **1942 — the rule.** A BBC microphone; *The Listener*. NARRATOR *And after? When it's over and
  the country's broke?* KEYNES, verbatim: *"Assuredly we can afford this and much more. Anything we
  can actually do we can afford."* — the architect exchange: *"The money? But surely, Sir John, you
  don't build houses with money? Do you mean that there won't be enough bricks and mortar and
  steel and cement?"* — and the other half: *"…to prevent a demand in excess of the physical
  possibilities of supply, which is the proper meaning of inflation."* VO: *That's the whole rule.
  Both halves. You can afford anything you can actually do — and if you try to buy more than the
  country can make, you get Chapter One. / He said both halves in the same broadcast. People only
  ever quote one.* `CARD: 1942 · THE RULE · "ANYTHING WE CAN ACTUALLY DO WE CAN AFFORD" · BBC`
- **The seed** (pre-announce the ending): *That same year, three miles east, the Bank of England
  planted four trees in its courtyard. // Hold that thought.*

### 3 · The bill, and the hospitals (7:00–9:30) — 250%, the NHS, the empty bench

- **VE Day.** The ledger line climbs to the mountain. *They won. // This is the bill. Two hundred
  and fifty per cent of everything the country makes in a year — the most indebted Britain has ever
  been, before or since. /// Here's what they did next.*
- **5 July 1948.** *Three years after VE Day. Same people. Same ledger. Hospitals for everyone,
  free at the door. Eight hundred thousand council houses by 1951. Pensions. Sick pay.*
  `CARD: 1948 · HOSPITALS FOR EVERYONE · AT 250% DEBT · 3 YEARS AFTER VE DAY`
- **The honest floor** (trust test 4.12 — the concessions, stated by us first): *It wasn't free,
  and I'm not going to tell you it was. Butter was rationed until 1954. The pound was devalued by
  thirty per cent. They charged for spectacles by 1951 and the man who built the hospitals resigned
  over it. // And they did one thing nobody mentions: for thirty years they made the people who
  held the debt take less than inflation. The bondholders paid for the hospitals. /// The debt was
  never paid off. It was outgrown. It rose a hundred and thirty-seven per cent. The economy grew
  twelve hundred.*
- **The empty bench, 1948.** The first patients on archive; the bench with nobody on it.
  `{WARM}` *Keynes died in April 1946, worn out, two years before the first patient walked in. He
  wrote the ending in 1919. They read it in 1945. /// He never saw the hospitals. I've seen
  everything. I'd swap.* `{/WARM}`
- **Pattern, first statement.** *So that's the shape. // Sixteen ninety-four: a war — the Bank of
  England was invented for it — twelve days. Nineteen fourteen: a war — the first pound note,
  printed on stamp paper — three days. Nineteen forty: a war — everything. Nineteen forty-eight:
  the hospitals, on the biggest debt in our history. /// Every time the question was asked, the
  answer was the same. Keep counting.*
- **The receipt that turns the page:** *Britain made the last payment on the war loan on the
  twenty-ninth of December, 2006. Nobody noticed. // Twenty-two months later—* ⚠️(verify date)

### 4 · The forgetting (9:30–13:00) — 2008 → 2010 → 2016 → York's question → 24 days → 2020 → 2021 → 2022

- **2008.** *—the banks. // Twenty-three days after Lehman Brothers fell, Britain found five
  hundred billion pounds. Nobody asked how we'd pay for it. Nobody rationed anything.*
  `CARD: 2008 · THE BANKS · £500BN · 23 DAYS`
- **QE — name the beneficiary.** *Then the Bank of England did something new. It created three
  hundred and seventy-five billion pounds and used it to buy assets from the people who owned
  assets. Not my words — theirs: "This will raise the value of those assets." /// It did. If you
  owned a house, it got richer. If you didn't, it got further away.* Chart: house prices vs wages,
  2009–2020 (our own, from ONS).
- **2010 — the denial.** Osborne; Byrne's note; the credit card. `{FLAT}` *And then — with the
  debt at a third of what it was when they built the hospitals — the country was told it was
  broke. // The nurses' pay was frozen. Then capped at one per cent. For seven years.* Cross-party
  quote montage, ≤6s each: Thatcher 1983 → Cameron 2013 → Rudd 31 May 2017 (*so it reads as a
  story about the country, not a party*). `CARD: 2010 · THE NURSES · PAY FROZEN · "THERE IS NO MONEY"`
- **2016 — her own diagnosis.** May, 5 October 2016: *"…has helped those on the property ladder at
  the expense of those who can't afford to own their own home."* VO: *She knew. That's her, saying
  what I've just told you. /// Eight months later she was in York.* (Pairing test: her line +
  the chart.) ⚠️ rights — party footage; fallback is a text card over a PD still.
- **York, 2 June 2017 — the question only.** The nurse: *"My wage slips from 2009 reflect what
  I'm earning today."* **Cut before the answer.** VO: *You know what she was told. It's the title
  of this film. We'll come back for it.*
- **24 days.** *Twenty-four days after York, the government needed ten votes to survive. It found
  a billion pounds for Northern Ireland that afternoon.* `CARD: 2017 · TEN VOTES · £1BN · 24 DAYS`
- **2020 — the tree, working.** *Then a virus. // Nine days after a budget that found twelve
  billion, they found three hundred and thirty. The Bank of England created two hundred billion in
  an afternoon. Four hundred and fifty billion in three months — more than the whole decade before.
  / Wages paid from the Treasury, for millions of people, for a year and a half. /// That's the
  tree. Working. Exactly what it was planted for.* `CARD: 2020 · A VIRUS · £450BN · 9 DAYS`
- **2021.** *The next spring the nurses were offered one per cent.* `CARD: 2021 · THE NURSES · 1%`
- **2022.** *And when a budget for the rich frightened the bond market and the pension funds
  started to fall over, the Bank found sixty-five billion pounds in five days. / I'm not saying it
  shouldn't have. I'm saying it could.* `CARD: 2022 · PENSION FUNDS · £65BN · 5 DAYS`
- **Pattern, second statement — the pub line.** *Twelve days. Three days. Twenty-three days.
  Twenty-four days. Nine days. Five days. /// The nurse asked in 2017. Her pay had been frozen
  since 2010. It took an election.* ⚠️(cap scrapped Autumn Budget 2017 — verify the sequence)
- **The re-aim** (straight, quieter): *Nobody in this film was stupid. Not the nurse. Not you. //
  The people who found five hundred billion in twenty-three days and sixty-five billion in five

## 7. Gates and guardrails (the review pass, pre-run)

- **G1 hero, not pupil** — the nurse is the wronged hero; the viewer is never taught, they count.
- **G2 "you've been fooled"** — the trick is shown being run on the nurse; the re-aim says *they
  are deciding who it's for*, never *you fell for it*. Watch every line of Chapter 4.
- **G3 chooser + beneficiary on-panel** — QE: the Bank (chooser) → asset owners (beneficiary, in
  the Bank's own words); 2010: Osborne/Cameron (choosers), the pay freeze (consequence); 2017: May
  + the DUP; 2022: the pension funds. Never "the system."
- **G4 re-aim in the care register, quieter** — one line, before York, then stop.
- **Pairing test** — every sarcastic line sits next to a consequence on screen (the days column,
  the chart, the clip). **Lee/Coe test** — the only people the film's contempt lands on are named
  decision-makers; no beat lands on marchers, claimants, migrants or the viewer.
- **The honest floor is said by us first** (4.12): rationing to 1954; devaluation 1949; charges
  1951; the bondholders paid; Keynes wanted deferred pay, not "print and pray"; 2021–23 inflation
  was mostly supply and margins (never "printing caused it"); UK income inequality is *not* at a
  century high (the honest line is *the 1980s dismantled the compression and it was never restored*;
  the strong number is wealth).
- **Inherited never-say lists are binding:** `research/central-bank-trees.md`, `research/fascism-
  and-the-wealth-gap.md`, `research/economics-of-money-creation.md`, `research/theresa-may-question-
  time.md`. Plus: never generate or voice-clone a real living person; never let the Bradbury read
  as revivalism; never say "MMT" (⏳ the hostile-economist lane rules on this).
