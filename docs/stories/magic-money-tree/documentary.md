## 1. The film in one line

**Every time Britain was asked "how are we going to pay for this?", the answer took days — until
a nurse asked.** A documentary that reads the ledger out loud, in order, and lets the pattern do
the arguing. Its grammar is three verbs — **shake, starve, plant** — and Theresa May gets to be
right about the first one.

## 2. The engine — seven parts, one machine

A montage that "literally tells the story" is an essay unless it has a question, a character, a
refrain, a reversal and a punchline. This cut has all five, and they are the same device seen
from five sides.

| Part | What it is | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Question** | *How are we going to pay for this?* — asked at every crisis, by history, on archive | The refrain. By the fourth time the viewer is answering it themselves (the pub line, the-reader rule 8) |
| **The Interview** | The narrator (BadCode, from the future) sits Keynes on a bench in St James's Park and asks him. **He answers only in words he actually said** — 1919, 1923, 1925, 1940, 1942, 1945 — and every one of them is public domain (UK since 1 Jan 2017 ✅) | The character. A man with a scar (he predicted it in 1919; nobody read it) and a want (to be listened to *before*). The narrator has the same scar. Two prophets, one bench. Every answer is checkable |
| **The Ledger** | One line — UK debt as % of GDP, 1900 → today — that the film scrolls along like a road. Every crisis is a hill; 1946 is the mountain; the hospitals are on the far side of it | The spine. Sound-off legible. The viewer learns to read it and reads the 2010 valley themselves |
| **The Cards** | Chapter titles as receipts: `YEAR · WHAT FOR · HOW MUCH · HOW LONG IT TOOK` — and after every claim, Oliver's *"and now, this"*: the dated, sourced clip | The proof. | **The Prior, met then split** | Open the history on Weimar — the film in the viewer's head when someone says "print money." Honour it. The narrator never says "capacity" or "absorb"; the verb on the card says it in one word, sound-off. And it lets May be right: *you can't shake it. Nobody ever has.* |

**Why this is not a lecture:** the narrator never states the moral. It reads dates, amounts and
days, and asks one question. The only person who explains economics is a dead man quoting himself.

### 2b. The economics, in the film's grammar — ruled 2026-08-26

Kai's brief (2026-08-26): land the film on *we can afford what we can actually do* — both halves —
so that a viewer who is trying to work out why the house costs so much leaves with the mechanism
and the right address for the blame. The adversarial pass on that brief was accepted in full. What
the film concludes, and how it is allowed to say it:

- **The claim.** Money is the easy part; the work is the limit. Money spent *into* work is absorbed
  by the work (1694 a navy; 1914; 1940 the Spitfire lines; 1948 the hospitals; 2020 the pay
  packets). Money shaken over assets lands on asset prices (1923, over a country that had stopped
  working; QE 2009–21 — *"this will raise the value of those assets"* — by the Bank's own design).
  Money withheld from work that is standing idle is not saved; it is lost (Keynes 1925; Brüning
  1930–33; Bevan 1951; the nurse 2011–18). Keynes said all of it in 1942, verbatim, and he is the
  only one who gets to say it in words. The film says it in three verbs on the cards.
- **The synthesis line (Chapter 4).** Germany burned twice, in order. **Britain 2010–19 did both at
  once:** the Bank shook the tree over the people who owned things, and the Treasury starved the
  people who did things. Not our reading — the Chancellor's: *"Our economic plan combines monetary
  activism with fiscal responsibility and supply side reform"* (Osborne, Budget statement, 20 March
  2013 ✅ gov.uk transcript; the same three-part formula in September 2009 and the February 2010 Mais
  lecture).
- **The concession (Chapter 5, the last narrator line before the trees):** *"She's right. You can't
  shake it. Nobody ever has. // You plant it."* May keeps her verb; 1851 Truro proves it has always
  meant the fantasy; the mulberries were *planted*, in the war, in a foot of dirt over the gold.
- **The pub lines (both approved):** *"You can print the money. You can't print the nurse. She was
  already there."* (Chapter 4, closing the pattern) and *"The tree's real. It only grows what you
  plant it in."* (Chapter 5, the last words before the drop).

**The eight rulings** (the pushbacks Kai accepted 2026-08-26 — each is now a never-say in §7):

1. **The two burns stay welded to their nouns.** Shake → wheelbarrows (1923). Starve → the vote,
   then the war (1930–33). The Nazis polled 2.6% *after* the hyperinflation; the vote came from the
   deflation. Never "both led to hyperinflation and war" as one clause.
2. **Never "we don't make anything."** Manufacturing is ~8% of GDP against ~25% in the 1970s, but
   real output has grown over the period — the *share* fell, not the making (⚠️ ONS index of
   production, exact figures at script stage). Offshoring is not why the printing landed on assets:
   Japan makes everything and printed more than anyone; Germany got ECB QE and still ate 2022. It is
   Reform's line word for word, and unaimed nostalgia demonstrably finds an immigration outlet
   (research §8). If the thread is used at all it is a named-chooser beat (who decided, who gained),
   never a cause — open question 10.
3. **Never "you have to be productive before you can spend."** That is Thatcher 1983 (*"the State
   has no source of money other than money which people earn"*) — the household fallacy the film
   exists to kill. Keynes's order is the reverse: the money buys the work. 1940 did not wait to be
   earned. *The tree grows what you plant it in.*
4. **Britain 2010–19 was the 1931 failure, not the 1923 one.** ~8% unemployment (⚠️ ONS, late 2011),
   the worst pay decade since the Napoleonic wars, capacity idle by the million. The nurse in York
   *was* the spare capacity. The wheelbarrow fear is the wrong burn for that decade; the Bank's own
   account of 2021–23 is energy and supply.
5. **The film never explains house prices.** 97% of money is made by commercial banks lending,
   mostly against existing houses; then supply; then buy-to-let; QE is the petrol from 2009. The
   film shows her 2016 sentence, the Bank's sentence and the chart, and the viewer joins them. Never
   "they printed and the houses went up."
6. **Never "currency debasement" in VO.** Goldbug vocabulary; the reader who uses it is halfway to
   Bitcoin or Reform. Say the felt version: *your pay buys less; the house costs more.*
7. **The paper joke gets its aside.** Bank of England notes were cotton rag, polymer since 2016; the
   1914 Bradbury went on postage-stamp paper because banknote paper was not available (✅ secondary,
   consistent across sources). So: *"Paper. Cotton, technically — you take the point."* The admitted
   precision is the trust move; a pedant catching us is the opposite. And the payoff is already in
   canon (`research/central-bank-trees.md`): the one banknote that really did come from a tree is the
   **oldest in the Bank's own collection — Ming, Hongwu, 1368–98, printed on mulberry bark** — and
   that is the Bank's stated reason for choosing mulberries. The joke is true at both ends.
8. **The moral is never stated.** The conclusion arrives as pattern + receipts + a pub line the
   viewer says themselves. Keynes states the rule (1942, PD). The narrator names verbs and counts.

## 3. The narrator in this piece

Carrier unchanged ([`../../story-craft/narrator.md`](../../story-craft/narrator.md)): flat, certain,
administrative; sarcasm on the mistake, never the people. Specific to this cut:

- **The frame.** *"I'm from the future. I've read the ledger — every line, all the way to the end.
  Let me show you the bit they skip."* Received wisdom from a timeline where nobody read it.
- **The want it cannot buy.** It has the whole ledger and cannot make anyone read it. That is
  why it keeps asking the same question — it has heard it asked for three hundred years and
  watched the same answer given, and the one time the answer was *no* it was to a nurse. The 1694 receipt (Chapter 2) is what licenses
  "three hundred years."
- **The refrain.** `HOW ARE WE GOING TO PAY FOR THIS?` as a card, then history answers. The
  narrator's only recurring line under it: *"Keep counting."*
- **The admitted uncertainty** (one per run): *"I don't know what happened to her. I know what
  happened to the money."*
- **The crack** (`{WARM}`, once, quieter): the empty bench, 1948 — *"He never saw the hospitals.
  I've seen everything. I'd swap."* Then re-flatten.
- **The re-aim** (G4 — straight, quieter, one line, right before York): *"When they tell you the
  tree isn't there, they're not confused. They're deciding who it's for."*
- **The concession** (honest floor, once more — after the clip, before the trees): *"She's right. /
  You can't shake it. Nobody ever has. // You plant it."* May keeps her verb. The three verbs are on
  the cards from Chapter 1, so the line lands as a receipt, not a reveal.
- **The pub lines** (Kai, 2026-08-26): *"You can print the money. You can't print the nurse. She was
  already there."* and *"The tree's real. It only grows what you plant it in."*
- **Two decision-makers, always named apart.** The **Bank** (QE, the £200bn of March 2020, the
  2022 operation) and the **Treasury / the Cabinet** (austerity, the DUP, the mini-budget, the pay
  cap). A financially literate viewer catches the conflation instantly ⚠️ §7 B3.
- **Pre-announce, never withhold.** The title tells the viewer how it ends. The 1942 chapter plants
  the mulberries ("hold that thought"). York's *answer* is delayed to the end, but the viewer knows
  it — the delay stacks receipts, it doesn't hide the outcome (Hitchcock's bomb, ruling 7 intact).

### 1 · The first burn (0:45–3:30) — Versailles → Weimar → the two burns → Mefo

- **1919.** The Hall of Mirrors, 28 June — the real signing film (`cbs-cbswwi-450` ✅) and the
  Kirtland photograph of the moment (Commons, PD ✅). *Twenty-one years earlier. Paris. The winners
  are writing the bill for the last war, and a thirty-six-year-old from the Treasury reads the
  numbers and walks out.* **Bench:** NARRATOR *Why did you resign?* — KEYNES, verbatim from the
  1919 preface. NARRATOR *What did you think would happen?* — KEYNES: *"If we aim deliberately at
  the impoverishment of Central Europe, vengeance, I dare predict, will not limp."*
  `CARD: 1919 · A PEACE · THE BILL · THE SEED`
- **1923 — burn one.** The Ruhr occupied (Bain / Agence Rol stills ✅); "In a Berlin Bank" (Bain
  ✅); the 100-trillion-mark note (✅); the wheelbarrows and the children with money-bricks —
  Bundesarchiv, ShareAlike ⚠️ Kai's call. `{FLAT}` *This is the film in your head when somebody
  says "print money." / Keep it. It's true. // January, 1923: France occupies the Ruhr — the
  country's industrial heart — and the government tells the workers there to down tools and pays
  them by printing. Two thousand presses. Thirty paper mills. On the twenty-fifth of October the
  Reichsbank printed a hundred and twenty thousand million million marks — and it wasn't enough for
  that day. / It could not print fast enough to keep up with its own printing.* /// *Money printed
  against work the state itself had stopped. The notes came out perfect. Correct serial numbers.
  Worth nothing. /// Shaken — over a country that had stopped working. That's the first verb.*
  `CARD: 1923 · PRINTING AGAINST HALTED WORK · ∞ · HOLLOW · SHAKE`
  *(⚠️ §7 #4: the "couldn't physically make the goods" gloss is contested — Germany's industry was
  intact; this is framed as a **choice to print** against production the state had halted. The
  mechanism survives; the honesty is the point.)*
- **November 1923 — the fix.** The Rentenmark notes (Commons, PD ✅). *Then they stopped. They
  killed the old mark and rooted a new one in land — three billion of mortgaged farms and
  factories, things you can stand on. // The inflation ended in weeks. /// Same country. Same
  presses. Same people. What changed was what the money was backed by: not gold — what Germany
  could make. / Planted.* `CARD: 1923 · A NEW CURRENCY · ROOTED IN FARMS AND FACTORIES · WEEKS · PLANT`
- **1930–33 — burn two.** ⚠️ the weakest footage beat (§8) — stills, the ledger line, and
  Brüning's decree as a document. *Seven years later, the crash. And now Germany did the opposite.
  It stopped. Cut wages, cut relief, cut the budget by a third — by decree, without a vote. Six
  million unemployed. // In 1928 the Nazis had two point six per cent. Four years of that, and they
  had thirty-seven.* Text card, Hitler's own 1931 pamphlet (verified): *"Although that was not the
  intention, this emergency decree will help my party to victory."* Then the approved line:
  *Germany burned twice. Once by printing without limit. Once by refusing to print at all. The
  first burn took their savings. The second took their republic. // Shake: wheelbarrows. Starve: the
  war. Different burns. Keep them apart.*
  `CARD: 1930–33 · AUSTERITY · −30% BY DECREE · 2.6% → 37% · STARVE`
- **1934–38 — the fake tree.** Schacht (Bain portrait ✅); a blank letterhead (ours); the Luftwaffe
  and the rallies — *The Nazis Strike* (`gov.archives.arc.36068` ✅). *One more thing before the
  bombs. / The man who saved the mark in 1923 set up a company in 1934. No staff, no premises, no
  business. It issued twelve billion marks of IOUs that the Reichsbank quietly turned into money —
  off the books, invisible to parliament and to the treaty. // It bought the Luftwaffe. /// So the
  bombs about to fall on London were bought with imaginary money by a man running a fake company.
  Remember that. The other side already knows the trick. They just do it in the dark.*
  `CARD: 1934–38 · REARMAMENT · ~12 BILLION MARKS · OFF THE BOOKS · PLANTED IN THE DARK`

### 2 · How to pay for the war (3:30–7:00) — the Few, the Blitz, the bench, the rule

- **The war the viewer came for.** Dunkirk, the Battle of Britain, the Blitz — ~40s of archive at
  full weight (`gov.archives.arc.36070` *The Battle of Britain*, `gov.archives.arc.65841`,
  `1942_Dover` ✅), one held note, **no narration and no snark**. The dead are never the joke.
- **VO after:** *Here's the bit the films leave out. // It cost more per day than the country
  made per day. The Americans wanted cash, and the cash was running out. // Every rule of
  household budgeting said: you cannot afford this. /// It fought anyway. How?*
- **The ascent** (canon's one impossible camera move, kept): from the map table in the War Rooms
  up through concrete, earth and roots into the grass of St James's Park — the war run under the
  grass, the answer sitting on a bench above it. 
- **The pedigree — 1694 and 1914** (≈45s; added 2026-08-26). Before Keynes answers, history does.
  NARRATOR *How do you pay for a war when there's no money?* `{FLAT}` *The same way as always.* //
  **1694** — the charter (Commons, PD; the Bank's own words: "primarily founded to fund the war effort
  against France" ✅): *Sixteen ninety-four. A war with France and no money for it. So they invented a

  England. It was planted for a war.* `CARD: 1694 · A WAR · £1.2M · 12 DAYS · PLANT` // **1914** — the
  Bradbury (Commons, PD ✅): *August, 1914. War on the Tuesday. An Act on the Thursday. A pound note on

  for postage stamps.* `{FLAT}` *Paper. Cotton, technically — you take the point. / The money was

- **Bench, 1940:** NARRATOR *And in 1940 — how?* KEYNES — verbatim, *How to Pay for the War*, ch. V ✅: *"A suitable
  proportion of each man's earnings must take the form of deferred pay."* NARRATOR gloss `{FLAT}`:
  *He did not say "print it and pray." / He said: spend whatever the war needs — the money is the

- **1942 — the rule.** *The Listener*, 2 April 1942 (the print ⚠️ — the broadcast date is

### 3 · The bill, and the hospitals (7:00–9:30) — 250%, the NHS, the crack, the empty bench

- **VE Day** (`111-adc-4267` Piccadilly ✅). The ledger line climbs to the mountain. *They won. //
  This is the bill. Around two hundred and fifty per cent of everything the country makes in a year
  — the most indebted Britain has ever been, before or since* (OBR: 249%, "an all-time high" ✅;
  the OBR also says ~270% — say *around 250*). */// Here's what they did next.*
- **5 July 1948.** ⚠️ no public-domain film exists — the 1948 leaflet facsimile (Wikisource ✅;
  copyright term to confirm) with a push-in, stills, the ledger. The leaflet's own words on screen:
  *"Everyone—rich or poor, man, woman or child—can use it or any part of it… it will relieve your
  money worries in time of illness."* VO: *Three years after VE Day. Same people. Same ledger.
  Hospitals for everyone, free at the door. Eight hundred thousand council houses by 1951.
  Pensions. Sick pay.* `CARD: 1948 · HOSPITALS FOR EVERYONE · AT ~250% DEBT · 3 YEARS AFTER VE DAY · PLANT`
- **The honest floor** (trust test 4.12 — the concessions, stated by us first): *It wasn't free,
  and I'm not going to tell you it was. Butter was rationed until 1954. The loan from America came
  with strings, and when the strings were pulled in 1947 the pound nearly broke; two years later
  they devalued it by thirty per cent. In 1951 they put charges on spectacles and teeth — to pay for
  a war in Korea — and the man who built the hospitals resigned.* **Bevan's own argument, card,
  National Archives (verified):** he told the Cabinet the factories couldn't physically build what
  the rearmament money was buying — *there weren't the machine tools* — so cutting health to fund it
  bought nothing. *The man who built the NHS made this film's argument in 1951: a cut is only a saving
  if somebody else can use the work. Nobody could. So it wasn't a saving. It was just less. // And they did one
  thing nobody mentions: for thirty years they made the people who held the debt take less than
  inflation. The bondholders paid for the hospitals. /// The debt was never paid off. It was
  outgrown. "In nominal terms, debt increased by 137 per cent over the 30 years, but nominal GDP
  increased by more than 1,200 per cent."* — the OBR's sentence, on screen, verbatim ✅.
- **The empty bench, 1948.** The first patients (stills); the bench with nobody on it.
  `{WARM}` *Keynes died in April 1946, worn out, two years before the first patient walked in. He
  wrote the ending in 1919. They read it in 1945. /// He never saw the hospitals. I've seen
  everything. I'd swap.* `{/WARM}`
- **Pattern, first statement.** *So that's the shape. // Sixteen ninety-four: a war — the Bank of
  England was invented for it — twelve days. Nineteen fourteen: a war — the first pound note, on
  stamp paper — three days. Nineteen forty: a war — everything. Nineteen forty-eight: the hospitals,
  on the biggest debt in our history. /// Every time the question was asked, the answer was the
  same. Plant it. Keep counting.*
- **The receipt that turns the page:** *Britain made the last payment on the war loan on the
  twenty-ninth of December, 2006 — forty-five million pounds, the last working day of the year.
  Nobody noticed. // Twenty-two months later—*

### 4 · The forgetting (9:30–13:00) — 2008 → 2010 → 2016 → York's question → 24 days → 2020 → 2021 → 2022 → 2025

- **QE — the shake. Name the chooser and the beneficiary.** *Then the Bank of England did something new. It
