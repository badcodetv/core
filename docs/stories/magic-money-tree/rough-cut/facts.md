## 1. Dunkirk: "More than 338,000 British and Allied troops evacuated" (00:06)

**Verdict:** ✅ correct as written.
**Source:** IWM, "What you need to know about the Dunkirk evacuations", https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-dunkirk-evacuations (primary: national museum). Its sentence: "Operation Dynamo … involved the rescue of more than 338,000 British and French soldiers … between 26 May and 4 June 1940." The precise tally is 338,226 (widely cited; not re-fetched at a primary naval source).
**Notes:** IWM says "British and French"; "Allied" is fine (about a third were French; a few Belgian). Keep "more than".

## 2. UK debt in 1945 ≈ 2½ × a year's GDP (00:44 card DEBT-1)

**Verdict:** ✅ "about two and a half times" holds on every measure; the peak is 1946, not 1945.
**Figures:** Bank of England *Millennium of Macroeconomic Data* (A29, consolidated national debt, calendar-year-end): 1944 219%, **1945 242%, 1946 259% (peak)**, 1947 244%. OBR box "Post-World War II debt reduction": "Shortly after the end of the Second World War, UK government debt peaked at around 270 per cent of GDP" (other OBR publications give 238-252% for 1946-47, depending on the series).
**Source:** https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/statistics/research-datasets/a-millennium-of-macroeconomic-data-for-the-uk.xlsx (primary, sheet "A29. The National Debt") and https://obr.uk/box/post-world-war-ii-debt-reduction/ (primary).
**Notes:** The card says 1945 (242%, "2.4×") and the narration says "about two and a half": both defensible. If you want the card to match the peak, use `1946`. Never say "259%" on screen unless the series is named.

## 3. KEYNES-1: "the proper meaning of inflation" (03:43)

**Verdict:** ✅ collated. The sentence sits in the passage the storyboard flagged as uncollated; the Cambridge scan of *Collected Writings* XXVII is now read.
**Exact text (the sentence):** "The second task is to prevent a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply, which is the proper meaning of inflation."
**Card:** *"…the proper meaning of inflation."* · `KEYNES · 1942`
**Context sentence before it:** "The first task is to make sure that there is enough demand to provide employment for everyone." After it: "For the physical possibilities of supply are very far from unlimited."
**Source:** Keynes, "How Much Does Finance Matter?", *The Listener*, 2 April 1942; *The Collected Writings of John Maynard Keynes* vol. XXVII, *Activities 1940-1946: Shaping the Post-War World: Employment and Commodities*, ed. Moggridge, pp. 264-270. Text read from the Cambridge Core page scan reproduced at https://www.bradford-delong.com/2020/05/john-maynard-keynes-how-much-does-finance-matter.html (PDF header "Downloaded from cambridge.org/core": the CW page itself, hosted by a secondary site; retrieved from the URV copy https://gandalf.fee.urv.cat/professors/AntonioQuesada/Curs2324/Keynes_How_Much_Does_Finance_Matter.pdf). Tooze's Chartbook quotes the same sentence: https://adamtooze.substack.com/p/chartbook-on-shutdown-keynes-and.
**Notes:** Narration "Demand beyond what the country can physically supply is, he said, 'the proper meaning of inflation'" is a fair paraphrase of "a demand in excess of the physical possibilities of supply".

## 7. Wartime tax rates: "EXCESS PROFITS TAX 100%", "HIGH INCOME TAX 97½%" (04:56, 12:40)

**Verdict:** ✏️ The film's two numbers are in the right territory but the UK's own figures disagree by year and series, so the narrator's "no figure" rule stays.
**What the sources say:**
- Excess profits tax: 100 per cent (from the 1941 Budget), but 20% of it was refundable after the war (Finance (No. 2) Act 1945, Part IV, s.50(3): "twenty per cent. of the excess profits tax for the period"), so the net was 80%. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo6/9-10/13/part/IV/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true (primary). Tax Watch UK: Parliament debated "if the tax should stand at 100 per cent or not" throughout the war (secondary).
- Top income tax: standard rate 10s in the pound (50%) from 1941 (Spectator, 11 April 1941, secondary). 97.5% = 19s 6d in the pound is the top rate "through the 1950s and 1960s"; at its wartime peak it is given as 98% (standard 50% + surtax 48%, 1944-45) or **99.25%** on investment income in 1941-42 (Wikipedia, "Income tax in the United Kingdom", secondary). No primary budget text was reached this pass.
**Suggested wording if a number is wanted:** "Excess profits were taxed at a hundred per cent, with a fifth to be handed back after the war. The top slice of income paid almost all of it." Otherwise keep the picture with no spoken figure.
**Notes:** The film card itself is fair to show because it is the American film's caption; do not caption it as the UK's official rate. Open item 6b in `footage.md` stays open for a primary budget speech (Hansard, Budget days 1941-44).

## 9. Unemployment "averaged under two in a hundred" 1950-1969 (08:50)

**Verdict:** ✏️ true on the official (registered) count, false on the Bank of England's own adjusted series. Say "registered".
**Numbers (BoE Millennium dataset, sheet A50, my arithmetic):** official post-1950 series (unemployed ÷ workforce): **average 1.7%** for 1950-69 (range 1.1% in 1955 to 2.4% in 1963); Feinstein series 1950-65 averages 1.5%; the BoE "preferred measure", which adds unregistered female unemployment, averages **2.4%** (1.5% in 1955 to 3.5% in 1969). The earlier research file quotes 1.6% (1950-69), consistent with the official count.
**Card:** `1950-1969 · REGISTERED UNEMPLOYMENT UNDER 2 IN 100`
**Source:** https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/statistics/research-datasets/a-millennium-of-macroeconomic-data-for-the-uk.xlsx (primary; underlying series Economic Trends Annual Supplement 1997 and Feinstein 1972).
**Notes:** For comparison the 1921-38 average was 13% (research file, not re-checked). The claim is solid for the audience's purposes; "registered" keeps it honest.

## 10. "Twenty-seven years running, the debt shrank against the size of the country" (08:55)

**Verdict:** ✅ literally true on the BoE series, and ✏️ for the line before it ("In pounds, it kept rising", 08:38).
**Year by year (BoE A29, consolidated debt ÷ nominal GDP at market prices, calendar-year-end):** 1946 259.0% (peak), 1947 244.4, 1948 219.7, 1949 208.7, 1950 200.6, 1951 178.5, 1952 165.1, 1953 156.5, 1954 150.5, 1955 139.3, 1956 128.3, 1957 121.7, 1958 116.6, 1959 112.4, 1960 106.5, 1961 101.8, 1962 100.6, 1963 96.5, 1964 89.0, 1965 84.3, 1966 80.7, 1967 80.6, 1968 74.3, 1969 67.0, 1970 59.6, 1971 56.3, 1972 52.1, **1973 48.3**; it **rises** in 1974 (53.2) and 1975. So it falls in every one of 1947-1973: **27 consecutive years**. Weakest link: 1966 to 1967 is a fall of 0.1 point.
**Nominal pounds:** £25.3bn (1946) to £39.3bn (1973), +56%, but it **fell slightly in 1948, 1951 and 1969**, so "kept rising" is not literally true year on year. Nominal GDP went from £9.6bn to £81.5bn (×8.5). OBR's headline for 1946-76 is "debt increased by 137 per cent … nominal GDP increased by more than 1,200 per cent" (different end year and series).
**Source:** BoE dataset as above; OBR https://obr.uk/box/post-world-war-ii-debt-reduction/ (primary). The earlier research file's "27 straight years" agrees.
**Notes:** The calendar-year values are the BoE's interpolation of March financial-year-end figures (¼ of the March of the same year + ¾ of the following March). On the ONS/OBR public sector net debt series (which starts in 1974 in the BoE sheet) the flat 1966-67 step could differ by a tenth of a point, so keep the claim at "twenty-seven years" (not "every single year, without exception") in any printed caption and label the chart's source.
**Suggested:** replace 08:38 (see table). Keep 08:55 unchanged.
