# The Magic Money Tree

**Not the joke tree.** The paper-comes-from-a-tree gag stays in the song. This
tree is a **sage** — think the tree Newton sat under, not a printer with leaves.
An anonymous tree in a London park that has understood money the whole time and
has never once been asked. In 1942 someone finally asks.

## Revised 2026-08-07 — it is young, and that is better

Earlier canon called it *ancient*, *waiting the length of human history*. The
grove lore ([`../the-grove.md`](../the-grove.md)) supersedes that: it grew from a
**Treasury £1 "Bradbury" planted by a Bank of England clerk in August 1914**, so
it is **twenty-eight years old** when Keynes sits down.

**Why the downgrade is a promotion:**

- **It explains the voice.** The locked register — warm, informal, cheeky,
  working-class London — never really fitted a millennium-old oracle. It fits a
  young feral tree perfectly. The double act works because Keynes is the
  poshest man in England and the tree is *not old enough to have earned any
  deference*, and is right anyway.
- **It relocates the ancientness to the family**, where it belongs. The tree
  doesn't remember 1694; its **mother** did, and the others still do. It
  testifies to family history, the way anyone does — secondhand, vivid, with
  opinions.
- **It explains why it, and not the Bank's trees, is the wise one.** The four
  mulberries in Garden Court have only ever seen the inside of a courtyard.
  **This one grew up in a public park, among people with no money**, watching
  ordinary Londoners be skint through the Depression and the Blitz. That is the
  entire reason it understands money and the institutions don't. *The trees that
  live in banks know what money is. The one that lives in a park knows what
  money is* **for**.
- **It makes the loneliness worse, not weaker.** Twenty-eight years in a park,
  a hundred thousand people walking past, not one of them stopping.

**Species note:** the notes are the seed, so it is the **lime's** child — an
ordinary young European lime, not the London plane the pre-2026-08-07 canon
specified. Still utterly unremarkable, which is the requirement.

## The mother

The tree's parent is the **single European lime that stood in the Bank of
England's Garden Court** from the 1780s — painted in 1906, photographed around
1920, and **cut down in the 1930s** when Herbert Baker dug up the garden.

**It doesn't know.** Nobody tells a tree anything. It mentions her in passing —
*"there's an old lime at Threadneedle Street, she'd know"* — and Keynes, who can
simply walk into the Bank and ask, finds out and has to come back and tell it.
Then the second blow: **four young mulberries planted over her last year**, roots
kept deliberately shallow because there is only a foot of soil above a graveyard.
Four replacements who will never be asked a question, only shaken.

That is the story's private grief running under its public one, and it costs
about six lines.

**First words:** *"Mate. You need to speak to me."* — *"…Who the hell are
you?"* — *"I'm the magic money tree. Nobody knows about me. But you seem to be
having a problem."*

**What it knows (its own personality, sparing with words):** it can grow as much
money as there is real work to buy — that was never the hard part, and it is the
*only* rule it has. Ask past that and it still fruits, because it can't refuse,
and what comes out is **hollow** (see [`../the-grove.md`](../the-grove.md) §1).
The hard part is what humans point it at. It is patient, kind, a little cheeky, and increasingly sad:
its best century was the one where a dying economist taught a government to use
it properly, and it has spent every decade since being shaken by people who
publicly insist it doesn't exist.

**Speaks sparingly.** Its role in each era-stop is a few lines of plain truth
against the ghost's precision — the friendship is a double act: the tree has
the wisdom, Keynes has the maths.

**The grove (restructured 2026-08-07 — no longer a final reveal):** inside every
central bank on earth stands a tree, spitting cash on command. The park tree is
not unique in *what* it is; it is unique in having ever been **talked to** rather
than merely shaken. This is now **teased in beat 2** ("I'm not the only one, and
I know the others"), **used as the engine of beat 3** (the tree testifies to
1694, Weimar, Mefo), and **paid off at the '71 stop** as the collars coming off
worldwide. Holding it back as a final reveal wasted it — as a *premise* it earns
the entire history lesson.

**Visual note:** resist all temptation to give it a face. Documentary realism
(house style): a real tree, real park, changing seasons/decades around a
constant bench; its "performances" are wind, light, leaf-fall timed against
dialogue.

**The grief register (the ending):** the tree cannot put its head in its
hands — so **it goes bare out of season**. The final scene is the bench in
winter: the tree stripped, the ghost head-in-hands beneath it, the two of them
watching everything they built being unbuilt by people who say the tree isn't
real. Its saddest line lives here (script to land it), and it is quiet, not
bitter — the tree doesn't hate the marchers; it knows exactly whose choices
starved them.
