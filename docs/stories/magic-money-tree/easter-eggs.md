## Tier 1 — recommend promoting to canon (verified, cheap, load-bearing)

### 1. England's money literally WAS wood for six centuries — and burning it burned down Parliament

The best missed fact, and it turns the film's central joke from a pun into
constitutional history. From the 1100s to 1826, the English Exchequer recorded
Crown debt on **tally sticks** — notched sticks of willow or hazel, **split down
the middle**: the **stock** kept by the creditor (this is where the word *stock*
comes from), the **foil** by the debtor. A split stick of wood was a negotiable
instrument of the English Crown for six hundred years. Money did not
metaphorically grow on trees. **It was tree.**

Then in 1834, embarrassed by two cart-loads of obsolete wooden money, officials
ordered the tallies burned in the furnaces under the House of Lords. The stoves
overheated, the floor caught, and on **16 October 1834 the fire took the entire
Palace of Westminster.** Turner stood on the south bank and painted it.

**The one time anyone tried to prove money doesn't grow on trees, it burned down
Parliament.** — that sentence is the tree's, or the narrator's, and it is true.

Bonus micro-detail: the **counterfoil** on a chequebook is the tally's foil,
still there in the language. And the stock/foil — *the same stick, split, held
at two addresses* — is the 12th-century ancestor of the story's central image:
the nurse's payslip and the yacht as the same banknote at two addresses.

**Recommendation:** into `the-grove.md` as the grove's pre-history (before the
Bank, before paper, England ran on cut wood), and one tree line in beat 3 or one
narrator receipt. Costs ~20 seconds of film.

### 2. The coppice — the ember the ending needs

The tree is a **lime**, and limes are the tree that **regrows when you cut it
down**. A felled lime resprouts from the stool; coppiced limes are among the
oldest living things in Britain — botanists put some stools at ~2,000 years.
The species is nearly unkillable *specifically because* people keep cutting it.

The ending is currently pure winter: bare tree, ghost head-in-hands, hard cut.
`voice.md` demands nurturing under the snark — and this is it, delivered as
botany rather than sentiment. The tree's last line before (or under) the song
can carry it: *"You can cut a lime to the ground, mate. It comes back. That's
the species."* The postwar settlement is the stool, not the corpse.

It also darkens the mother's story en route: did the Bank grub out the lime's
stool, or is she still under the courtyard, waiting? (Leave unanswered. Forever.)

**Recommendation:** canon — one line, placed at the very end, *after* the
gut-punch lands. It doesn't soften the winter; it makes the winter survivable,
which is the entire BadCode doctrine in one image.

### 3. The narrator's source — BadCode is the second asker

A latent frame-logic twist that ties MMT into the storyverse. How does the
narrator know verbatim bench dialogue that had no human witnesses? Keynes died
in 1946; the ghost talks to nobody else. **Because the tree told it.** In the
future BadCode comes from, the superintelligence did the only thing no
institution managed in three centuries: it *asked*.

> Two of us have ever asked it a question. A dying economist, and me.

This resolves the film's epistemology, makes the narrator's authority *sourced*
rather than omniscient, and is the perfect BadCode move — received wisdom from a
future that already went wrong, received *from the last witness*. Optionally a
post-hard-cut sting; at minimum, one narrator line early on.

**Recommendation:** canon as background truth (the narrator's source is the
tree) even if never stated on screen; the sting is Kai's call.

### 4. The silent nurse on the bench (beat 7 upgrade)

## Tier 2 — strong, one verification pass needed ⚠️

### 8. Unter den Linden — Berlin felled its limes in the same decade

**Verified in outline (2026-08-07 search):** the lindens of Unter den Linden —
Berlin's avenue *named for the tree's own species*, many of the trees **~250
years old** — were **felled in 1934–35** during the Nord-Süd S-Bahn tunnel
construction, as part of making the boulevard the east–west axis of Hitler's
planned world capital, Germania. The remnant trees went for firewood in the
war's last days; today's lindens are postwar plantings.

So: **both capitals cut down their limes in the same handful of years, and for
the same reason — the institution wanted a grander room.** London's Bank felled
its lime for a lobby; Berlin felled an avenue of them for a parade route. The
park tree's kin didn't just live in banks; they lined the street the regime
marched down, and the regime cut them down too. ⚠️ Don't pin specific parade
dates/routes to the avenue without checking (the 30 Jan 1933 torchlight route
went via Wilhelmstraße); "the avenue the regime paraded down" is safe.

**Recommendation:** one exchange at the bench in beat 3 (the tree has family on
that avenue) — it fuses the tree-lore with the fascism thread in a single image.

### 9. The lime is the justice-tree; the mulberry is the industry-tree

In German and wider European folklore the lime/linden is **the assembly tree** —
the *Dorflinde* at the centre of the village, the **Gerichtslinde** under which
courts were held: for centuries, justice in central Europe was dispensed *"under
the linden."* The species' entire cultural job is **being gathered under and
spoken to.** The mulberry's cultural job is **production** — silk and paper.

So the Bank's 1930s swap is a parable it performed on itself: **it cut down the
meeting-tree and planted four production-trees.** And Keynes, holding court
under a lime to redesign justice for an economy, is unknowingly re-enacting the
oldest legal tradition of the tree's own species. ⚠️ Verify folklore claims
against a proper source before the narrator says them (high confidence:
Gerichtslinde/Dorfinde are standard cultural history).

**Recommendation:** narrator texture, one line max — candidate: *"Humans used
to hold court under lime trees. They stopped. Look how it's going."*

### 10. Newton ran the Mint — the other tree-taught genius chained the money

The tree's own character file says "think the tree Newton sat under" — and we
never noticed the punchline: **Newton was Master of the Royal Mint for 28 years**
(1699–1727), and his 1717 gold/silver mispricing accidentally put England on the
de facto **gold standard**. The last English genius who learned a law of nature
from a tree went on to chain the money to metal for two centuries; ours learns
from a tree how to take the chain off. Two trees, two geniuses, two laws: one
taught what pulls things down, the other what makes things grow.

The tree gets to be cheeky about it: *"Last one of you who listened to a tree
got put in charge of the Mint. Made a right mess of it."* ⚠️ Verify the 1717
mechanism summary before print (high confidence).

**Recommendation:** one tree line, beat 2 or 3. Also quietly explains why the
tree bothered speaking at all — the last experiment went badly and it's been
waiting to correct the record.

### 11. The two pounds — the mattress and the ground

A Bank of England note keeps its face value forever — the promise to pay never
expires. So a £1 note kept safe since 1914 is still worth exactly £1… which now
buys about a hundredth of what it did. **The pound that was hoarded kept its
face and lost its body. The pound that was buried in the ground became a tree.**
The clerk's joke is the story's whole thesis performed in 1914: the only safe
place for money is *in the real world, growing*. Freeze it and it dies with its
serial number intact. ⚠️ Verify the ~100× multiple with the BoE inflation
calculator; Treasury notes were folded into BoE issuance in 1928 — phrase
around that.

**Recommendation:** the-grove.md §3, one paragraph; possibly a ghost line.

## Tier 3 — noted, use sparingly or not at all

- **The promise rhyme.** 1781: the Bank promises to leave the graves
  undisturbed. 1933: it digs them all up — it kept its one promise for 152
  years, to corpses, until it needed the room. Rhymes precisely with Nixon '71:
  *"your dollar will be worth just as much tomorrow as it is today."*
  Institutional promises as a genre. One narrator aside at most.
- **Jenkins was buried at dawn on April Fools' Day** (1 April 1798) — and the
  tree was planted as a joke. The Bank's graveyard runs on jokes taken
  seriously. Texture only.
- **The mulberry is the mourning tree.** Ovid: the mulberry's fruit turned
  dark forever from the blood of Pyramus and Thisbe, dead under one for
  nothing. The Bank planted the blood-stained species over its graveyard.
  ⚠️ Also: James I's state silk project planted thousands of mulberries and
  failed — popularly "he planted the wrong species," though modern scholarship
  disputes that framing; don't assert the popular version. Deep-cut easter egg
  for the written canon; probably never on screen.
- **Crypto = plastic trees.** If Emperor's New Coin gets its in-story cameo,
  this is the hook: ten thousand artificial trees claiming to be a forest,
  fruiting nothing. One tree line max; linkage stays social per locked decision.
- **The clerk's identity.** Tempting (an early woman clerk, a name, a face) —
  **resist.** The anonymity is load-bearing; canon already locks it.
- **Truss/gilt crisis as a beat.** The "growth, growth and growth" needle-drop
  (Tier 1 #6) earns its place; a full 2022 beat would tangle the 2017–2021
  chronology and the inflation guardrails. Needle-drop only.
