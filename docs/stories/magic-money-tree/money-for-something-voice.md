# Money For Something — Jack's comedy voice

**What this is:** how Jack writes the host, taken from the lines he wrote and the choices he made
on 10 October 2026 (the first topical monologue). Read it before drafting a beat brief, an angle
list or a tightened line for this show. **Jack writes the jokes**; this sheet is so first drafts
and suggestions sound like him, not so anyone writes for him.

Everything under "Jack's words" is quoted. Everything under "My reading" is an inference from one
session and has not been confirmed by him.

## My reading of the pattern

**Shape of a beat**

- Open cold on the fact. No greeting, no set-up, no "and finally".
- Fact first, said plainly, with the number. Then the turn.
- One beat is one to three short sentences. The punchline is the last few words.
- Leave with a throwaway: "Moving on." "In other news." "Next." "Onwards." "What's next." "Anyway."
  A different one each time.

**Where the jokes come from**

- Vulgar and physical, said flat. Sex, bodies, violence, swearing in full.
- A false sincere line, then the pull-back: "to help the poor with Keynesian economics. Just
  fucking with you."
- Fake relief: "Thank God there is no book or film warning us of the dangers of this."
- Sarcasm against a plain number: "And as you can see, the country is flourishing."
- A list of three that gets worse: "fighting crackheads, debating with fascists and calming down
  the elderly about AI."
- National stereotypes as a quick aside (Italians, the French).
- A callback that needs no set-up: "Nurses are still fucked, by the way. Just saying."
- A personal insult for the rich man in the story, carried from one beat to a later one (Bezos
  in beat 1, again in beat 8).

**The host**

- From the future. He has seen how it ends. He lets it slip in a half-sentence and moves on:
  "I know what happens, but no spoilers."
- Bored, not angry. He is reading a repeat.
- Exactly one sincere line in the whole piece, at the very end: "Remember, you can still change.
  There is hope for this timeline." Then no words.
- Read straight. No smile, no wink, no raised eyebrow, no pause for a laugh. No studio laughter.

**How we worked, and it worked**

1. I give a beat brief: the checked fact, what the beat is for, what there is to joke about.
2. He sends a rough line.
3. I fix grammar and facts only, split it into clips, and say plainly what worries me. Once.
4. He decides. If he says "keep this", it is kept, and the worry is written beside the line.
5. When he is stuck he asks for "angles": a bulleted list of ways in, each one line, with the
   facts above them. Ten or twelve is the right number.

## What he changed or turned down

- He turned down long set-ups (the six-line opening became three clips of his own).
- He turned down the "play it straight, no joke" rule on the banks beat; he added one line of
  sarcasm after the numbers.
- He kept his own lines over safer rewrites on beats 4, 6 and 7. Do not re-argue those.
- He accepted fact fixes every time ("off the government", "ex-prince", the £25 billion).
- He accepted turning "mouth-breathing followers" into a line that sides with the voters. The
  rule that the joke lands on the powerful man, never on the people who vote for him, is one he
  will take when it is put as a better joke.

## Things to check every time

- A name, a number, a date, a title (he wrote "prince"; it is "ex-prince").
- Who actually did the thing (the Health Secretary asked; the Prime Minister and Chancellor refused).
- Whether the money was promised or spent, and by which party.
- That a claim about a living person has a source. If not, say so once and write it beside the line.
