# Money as Flow — research brief

**For:** the Storyverse (BadCode). **Date:** 2026-08-07.
**Brief:** find the real argument under "money wants to flow," with **no villains** — markets good,
money good, science good, the material world good. The claim is **distributional**, not accusatory.

**How to read the labels.** Every claim below is tagged:

- **[MAINSTREAM]** — standard economics, in textbooks, uncontroversial.
- **[SERIOUS/HETERODOX]** — real scholarship, real evidence, contested or minority.
- **[METAPHOR]** — poetically useful, not a factual claim. Do not put it in an economist's mouth.
- **[SOFT]** — self-help, theology, or literature. True as *culture*, not as *evidence*.

**The single most important warning is in §3.** Our instinct — "past a threshold, money stops
producing experience" — is **only partly supported**, and the best evidence cuts against the
strong version. Read §3 before writing a word of lyric.

## 1. Ken Honda and *Happy Money*

### Who he is

Ken Honda is Japan's best-selling author on money psychology — self-styled the "Zen Millionaire"
and the "Happy Panda." Per his own site: **120 books, ~9 million copies sold**, with *Happy Money:
The Japanese Art of Making Peace With Your Money* (Simon & Schuster, 2019) translated into 15
languages. He was an accountant and business owner who "retired" at 29. The origin story he tells:
as a child serving tea in his father's accounting firm, he noticed some wealthy clients were serene
and others were agitated — same money, different relationship to it. A family friend's murder-suicide
after a run-in with loan sharks set him on the question of why money moves people so violently.
([kenhonda.com/about](https://kenhonda.com/about/))

### The thesis

1. **Money carries an emotional charge.** *Happy Money* is money that arrives and departs with
   gratitude — paid gladly, received gladly. *Unhappy Money* is money moved through resentment,
   fear, obligation, or contempt. Same currency; different experience.
2. **Money is flow, not stock.** Honda's central image is that money is *energy passing through
   you*, and the health of your finances is the health of the flow — in and out — rather than the
   size of the pile.
3. ***Arigato okane*** ("thank you money"): the practice of literally thanking money as it comes
   in and as it goes out. The cultural root is the Japanese habit of *arigato* as a near-continuous
   social lubricant, plus a Shinto-inflected sense that objects carry spirit.
4. **EQ over IQ around money.** Financial trouble is usually emotional, not arithmetical.

### Be blunt about genre

**[SOFT]** — this is popular self-help. Specifically:

- **Genuinely useful:** the observation that *the same financial position produces wildly different
  subjective experience depending on the person's relationship to it* is true, well-evidenced
  elsewhere (see money scripts, below), and is exactly the insight our story needs. The *arigato
  okane* practice is a real cultural artifact and a good image.
- **Unfalsifiable and to be avoided:** the implied claim that gratitude *causes money to arrive*.
  This is law-of-attraction machinery. It is not testable, it is not true in any mechanism we can
  point at, and adopting it would make BadCode sound like a wealth guru. **Do not use the register
  "money flows to those who thank it."** Use "money that never moves never does anything" — a claim
  about circulation, not about manifestation.
- **The trap:** Honda's frame is *individual and therapeutic*. Ours is *systemic and distributional*.
  If we borrow his warmth we must not borrow his politics-free-ness. He tells you to make peace with
  your money; we are asking what money is *for* at the scale of a society.

### The evidence base he lacks: money scripts

**[SERIOUS/HETERODOX — real psychometrics, modest field]** Brad Klontz and colleagues developed the
**Klontz Money Script Inventory (KMSI)** — "Money Beliefs and Financial Behaviors: Development of the
Klontz Money Script Inventory," *Journal of Financial Therapy* 2(1), 2011,
[doi:10.4148/jft.v2i1.451](https://doi.org/10.4148/jft.v2i1.451) — the most-cited instrument in the
area (~175 citations). It identifies four **money scripts**: *money avoidance*, *money worship*,
*money status*, and *money vigilance*. These are unconscious, typically formed in childhood,
typically inherited from family, and they predict financial behaviour and outcomes.

**This is the respectable version of Honda's core intuition.** Honda says money has an emotional
charge; Klontz measured it and validated the instrument. Cite Klontz when we need the claim to hold
weight; cite Honda when we need the image. Related: Klontz Money Behavior Inventory (2012),
[doi:10.4148/jft.v3i1.1485](https://doi.org/10.4148/jft.v3i1.1485).

## 2. "Money wants to flow" — what is actually under it

### 2.1 Velocity of money — **[MAINSTREAM]**

The **equation of exchange**: **MV = PQ**. M = money stock, V = velocity (the average number of
times a unit of currency is spent in a period), P = price level, Q = real output. Velocity is not
measured directly — it is *derived* as nominal GDP ÷ money stock. That matters: velocity is a
residual, an accounting identity, not an independently observed force. **Do not say velocity
"causes" anything.** It is a description of how fast money is turning over.

**The real numbers** (FRED series `M2V`, US, quarterly, retrieved 2026-08-07 —
[fred.stlouisfed.org/series/M2V](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/M2V)):

| Date | M2 velocity |
| --- | --- |
| 1997 Q3 (all-time peak) | **2.192** |
| 2000 Q1 | 2.135 |
| 2006 Q3 | 2.003 |
| 2008 Q3 (crisis) | 1.904 |
| 2009 Q3 | 1.707 |
| 2014 Q3 | 1.550 |
| 2019 Q4 (pre-COVID) | 1.435 |
| **2020 Q2 (all-time trough)** | **1.126** |
| 2021 Q3 | 1.152 |
| 2023 Q3 | 1.351 |
| 2026 Q2 (latest) | 1.412 |

**Headline stat: US M2 velocity fell 49% from its 1997 peak to its 2020 trough** (2.192 → 1.126),
and as of mid-2026 has only recovered to 1.412 — still **36% below the 1997 peak**. Each dollar
does roughly half as much work per year as it did in 1997.

The St. Louis Fed's own explanation uses our word. On why a massive increase in the monetary base
after 2008 did not produce proportionate inflation:

> "The answer lies in the private sector's dramatic increase in their willingness to **hoard** money
> instead of spend it. Such an unprecedented increase in money demand has slowed down the velocity
> of money."
> — Yi Wen & Maria Arias, "What Does Money Velocity Tell Us about Low Inflation in the U.S.?",
> Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, *On the Economy*, September 2014.

**A Federal Reserve Bank said "hoard."** That is our best single piece of ammunition, because it is
the establishment describing the phenomenon in our vocabulary, with no villain in sight.

**Honest caveat:** falling velocity is *not* automatically a moral fact. It partly reflects money
demand, financial innovation, low interest rates (holding cash is cheap when rates are near zero),
and — importantly — that M2 itself was expanded enormously. Velocity falling because the
denominator exploded is arithmetic, not vice. Use the number; don't over-explain it.

### 2.2 The paradox of thrift — **[MAINSTREAM, with caveats]**

Keynes's formalisation of a very old idea (it is in Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees*, 1714): an
increase in autonomous saving lowers aggregate demand, lowers output, and can therefore lower *total*
saving. It is an instance of the **fallacy of composition** — what is true of the part is not true of
the whole. Individually rational thrift, collectively harmful.

**This is the rigorous skeleton of "hoarded money is money prevented from doing its job."**

**Caveats to state honestly:** the paradox bites in a demand-constrained economy (a recession, a
liquidity trap). In a supply-constrained economy at full employment, saving finances investment and
thrift is *good*. Keynes was describing a depression. Anyone who says thrift is always bad is wrong,
and a market-literate reader will spot it instantly.

### 2.3 Liquidity preference — **[MAINSTREAM]**

Keynes's account of *why* people hold money rather than spend or lend it: transactions, precaution,
and speculation. The key move for us is that **money's usefulness as a store of value is exactly
what lets it stop circulating.** Money is both the medium of exchange and the thing you can sit on;
those two functions are in tension. That tension is real economics, and it is the whole of our
metaphor.

### 2.4 The marginal propensity to consume — **the rigorous core of our claim — [MAINSTREAM]**

**MPC** = the fraction of an additional dollar of income that gets spent rather than saved. The
empirical finding, replicated across shocks and countries: **MPC is higher for lower-income and
lower-liquidity households.** A dollar given to a poor household re-enters circulation fast; a
dollar given to a rich household mostly stops.

Hard numbers from the 2020 CARES Act stimulus — Baker, Farrokhnia, Meyer, Pagel & Yannelis,
"Income, Liquidity, and the Consumption Response to the 2020 Economic Stimulus Payments," **NBER
Working Paper 27097** (May 2020, rev. Sept 2020),
[nber.org/papers/w27097](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27097):

> "Households respond rapidly to the receipt of stimulus payments, with spending increasing by
> **$0.25–$0.40 per dollar of stimulus** during the first weeks. Households with lower incomes,
> greater income drops, and lower levels of liquidity display stronger responses highlighting the
> importance of targeting. Liquidity plays the most important role, with **no significant spending
> response for households with large checking account balances**."

Their regression by quartile of pre-stimulus bank balance (Table, cols. 4–7):

| Bank-balance quartile | Spending response per $1 |
| --- | --- |
| Q1 (lowest balances) | **0.468** |
| Q2 | 0.376 |
| Q3 | 0.378 |
| Q4 (highest balances) | **0.256** |

**The poorest quartile spent ~83% more of each stimulus dollar than the richest quartile.**

**Best single line for a panel:** *the same dollar does more work in a poorer hand.* That is
mainstream economics, it requires no villain, and a market-liking reader will accept it, because it
is an argument about **velocity and targeting efficiency**, not about desert.

Further reading with real numbers: Jappelli & Pistaferri, "Microeconomic Heterogeneity and
Macroeconomic Shocks," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 32(3), 2018,
[doi:10.1257/jep.32.3.167](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.32.3.167); Krueger, Mitman & Perri,
"Macroeconomics and Household Heterogeneity," NBER WP 22319.

### 2.5 Gesell, demurrage, and Wörgl — **[SERIOUS/HETERODOX]**

**Silvio Gesell** (1862–1930), German-Argentine merchant, proposed **Freigeld** ("free money"):
currency that *decays* if held, so that holding it costs you and spending it doesn't. Mechanism:
**stamped money** — a note retains its value only if a stamp bought at the post office is affixed
each month. **Gesell's proposed rate was 1 per mil per month = 5.4% per annum.**

**Keynes on Gesell, verbatim** — *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936),
Chapter 23, §VI:

> "the strange, unduly neglected prophet Silvio Gesell (1862–1930), whose work contains flashes of
> deep insight and who only just failed to reach down to the essence of the matter."

> "I believe that the future will learn more from the spirit of Gesell than from that of Marx."

> "**The idea behind stamped money is sound.** It is, indeed, possible that means might be found to
> apply it in practice on a modest scale. But there are many difficulties which Gesell did not
> face."

**Keynes's actual objection** (state it — it is the honest part): Gesell had "constructed only half a
theory of the rate of interest" because "the notion of liquidity-preference had escaped him." And
practically: if you stamp currency notes, "**a long series of substitutes would step into their
shoes — bank-money, debts at call, foreign money, jewellery and the precious metals generally.**"
Money is not unique in carrying a liquidity premium; it just has the biggest one. Tax the flow of
one asset and the flow moves to another.

**The Wörgl experiment (Austria, 1932–33)** — the real, documented case. The Tyrolean town of Wörgl,
under mayor **Michael Unterguggenberger**, issued "Certified Compensation Bills" (stamp scrip)
beginning **31 July 1932**, during the Great Depression. Reported results: employment grew and the
town completed public works — new houses, a reservoir, a ski jump, a bridge — while the rest of
Austria stayed in deep unemployment. It attracted the attention of Irving Fisher and French Premier
Édouard Daladier. **Austria's central bank, the Oesterreichische Nationalbank, shut it down on
1 September 1933** to preserve the federal monopoly on legal tender.

**Do not overclaim Wörgl.** It ran ~13 months in a town of ~4,200 people. There was no control group.
Some of the effect is straightforward fiscal stimulus (the town was spending) rather than demurrage
per se. Wikipedia's own entry flags that "the extent of its long-term sustainability and
effectiveness has been under question," citing a 2026 *International Journal of Community Currency
Research* reassessment. **Wörgl is a beautiful story and weak evidence.** Use it as a story. It is
perfect for us precisely because it is a story: a town that made its money rot on purpose, and it
worked well enough that the central bank stopped it.

Modern descendants — local and complementary currencies (Ithaca Hours, Bristol Pound, Sardex,
Chiemgauer, the WIR Bank in Switzerland) — have measurable but *small and local* effects. They are
proof of concept, not proof of scale.

### 2.6 Stock vs flow — is "money is a flow, not a store" defensible? — **[MAINSTREAM]**

**Partly, and we should be precise, because this is where a sharp reader will catch us.**

In economics, **stock vs flow** is a formal distinction. A **stock** is a quantity at a point in
time (wealth, the money supply, capital). A **flow** is a quantity per unit of time (income,
spending, investment). They are different dimensional types: you cannot add them.

So: **the money supply M is unambiguously a stock.** Saying "money is a flow, not a store" is, read
literally, **wrong** — and worse, it is *sloppy in a way that undermines us*, because money's
canonical textbook functions are exactly three: medium of exchange, unit of account, **and store of
value**.

**The defensible version:** money is a stock whose *social value is realised only through the flow
it enables*. Spending (MV, or PQ) is the flow; the pile is the stock. A stock of money that
generates no flow generates no economic activity. That is true, precise, and still poetic.

**Formulation to use:** *"Money is a stock. Its whole point is a flow."*
**Formulation to avoid:** *"Money is not a store of value."* (Textbook-false.)

## 3. The wellbeing threshold — READ THIS BEFORE WRITING

**This is where our instinct is most at risk.** The team's line — "past a threshold, accumulation
stops producing experience" — is the *popular* reading of this literature and it is **substantially
wrong as stated.** Here is what the evidence actually says.

### 3.1 The 2023 adversarial collaboration

Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, **"Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved,"** *PNAS*
120(10), March 2023, [doi:10.1073/pnas.2208661120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208661120).

**The setup.** Kahneman & Deaton (2010) found happiness plateaued above ~$75,000. Killingsworth
(2021) found it kept rising. Two of the authors of those papers ran an **adversarial collaboration**
— joint research with a friendly arbiter — to resolve it.

**Method:** experience sampling via smartphone, **n = 33,391** employed US adults aged 18–65, median
age 33, **median household income $85,000** (25th pct $45,000; 75th pct $137,500).

**What they found:**

- **The plateau is real but only for the unhappy minority.** For the least happy ~15–20% of people,
  happiness rises steeply with log(income) up to **$100,000** and then flattens to a statistically
  non-significant slope. (5th percentile: slope 2.34 below $100k, 0.25 above — n.s. 15th percentile:
  1.90 below, 0.34 above — n.s.)
- **For everyone else it keeps rising, and for the happiest it accelerates.** "For the happiest 30%
  of people... happiness rises with log(income) at an **accelerated** rate beyond 100k" (85th
  percentile: acceleration significant at P = 0.023).
- The famous **$75,000 figure is an artifact**: "The threshold of $75,000, which has been frequently
  quoted, is simply the **midpoint of the '60 to 90K' income category**."

**The quotable sentence, and the most useful thing in the paper for us:**

> "This income threshold may represent the point beyond which the miseries that remain are not
> alleviated by high income. **Heartbreak, bereavement, and clinical depression** may be examples of
> such miseries."

**The anti-overclaim sentence we must respect:**

> "the relationship is **weak**, even if statistically robust. The correlation between average
> happiness and log(income) is **0.09**... and the difference between the medians of happiness at
> household incomes of **$15,000 and $250,000 is about five points on a 100-point scale**."

**Read that again.** A **16× difference in income** buys about **5 points on a 100-point happiness
scale**. That is a stunning, surprising, *true* statistic — and it supports our argument far better
than the false threshold claim does. It says: money buys experience, but at a brutal exchange rate.

The paper also drew published critiques and a reply: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, "Reply to
Rohrer and Wenz and Arslan," *PNAS*, Oct 2024,
[doi:10.1073/pnas.2322160121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322160121). The finding is contested at
the edges. Don't present it as settled.

### 3.2 The strongest evidence *against* a satiation point

Stevenson & Wolfers, **"Subjective Well-Being and Income: Is There Any Evidence of Satiation?"**,
NBER WP 18992 / *AER P&P*, 2013, [nber.org/papers/w18992](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18992):

> "Many scholars have argued that once 'basic needs' have been met, higher income is no longer
> associated with higher subjective well-being. We assess the validity of this claim... **we find no
> support for this claim.** The relationship between well-being and income is roughly linear-log and
> does not diminish as incomes rise. **If there is a satiation point, we are yet to reach it.**"

**We should quote this against ourselves.** It is intellectually honest, it pre-empts the obvious
objection, and — crucially — **it does not actually damage our argument**, because our argument does
not depend on satiation. See §3.4.

### 3.3 Evaluative vs experienced wellbeing — **[MAINSTREAM], and it matters**

Two different things get called "happiness":

- **Evaluative wellbeing** — "how satisfied are you with your life?" (Cantril ladder, life
  satisfaction). Reflective, comparative, judgement-based.
- **Experienced wellbeing** — "how do you feel *right now*?" (experience sampling, day
  reconstruction). Momentary affect.

**They behave differently with income.** Evaluative wellbeing tracks log(income) robustly and
apparently without limit — it is partly a *ranking* judgement, so relative position matters.
Experienced wellbeing is flatter and noisier. Kahneman & Deaton's original title says it exactly:
"High income improves **evaluation of life** but not **emotional well-being**"
([doi:10.1073/pnas.1011492107](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107)).

**For our purposes this is the key distinction and we should build on it, not on the threshold.**
"The yield is experience" is a claim about *experienced* wellbeing. The evidence that money keeps
buying life-*satisfaction* forever is exactly consistent with the possibility that what it is
buying, up there, is **rank** rather than **experience**.

### 3.4 Diminishing marginal utility — the argument that actually works — **[MAINSTREAM]**

**Diminishing marginal utility of income** is textbook, uncontroversial, and sufficient. Formally:
utility is a concave function of income, u′(y) > 0 and u″(y) < 0. Economists standardly work with
**log utility** (u = ln y) or CRRA utility. Log utility means: **equal *proportional* increases in
income produce equal increases in utility.**

**This is the whole argument, and it needs no threshold at all.** Under log utility:

- Going from **$15,000 to $30,000** produces the *same* welfare gain as going from
  **$500,000 to $1,000,000**.
- Therefore **$15,000 given to someone on $15,000 produces as much welfare as $500,000 given to
  someone on $500,000** — a **33× efficiency ratio**.
- Extend it: a dollar to someone on $30,000 is worth, in welfare terms, roughly **1,000×** a dollar
  to someone on $30,000,000.

**This is the rigorous version of "to an idealist, a billionaire is withholding other people's
ability to experience" — and it survives contact with an economist**, because it is just concavity.
It does not require a satiation point. It does not require anyone to be bad. It does not even
require anyone to have done anything wrong. **It is arithmetic about curvature.**

**Use diminishing marginal utility, not the threshold.** It is stronger, it is unimpeachable, and it
is compatible with Stevenson & Wolfers being completely right that happiness never satiates.

**Honest caveat:** interpersonal utility comparison — asserting that a dollar means more to A than
to B — is philosophically contested and was deliberately expelled from mid-century welfare
economics (the ordinalist revolution). Everyone does it anyway, because policy is impossible without
it, and it underpins all optimal-taxation theory (Mirrlees, Diamond & Saez). But a hostile economist
will raise it. The answer: we're making a moral argument informed by economics, not deriving a
Pareto improvement.

## 4. The moral philosophy of "enough"

### 4.1 Sufficientarianism — Harry Frankfurt — **the best home for our position**

Harry Frankfurt, **"Equality as a Moral Ideal,"** *Ethics* 98(1), 1987, pp. 21–43. The **doctrine of
sufficiency**:

> "**What is important from the moral point of view is not that everyone should have the same but
> that each should have enough.** If everyone had enough, it would be of no moral consequence
> whether some had more than others."
> — Frankfurt 1987, p. 21

> "**The fundamental error of egalitarianism lies in supposing that it is morally important whether
> one person has less than another regardless of how much either of them has.**"
> — Frankfurt 1987, p. 34

> "It is whether people have good lives, and not how their lives compare with the lives of others."
> — Frankfurt 1997, p. 6

(Quotations via the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, "Equality," §6.2,
[plato.stanford.edu/entries/equality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/equality/); Frankfurt
expanded this into *On Inequality*, Princeton UP, 2015.)

**Why this is our philosophical home, exactly as the brief hoped.** Frankfurt is **explicitly not an
egalitarian**. He attacks equality as a moral ideal *from the direction of caring about people's
actual lives*. He gives us:

- **No villains.** Nobody is condemned for having more. The rich person is not the problem; the
  person without enough is the problem.
- **Markets intact.** Sufficiency says nothing against markets, profit, or inequality *per se*.
- **A positive goal.** "Everyone should reach the level where material problems are solved" is
  *literally the doctrine of sufficiency*. It is a named, respected, 40-year-old position in
  analytic political philosophy.
- **A diagnosis of why inequality feels wrong:** Frankfurt's own move — the reason inequality
  bothers us is usually that it is *evidence* someone lacks enough, and we've confused the symptom
  for the disease.

**Standard objections to know:** (1) the **threshold problem** — where is "enough," and isn't any
line arbitrary? (2) the **indifference-above-the-line problem** — sufficientarianism seems to say we
should be wholly indifferent between a world where everyone has just enough and one where a few have
astronomically more, which many find implausible. (3) Frankfurt arguably *understates* how much
concentrated wealth converts into political power, which then determines whether others reach
sufficiency at all. Objection (3) is the bridge to Robeyns.

### 4.2 Limitarianism — Ingrid Robeyns

Ingrid Robeyns (Utrecht), *Limitarianism: The Case Against Extreme Wealth* (Astra House / Allen Lane,
2024); academically, **"Why Limitarianism?", *Journal of Political Philosophy* 30(2), 2022**,
[doi:10.1111/jopp.12275](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopp.12275); and "Having Too Much" (2017).

**The thesis:** it is **morally impermissible to be excessively rich** — to hold more economic
resources than some "riches line." Note carefully what it is *not*: limitarianism "differs from
socialism in that it does not negate the idea of private property, nor completely bar the
accumulation of wealth. It rather sets to determine and enforce a limit on the accumulation thereof."

**Robeyns's two main arguments:**
1. **The democratic argument** — concentrated wealth converts into disproportionate political power,
   corroding democracy. (This one is about *structure*, not *sin*.)
2. **The unmet-urgent-needs argument** — surplus wealth above the riches line could meet urgent
   needs and remedy global disadvantage, so holding it is not permissible.

**Objections to state:** the riches-line is as arbitrary as any threshold (see Volacu & Dumitru's
exchange with Robeyns, *Philosophia* 2018,
[doi:10.1007/s11406-018-0030-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11406-018-0030-6)); "Limitarianism: Pattern,
Principle, or Presumption?" (*JAPP* 2021, [doi:10.1111/japp.12502](https://doi.org/10.1111/japp.12502))
questions whether it is a genuine distinct principle or just prioritarianism wearing a hat; and there
are the standard incentive/innovation objections.

**Brief-fit warning: limitarianism is closer to having a villain than sufficientarianism is.** It
says being very rich is *impermissible* — a claim about persons. Frankfurt says only that everyone
should have enough — a claim about a floor. **Lead with Frankfurt. Keep Robeyns for the political-
power argument only**, where the target is a structural dynamic rather than a person.

### 4.3 Singer and the demandingness problem

Peter Singer, **"Famine, Affluence, and Morality,"** *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(3), 1972.
The principle, verbatim:

> "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing
> anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it."

The drowning child, verbatim:

> "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I ought to wade in and pull
> the child out. This will mean getting my clothes muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death
> of the child would presumably be a very bad thing."

And the sting — Singer knows exactly what he has done:

> "The uncontroversial appearance of the principle just stated is deceptive. If it were acted upon,
> even in its qualified form, **our lives, our society, and our world would be fundamentally
> changed.**"

He then removes the two escape hatches: **proximity** ("It makes no moral difference whether the
person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know,
ten thousand miles away") and **numbers** ("Should I consider that I am less obliged to pull the
drowning child out of the pond if on looking around I see other people... who have also noticed the
child but are doing nothing?").

**The strongest critiques, which we should honour:**
- **Demandingness.** Singer's principle has no stopping point short of reducing yourself to marginal
  utility. Most people take this as a *reductio*.
- **Susan Wolf, "Moral Saints," *Journal of Philosophy* 79(8), 1982.** A life of total moral
  devotion is not an attractive human life. The moral saint has no room for the particular loves,
  projects, and idiosyncratic passions that make a person a person. **This is a genuinely important
  corrective for us** — it says a good life is not an optimised life, which is very close to "the
  yield is experience."
- **Post-FTX critiques of effective altruism.** The collapse of FTX in 2022 (Sam Bankman-Fried, an
  EA figurehead, convicted of fraud in 2023) triggered a broad reassessment: that expected-value
  maximisation over the long run licenses ends-justify-means reasoning; that "earning to give"
  launders the accumulation it claims to redeem; and that longtermism displaces present suffering
  with speculative future populations.

**Brief-fit warning: Singer is a villain-generator.** His argument makes every affluent reader
culpable, personally, continuously. That is the *opposite* of our register. **Use the drowning child
as an image; don't adopt the obligation structure.** Our version is: the pond is drained by design,
and nobody meant it.

### 4.4 Aristotle — *oikonomia* vs *chrematistike* — **[SOFT/foundational]**

*Politics*, Book I, chs. 8–10. Aristotle distinguishes **household management** (*oikonomia*) —
acquisition for the sake of living well, which is **naturally bounded** — from **wealth-getting**
(*chrematistike*) — acquisition for its own sake, which is **unbounded**.

Verbatim (Ellis translation, Gutenberg #6762):

> "There is also another species of acquisition which they particularly call pecuniary, and with
> great propriety; and by this indeed it seems that **there are no bounds to riches and wealth**."
> [1257a]

> "that economy which is natural, and whose object is to provide food, is **not like this unlimited
> in its extent, but has its bounds**."

> "usury is most reasonably detested, as it is increasing our fortune **by money itself, and not
> employing it for the purpose it was originally intended, namely exchange**. And this is the
> explanation of the name (TOKOS), which means **the breeding of money**. For as offspring resemble
> their parents, so usury is **money bred of money**. Whence of all forms of money-making it is
> most against nature." [1258b]

**"Money bred of money" is a superb phrase for us.** Note also the error Aristotle names: making
money "when it was intended for exchange" — i.e. *taking the medium for the end*. That is our whole
thesis in a 2,300-year-old sentence.

**Caveat:** Aristotle's condemnation of interest is *wrong* by any modern standard and was used for
centuries to justify real harm. We're borrowing his distinction, not his monetary policy.

### 4.5 Epicurus, the Stoics, and Sen

**[SOFT]** Epicurus: pleasure is the absence of pain and disturbance (*ataraxia*), and natural and
necessary desires are few and easily satisfied; it is the *unnecessary* desires (wealth, fame) that
are unbounded and therefore unsatisfiable. Seneca — himself extraordinarily rich, which is
either hypocrisy or the most interesting data point in Stoicism — argued wealth is a "preferred
indifferent": fine to have, fatal to need.

**Amartya Sen's capability approach** — **[MAINSTREAM, Nobel-respectable]** — is the rigorous
descendant. Sen (Nobel 1998) and Martha Nussbaum argue that wellbeing should be assessed in terms of
**capabilities** (what a person is actually able to do and be) and **functionings** (what they
actually do and are), **not** in terms of resources held or utility felt. Income is only an
instrument, and the rate at which it converts into capability varies enormously between people
(disability, location, social norms — "conversion factors"). See
[plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/);
Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999); Nussbaum, *Creating Capabilities* (2011). It underpins the UN
**Human Development Index**.

**Sen is arguably the single most defensible version of "the yield is experience"** — with the
refinement that the yield is *capability*, i.e. freedom to have experiences, not the experiences
themselves. Nobel-credentialed, no villains, market-compatible.

## 5. "Money as prisoner" — the image, and its ancestry

The image of money as *blocked energy*, and hoarding as *stagnation*, is very old and very
cross-cultural. This is the best-stocked section in the whole brief.

### 5.1 The Parable of the Talents — **the servant who buried it is the one condemned**

Matthew 25:14–30 (NIV). A master entrusts three servants with five, two, and one talent. Two trade
and double; the third:

> "**went off, dug a hole in the ground, and hid his master's money.**" (v. 18)

His defence, and the verdict:

> "'So I was afraid and went out and **hid your gold in the ground**. See, here is what belongs to
> you.' His master replied, '**You wicked, lazy servant!**... Well then, you should have put my
> money on deposit with the bankers, so that when I returned I would have received it back with
> interest.'" (vv. 25–27)

**This is a startlingly exact scriptural fit.** The condemned man **loses nothing**. He returns the
full principal. He is condemned **purely for taking money out of circulation.** Note the delicious
detail that the master explicitly endorses **interest** — the very thing Aristotle and the medieval
church condemned.

**Standard readings, for honesty:** the parable is conventionally read as being about *spiritual*
gifts and faithful stewardship pending the master's return, not about monetary policy — "talent" as
"ability" derives from this parable. But a significant minority reading (William Herzog, Richard
Rohrbaugh) inverts it: in a first-century peasant economy the third servant is the *hero*, refusing
to participate in an extractive system, and the "hard man harvesting where he has not sown" is a
portrait of exploitation, not God. **Both readings are useful to us** — the second is a built-in
counter-argument we can voice and answer.

### 5.2 Zakat — hoarding condemned by name

**Zakat** is the third of the Five Pillars of Islam, ranked in the Qur'an immediately after prayer.
Customarily **2.5% (1/40) of disposable wealth annually**, levied on *held wealth* above a minimum
threshold (**nisab**) — crucially **a wealth tax, not an income tax**. Because it taxes the stock
every year, **zakat is functionally a 2.5% annual demurrage on idle wealth.** It is Gesell's stamp
scrip, arrived at 1,300 years earlier by a different route.

The Qur'an condemns hoarding (*kanz*) explicitly — **Surah At-Tawbah 9:34–35** (Sahih International):

> "And **those who hoard gold and silver and spend it not in the way of Allah** — give them tidings
> of a painful punishment. The Day when it will be heated in the fire of Hell and seared therewith
> will be their foreheads, their flanks, and their backs, [it will be said], '**This is what you
> hoarded for yourselves, so taste what you used to hoard.**'"

Note the image: **the hoard itself becomes the instrument of punishment, heated and applied to the
body.** That is money-as-prisoner inverted — the hoarder imprisoned by the hoard. Visually
extraordinary for a comic panel.

Islamic finance also prohibits **riba** (interest/usury) and **gharar** (excessive uncertainty), and
the underlying logic across all three prohibitions is consistent: **money should not earn merely by
existing; it should earn by participating in real activity and real risk.**

### 5.3 Jubilee and shmita — the debt reset as institution

**Shmita** (Hebrew: "release") — Exodus 23:10–11, Leviticus 25:2–7, Deuteronomy 15:1–3. Every
**seventh year** the land lies fallow, and **personal debts are considered forgiven at sunset on 29
Elul.** The debt release is not land-dependent, so it applies everywhere.

**Jubilee** — Leviticus 25:8–13. After seven cycles of seven (49 years), the fiftieth year:

> "You shall make the fiftieth year holy, and **proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its
> inhabitants**. It shall be a jubilee to you; and **each of you shall return to his own property**,
> and each of you shall return to his family."

Indentured servants freed, debts forgiven, land returned to original families. Leviticus 25:23 gives
the reason: **"The land must not be sold permanently."**

**The structural insight, and it is a good one:** these are **built-in circuit breakers against the
ratchet of accumulation** — periodic, scheduled, impersonal, blaming nobody. Not a revolution: a
maintenance cycle. **This is an outstandingly no-villain framing** — the reset is in the calendar,
like a leap year. (Honest note: historians dispute how consistently Jubilee was ever actually
practised; it may be substantially aspirational law.)

### 5.4 Medieval usury doctrine — what it actually objected to

The scholastic objection was **not** "profit is bad." It was narrower and more interesting:

- **Money is barren** (*nummus non parit nummos* — money does not beget money). Charging for its use
  is charging for something that produces nothing, inherited straight from Aristotle's *tokos*.
- **Selling time**, which belongs to God alone, not to the lender.
- **Risk-free return**: the lender who bears no risk earns no just title to gain. Partnerships
  (*societas*), where the financier shares real risk, were **permitted**. This is precisely the
  Islamic *mudarabah*/*musharakah* logic.

**The doctrine did not object to gain. It objected to gain without participation.** That is a
sharper, more defensible, and much more useful formulation than "usury is evil" — and it is
completely compatible with liking markets.

### 5.5 Dante — the hoarders and the spendthrifts in the same circle

***Inferno* VII** — the Fourth Circle. Longfellow translation (Gutenberg #1004). **This is the single
best no-villain artifact in the entire brief.**

Dante sees two crowds rolling enormous weights against each other with their chests, colliding,
turning, and rolling back forever:

> "Here saw I people, more than elsewhere, many, / On one side and the other, with great howls, /
> Rolling weights forward by main force of chest. / They clashed together, and then at that point /
> Each one turned backward, rolling retrograde, / Crying, **'Why keepest?' and, 'Why squanderest
> thou?'**"

Virgil's diagnosis — and note that **both groups have made the same error**, a failure of *measure*:

> "**All of them were asquint / In intellect in the first life, so much / That there with measure
> they no spending made.**"

The two are told apart only by their gesture in the resurrection:

> "**These from the sepulchre shall rise again / With the fist closed, and these with tresses
> shorn.**"

> "**Ill giving and ill keeping** the fair world / Have ta'en from them, and placed them in this
> scuffle"

And the closing line, which is the best single lyric in this document:

> "**For all the gold that is beneath the moon, / Or ever has been, of these weary souls / Could
> never make a single one repose.**"

**Why this is perfect for us.** Dante puts the **miser and the spendthrift in the same circle**,
punishes them **identically**, and has them **shout their two opposite accusations at each other
forever** — because they committed the *same* sin from opposite directions: they had no *measure*.
Neither is the villain. Both are wrong about what money is for. That is our thesis, staged as
slapstick, in 1320.

## 6. Materialist vs idealist — the point of an economy

### 6.1 Kuznets's warning — verbatim, from the primary source

The most important citation in this section, and we should get it exactly right because it is
frequently misquoted. **Simon Kuznets — who built the US national income accounts and won the 1971
Nobel for it — warned Congress about them in the very document that delivered them.**

*National Income, 1929–32*, **Senate Document No. 124, 73rd Congress, 2nd Session (1934), p. 7**.
Verified against the scanned original at FRASER, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis
([fraser.stlouisfed.org](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/national-income-1929-1932-971)):

> "Economic welfare cannot be adequately measured unless the personal distribution of income is
> known. And no income measurement undertakes to estimate the reverse side of income, that is, the
> intensity and unpleasantness of effort going into the earning of income. **The welfare of a nation
> can, therefore, scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income as defined above.**"

He goes on to name the exact abuse:

> "we find all too commonly such inferences that a decline of 30 percent in the national income...
> means a 30 percent decline in the total productivity of the nation, and a corresponding decline in
> its welfare."

**Note the first clause especially — "unless the personal distribution of income is known."** The
inventor of GDP said, in the founding document, that **the aggregate is meaningless without the
distribution.** That is our entire distributional argument, endorsed in advance by the man who built
the number.

(He said it again in 1962: "Distinctions must be kept in mind between quantity and quality of
growth... Goals for more growth should specify more growth of what and for what." — *The New
Republic*, 20 October 1962.)

### 6.2 Robert F. Kennedy, University of Kansas, 18 March 1968 — verbatim

The most famous statement of the idea. Transcript via the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library.
Full passage:

> "Too much and for too long, we seemed to have surrendered personal excellence and community values
> in the mere accumulation of material things. Our Gross National Product, now, is over $800 billion
> dollars a year, but that Gross National Product — if we judge the United States of America by that
> — that Gross National Product counts air pollution and cigarette advertising, and ambulances to
> clear our highways of carnage. It counts special locks for our doors and the jails for the people
> who break them. It counts the destruction of the redwood and the loss of our natural wonder in
> chaotic sprawl. It counts napalm and counts nuclear warheads and armored cars for the police to
> fight the riots in our cities. It counts Whitman's rifle and Speck's knife, and the television
> programs which glorify violence in order to sell toys to our children.
>
> Yet the gross national product does not allow for the health of our children, the quality of their
> education or the joy of their play. It does not include the beauty of our poetry or the strength
> of our marriages, the intelligence of our public debate or the integrity of our public officials.
> **It measures neither our wit nor our courage, neither our wisdom nor our learning, neither our
> compassion nor our devotion to our country, it measures everything in short, except that which
> makes life worthwhile.** And it can tell us everything about America except why we are proud that
> we are Americans."

Immediately before it, the line that names our threshold argument better than any philosopher does:

> "But even if we act to erase material poverty, there is another greater task, it is to confront
> **the poverty of satisfaction — purpose and dignity — that afflicts us all.**"

**"The poverty of satisfaction" is possibly the best three-word phrase in this entire document.**

### 6.3 The Beyond-GDP / wellbeing-economy literature — **[SERIOUS, mixed quality]**

- **Doughnut Economics** — Kate Raworth (Oxford), *A Safe and Just Space for Humanity* (Oxfam, 2012)
  and *Doughnut Economics* (2017). A ring between a **social foundation** (below which people lack
  life's essentials) and an **ecological ceiling** (planetary boundaries). The goal is the ring, not
  growth. Adopted by Amsterdam (2020), Brussels, and others. A 2018 empirical application across 150
  countries found **not a single country meets its citizens' basic needs at a globally sustainable
  level of resource use** — a genuinely arresting statistic.
  **State the criticism:** the model has been criticised for lacking empirical support; Acharya,
  Geloso & Psurek formalised and tested its central thesis that capitalism and economic freedom
  create social and ecological imbalances and **found no support** — often the reverse. Given our
  no-villain brief, this critique is *convenient*: it supports "markets aren't the enemy."
- **New Zealand's Wellbeing Budget (2019)** — the first developed economy to set its budget
  priorities by wellbeing rather than GDP, structured on the Treasury's **Living Standards
  Framework** ([treasury.govt.nz](https://www.treasury.govt.nz/publications/wellbeing-budget/wellbeing-budget-2019)).
  Real, institutional, and the honest assessment is *mixed* — critics call it more a reporting
  framework than a decision rule.
- **Bhutan's Gross National Happiness** — instituted as a constitutional goal in 2008. **Use with
  care and always with the caveat.** GNH has been described by critics as a propaganda instrument:
  before democratisation, the Bhutanese state carried out mass expulsion of ethnic Nepali Hindus
  (Lhotshampa) **in the name of GNH cultural preservation** — roughly one-sixth of the population.
  **Citing Bhutan uncritically would be a serious own-goal.** If we use it, use it as a cautionary
  tale: *a wellbeing metric is only as good as who defines wellbeing.* That is a genuinely
  BadCode-shaped irony.
- **Sen & Nussbaum's capability approach** (§4.5) and the **Human Development Index** are the most
  institutionally successful version of "measure the flourishing, not the flow of cash."

## 8. Recommendations — how to use this without lying

1. **Drop the threshold argument. Use diminishing marginal utility instead.** "Past a point money
   stops producing experience" is *not* well supported and the best study (Stevenson & Wolfers)
   contradicts it. "A dollar does more for someone who has fewer of them" is unimpeachable,
   textbook, needs no villain, and gets us everything we wanted. **This is the single most important
   correction in this brief.**
2. **The strongest true version of our thesis is about the exchange rate, not the ceiling.** Money
   never stops buying experience — it just buys it at a catastrophically worsening rate. 16× income
   for 5 points out of 100. That is *more* damning than a plateau and it is *true*.
3. **Lead philosophically with Frankfurt's sufficiency**, not Robeyns's limitarianism and not
   Singer's obligation. Frankfurt is explicitly anti-egalitarian, market-neutral, and villain-free.
4. **"Money is a stock. Its whole point is a flow."** Never "money is not a store of value."
5. **The MPC evidence is the bridge between the metaphor and the economics.** It is literally the
   measurement of how fast money flows from different hands. Everything poetic we want to say about
   circulation has a number attached to it, and the number is in the *AER* and NBER.
6. **Dante is the best single artifact in this brief.** Hoarder and spendthrift, same circle, same
   sentence, shouting opposite accusations forever, because both lacked *measure*. If we need one
   image for "there are no villains, only an error about what money is for," that is it.
7. **Never cite Bhutan without the caveat**, and never adopt Honda's manifestation register.
8. **When in doubt, quote Kuznets.** The man who built GDP telling Congress it can't measure welfare
   *without the distribution* is the most authoritative, least arguable, most surprising sentence
   available to us — and it is 92 years old.
