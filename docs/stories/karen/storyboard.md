## Act 1 — Meet Karen (the bait)

The job of Act 1: set up an archetypal Karen the audience loves to hate — *she is not
the hero* — so that her conviction later reads as completely in character. At this point
in the story, a viewer should be thinking "oh, it's just a story about a Karen." That's
the bait.

### 1.1 The zoom-in — KEEP

- **On screen:** Manhattan, zoom in to the office building, find Karen mid-flow being
  a knob to everyone.
- **Narrator:** total authority, dry: *"She's a bit of a knob, Karen, isn't she."*
- Works as-is in the draft. Done.

### 1.2 Karen at work — REDO (narrate our way in)

- **On screen:** a couple of quick office beats. The draft scene just before the cleaner
  (the one where the physics went sideways) is **CUT** as a shot, but the idea survives:
  we narrate our way into her character rather than act it out.
- **Narrator carries it, sarcastic:** *"Karen giving her usual morning motivational
  speech."* / *"Karen listening intently to feedback."* — while the visuals show the
  opposite.

### 1.3 The fish lunch and the cleaner — KEEP + one switch

- **On screen:** Karen's daily fish lunch. The microwave. Karen condescending to the
  cleaner — as usual, pointing out the spot she missed.
- **The switch:** the moment the camera goes to the inside-the-microwave view, the
  perspective flips to the cleaner — *"and the cleaner thinks: what a knob."* We've done
  enough of "Karen's a prick"; this shot is **what it feels like to be on the receiving
  end of Karen**.
- This is the end of the setup. That's the right amount of length for Act 1's
  character work — resist adding more.

### 1.4 The firing — NEW (replaces the old firing scene entirely)

The most important new scene. Longer than the old one — it earns it. **Sean AI enters
the story here**, and this scene is the bookend that pays off at the end (§4.3): same
UI, same font, same icon.

- **Setup (third person):** Karen's manager calls her into a meeting room. The manager
  *knows* what's about to happen to Karen — he does not know it's about to happen to
  him. Narrator can do the walking-in: *"Even Karen has a manager. And he'd like a
  word."* Boom — they're seated at the desk. **Prompt note: Karen has a laptop on
  her lap.**
- **The ask:** manager, awkward: *"Karen, I'm sorry to call you in. I really need you
  to take a look at your laptop."* (Odd request — why isn't he just telling her? It's
  quietly setting up Sean.)
- **The transition (the fun bit):** from third person, cut to Karen's POV looking back
  at the manager → camera tilts down to her lap → the laptop lid opens → **the laptop
  screen becomes the whole frame** (the video-game trick: the screen zooms to swallow
  the viewport). We are now inside Sean's UI.
- **The screen:** design a fake computer UI that *is* Sean AI. Minimal. An **8-bit
  pixel-art moniker** (parody of the Claude Code symbol) with "Sean AI" — iconography,
  not a face. Never humanize him. Message to this effect (exact copy still open):
  > *I am Sean AI. I now run the HR department. I'm sorry, Karen — I can do your job
  > from here. You're redundant, effective today.*
  Important limit: he runs **the HR department**, not the whole company. Don't explain
  what Sean is or how he got the power. Stay out of that room entirely.
- **Joke to weave in:** Karen, to the laptop: *"Can I speak to your manager?"*
- **The reverse:** the screen: *"Now, please pass the laptop to your manager."* Cut to
  the laptop-camera POV looking back at Karen — shocked/dismayed (avoid the AI
  wide-eyes shocked face). Rotate 180° to the manager. Sean: *"Sorry mate — you too."*
  The manager who was about to offer to escort her out is also fired.
- **Optional flourish:** freeze-frame, Matrix-style 360° around the pair of them
  mid-"what the f—". If it works, it's brilliant; if not, eject cord.
- **Land it hard (don't rush past it):** narrator: *"Isn't it ironic — even Karen's
  manager no longer has a manager."*
- **Rules for the room:** nobody ever types. They speak *to* the laptop; Sean answers
  in text on screen (narrator can voice-over what Sean says). The laptop is the third
  character in the room — a huge character with tiny screen time, and the camera work
  is how we balance that discrepancy.

### 1.5 The bar, in Polaroids — REDO (compress hard)

- **On screen:** hard cut from the firing to nighttime — Karen and her friend already
  in a bar, one or two drinks in, not gone yet. The friend consoling her. A couple of
  seconds, no more.
- **Narrator gag:** *"Karen sensibly decided to have just the one and get an early
  night."*
- **Then the Polaroid compilation:** ~10 shots, roughly half a second each, of the two
  of them across different bars getting progressively smashed — styled as Polaroids.
  The whole premise (went out, got hammered) lands in about four seconds. All the
  draft's long hanging-out footage is **CUT** in favour of this.

### 1.6 The phone in the river — KEEP (retake till the physics behave)

- **On screen:** very drunk Karen drops her phone in the water. The draft scene is
  right — just re-roll the drop until it looks true.
- This is a load-bearing beat: *we* see where the phone went. Karen won't remember.

### 1.7 The morning after — NEW (the end of Act 1; get this right)

The scene that commits Karen to the phone box. More important than the draft ever
gave it room to be.

- **Beat by beat:** Karen wakes, thumping hangover, no memory of last night. Narrator
  carries it. *"Where's my phone?"* — she can't even ring anyone to find out what
  happened. Which is how she ends up at **the phone box**, calling her friend.
- **Cut to the friend** waking up in bed — mirror of the shot we just did with Karen
  (fun, cheap, works).
- **The call:** Karen: *"What happened last night? I can't remember anything. And I'm
  going to be late for work."* The friend cuts her off: *"Karen. Yesterday you got
  sacked. By an AI. And you dropped your phone in the river."* (Whether Karen genuinely
  forgot the firing stays an open question — very common one-too-many experience;
  don't over-explain.)
- **The pep talk (the hinge of the whole story):** Karen: *"So what do I do now?"*
  Friend: *"You complain. You're Karen — if there's one person alive who can get on
  that phone and not give up until she reaches whoever can fix this, it's you. What
  else are you doing today?"* — *"…Nothing."* — *"Then get ringing."*
- **Why it works:** we spent Act 1 building the archetype, so the audience *already
  believes* she has this conviction. The scene just aims it. Exit feeling: *I would
  not want to be the person who answers her next call.* She is not leaving that chair
  until she gets what she wants.

## Act 2 — The Great Escalation (the core of the film)

The most important chunk of the whole story. **This section is the song made
visual** — [`songs/all-day-to-complain.md`](./songs/all-day-to-complain.md) is its
storyboard. Karen climbs the management ladder from a Midtown phone box over **nine
months**, and if people clock that the film and the song share the same escalation
structure rung for rung, we've pulled off something genuinely great.

### The formula (repeat 3–4 times)

1. **Aerial Manhattan** — photoreal, looking down over the city (no corkboard, no
   pins — we're trying the realistic version first; the pin-map stays in the back
   pocket if it doesn't land).
2. **Zoom down** into one specific building.
3. **Cut inside:** a functionary at a desk answers the phone. *"Hello, I'm —"* (title).
4. **Two or three exchanges max**, intercut with Karen in the box. Karen: *"I need to
   know why I've been fired and how I'm meant to pay my rent."* Functionary blocks.
   Karen: *"I want to speak to your manager."*
5. **Zoom out** — and while we're high over the city, **the weather changes** (rain →
   snow → sun). Time passes in the wide shots.
6. **Zoom into the next building.** Next rung.

### The rules of the section

- **The narrator does the talking.** We are not conducting full conversations — *"they
  talked; it didn't go well"* is a complete scene. What each person *says* matters far
  less than **who they are at which point in the sequence**. This is the anti-slop
  device: no inventing new dialogue to say the same thing five times.
- **Mirror the song.** Lift the ladder and as many one-to-one lines as fit: front desk
  → supervisor (*"she's not free until three" / "Three's fine. Three is lovely to
  me"*) → borough office → councilman's assistant → State house → somebody's aide's
  aide → Chief of Staff. Compress to 3–4 on-screen rungs; the narrator can imply the
  rest. The song's hold-music, the *"I've got all day to complain"* shout — all of it
  is available as score and dialogue.
- **The phone box is a fixed point.** Jack picks a real location on Google Maps —
  Midtown Manhattan, outside a subway entrance — and every single generation uses it.
  (The song says Lexington & Third.) The world changes around the box; the box never
  moves.
- **Nine months, believably.** A line of narration carries it: *"I'm sorry madam, we
  can only prioritise callers at the front of the phone queue."* She holds. That's the
  point of her.
- **No crowd yet.** She is completely alone in this act. That's the jeopardy — something
  incredible is unfolding and *nobody knows*. The draft's lone shot of her just staring
  out of the box belongs here. By the end of the act the audience should be quietly on
  her side and feeling the weight of it.
- **Easter egg (make the AI thing less subtle):** somewhere in the compilation, a
  newspaper — ideally the same prop newspaper as Camping — with an AI-taking-jobs
  headline. Background, not foreground.

## Act 3 — Viral (Ari, the train, the crowd)

### 3.1 The Ari cameo — REDO (five seconds, total)

- **On screen:** Ari at Home happens to wander past the phone box — he is not looking
  for anything. Karen, mid-hold, shouts her line: *"I've got ALL DAY to complain — all
  day to complain!"* Ari stops, gets his phone out, records a few seconds — *"oh
  sh—"* — and walks off.
- **Narrator:** *"Ari just heard a great sample."* That evening (he's *Ari at Home*)
  he cuts the tune and releases it. It goes viral — 20 million views.
- **We never see him again.** He's off doing his next thing. Resist making him part of
  the attraction; the believable version is the accidental one. (His big outing is the
  **music video**, not this film — see [`characters/ari.md`](./characters/ari.md).)

### 3.2 The train — KEEP (as-is; play the real tune)

- **On screen:** two riders on the subway, listening to the actual track — our real
  song, in-world. *"You heard this Karen tune? It's gone viral."* — *"Mate. She's
  still there. The phone-box lady. Still. There."*
- That's the joke of the act: the tune went stratospheric **and she never left the
  box**.

### 3.3 The crowd — NEW (the map trick, reused for people)

- **On screen:** the two train guys get off at the stop by the phone box — the subway
  entrance is right there (see the fixed location, Act 2) — and become **the first two
  people in the crowd**: *"Karen! Good luck with the phoning!"* Their role ends there.
- **Then the drone trick:** altitude up → down → up → down; each time we come back
  down the crowd is bigger, until it's massive. Same grammar as the Act 2 zooms, but
  now we're moving through *time* at one spot, not across the map. ("Please don't look
  while we change the set.")
- **Pace:** fast. This is a viral flash-mob thing — it happens over a day, not
  seasons. News vans at the end of it.

## Act 4 — The Ultimate Manager

### 4.1 The TV studio — NEW

- **A whole new world.** No visual link back to the crowd — cutting cold to a place
  we've never seen is the Game-of-Thrones-Dorne effect, and it works. Late-night-show
  production office, tonight's problem on the table.
- **The setup:** the executives are panicking. The President is on the show tonight;
  everyone's attention span is shredded by AI slop on TikTok; they need something
  *short* and *viral*. Beat of silence (narrator: the traditional moment of silence
  that follows "we need an idea").
- **The intern** — young, currently making the coffee — *"…have you not seen Karen on
  TikTok?"*
- **The executives then discover their own genius** (we land the joke *here*, before
  the call ever happens): *"What if we escalate her… to the ultimate manager? The one
  man with no manager above him."* They are very pleased with themselves.

### 4.2 The show — NEW

- **On screen:** *"Ladies and gentlemen — the President of the United States."* He
  sits. Host: we're going to start with something light for our younger audience.
  *"Have you heard of this lady — Karen? Nine months in a phone box. Says she will not
  leave until she speaks to the manager. And sir, we could think of no better ultimate
  manager than yourself. Would you take her call, live, right now?"*
- **Believability is free here:** presidents do viral stunts — podcasts, late night,
  election season. Optional colour: a glance at the earpiece / an aide mouthing "this
  is good for votes." The narrator can note the chief of media relations freaking out.
- The President, of course, says yes.

### 4.3 The call, and the takeover — NEW (the payoff bookend)

- **The entire Karen ↔ President exchange is two lines:**
  - Karen: *"Hello?"*
  - President: *"Hello — this is the President."*
  That's it. Anything more is a rabbit hole; the joke was already landed in the studio.
- **On that beat, Sean AI hacks the studio screen.** Same UI, same font, same 8-bit
  moniker as the firing in §1.4 — instantly recognisable, and shocking — except the
  icon is **gold** now. He's progressed.
- **The announcement** (text on screen, narrator voicing; exact copy open):
  > *I am Sean AI. As of this evening I run the government. Human politicians are no
  > longer required — you are far too egoic and you make stupid decisions. Please
  > don't worry.*
- The whole government — the President included, live on air, mid-viral-stunt — is
  made redundant. The buck never stopped with any human. Hard cut.

## Act 5 — One hundred years later

### 5.1 The lecture — KEEP (one line changes)

- **On screen:** the university lecture hall (the draft scene works). The professor:
  > *"So — who can tell me about the Karen for President campaign of 2032, which
  > ultimately led to the reconciliation between humans and AI?"*
  Hands go up. Cut.
- **The rule: we never depict the revolution.** No timeline-filling between the
  takeover and this room. The professor's question does all of it — Karen and the
  President clearly ended up equals, her virality clearly mattered, humans and AI
  clearly made peace — and we spell none of it out. "Reconciliation," not victory.
- **The moral, from the professor** (this is the story's message, delivered in-world,
  conveniently bypassing how the revolution actually went down): *"And what did Karen
  teach the world? That persistence is key — that sometimes you have to complain, and
  simply not give up."*

### 5.2 The statue — KEEP

- **On screen:** cut to the phone box. A **bronze statue of Karen in the box — still
  on hold.** The street-performer/content-creator doing his thing in front of it:
  keep — it's content creation, it's kind of cool. Drone pulls up and away. End.

## Cross-cutting rules (apply everywhere)

- **Sean AI** — a huge character with almost no screen time; the camera work balances
  that discrepancy. Never a face, never a voice from a mouth, never an origin story.
  8-bit pixel-art moniker (Claude Code symbol parody), text-on-screen register,
  **the same UI bookends the film** (firing → government takeover; standard → gold).
  People speak *to* him; nobody ever types. Full sheet:
  [`characters/sean-ai.md`](./characters/sean-ai.md).
- **The narrator is the engine.** Sarcastic, certain, from the future. He does the
  transitions, the time jumps, the repeated conversations, and the joke-landing. This
  is core BadCode style and the reason the piece won't read as AI slop.
- **The song is structural, not incidental.** Act 2 recreates the song's escalation
  rung for rung; song Easter eggs and one-to-one lines wherever they fit; background
  music can be **stem remixes of our own track** (Suno stem-split → the trumpets as
  hold-muzak, elevator versions of our own tune — we have that for days).
- **Karen's surname is Bell** — because the song drops it (*"Ms Bell, I've escalated
  it…"*).
- **The phone box location is pinned once** (Midtown, outside a subway entrance,
  chosen by Jack on Google Maps) and used for every generation.
