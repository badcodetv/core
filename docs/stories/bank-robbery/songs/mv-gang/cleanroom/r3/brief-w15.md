## 2. Glossy Prohibition glamour vs plastic AI slop

What high-budget 1920s productions did:
- *Boardwalk Empire* shot on 35mm, 3-perf Super 35, Kodak Vision3 500T and 250D ([StudioDaily](https://www.studiodaily.com/2010/09/shooting-boardwalk-empire-on-super-35/)) **solid**. Wealthier interiors carry more electric lighting, consistent with the period ([floobynooby blog](https://floobynooby.blogspot.com/2014/04/the-cinematography-of-boardwalk-empire.html)) **thin**. Built an outdoor boardwalk set because indoor lighting "would never feel real" **thin**.
- *The Great Gatsby* (2013): Catherine Martin did production and costume design and won both Oscars; Miuccia Prada made 40+ looks from Prada/Miu Miu archives; Brooks Brothers supplied about 1,200 outfits; Tiffany supplied jewellery ([Senatus](https://m.senatus.net/article/miuccia-prada-designs-prada-and-miu-miu-costumes-great-gatsby), [NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/catherine-martin-designs-australia-and-great-gatsby), [Wikipedia](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Martin_(designer))) **solid**. Martin cited *Sunrise* (1927) and *Speedy* (1928) as inspiration **thin**.
- Period accuracy note: Hollywood's beads-and-fringe-everywhere flapper is a myth; heavy beading was evening wear for the rich, and short all-over fringe is a later revival look ([VintageDancer](https://vintagedancer.com/1920s/flapper-costume-guide/)) **thin**. For gloss, the myth may be what you want; say so on purpose.

Glossy, in concrete terms (**unverified** synthesis from the above plus general craft):
- **Light:** hard, motivated practicals (bare bulbs, marquee chase lights, wall sconces, a window shaft through cigar smoke); deep blacks with bright specular highlights; haze so beams read; rim light on hats and shoulders; wet streets for reflections.
- **Materials:** things that *reflect* specifically: black lacquer, chrome trim, brass rails, polished marble with veining, mirrored deco panels, cut crystal, satin, silk velvet, patent leather, nickel-plated hardware. Deco geometry: sunbursts, chevrons, stepped ziggurats, fan motifs, fluted columns, terrazzo floors.
- **Colour:** restrained base (black, cream, deep oxblood, bottle green, champagne gold) with one saturated accent per frame; warm tungsten interiors vs cooler night exteriors. Gloss comes from contrast and specular highlights, not saturation.
- **Wardrobe:** three-piece chalk-stripe or pinstripe suits, wide peak lapels, high-waisted trousers with turn-ups, spats, two-tone brogues, fedoras and homburgs, overcoats with fur collars, pocket squares, watch chains, kid gloves.
- **Cars:** long bonnets, running boards, spoked or wire wheels, white-wall tyres, side-mounted spares, big chrome headlamps; deep paint that holds reflections of neon.
- **Lens/film feel:** film grain, gentle halation round bright bulbs, shallow focus on long lenses for close-ups, anamorphic-style oval bokeh and streak flares (anachronistic but "movie"), slight softness in the corners.

Plastic AI slop, in concrete terms: skin or fabric "like a mannequin dipped in wax, with no pores, no texture and a uniform sheen" ([oakgen](https://oakgen.ai/blog/fix-plastic-skin-ai-portraits)) **thin**; AI "smooths out textures too much, making skin, surfaces and fabric appear unnaturally flawless" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2025/02/16/ai-slop-is-flooding-the-internet-this-is-how-can-you-tell-if-an-image-is-artificially-gene)) **solid**; over-smoothed surfaces with over-sharpened details like lashes and jewellery ([crepal](https://crepal.ai/blog/agent/plastic-skin-adult-ai-images/)) **thin**; synthetic sheen, mirror symmetry, garbled text ([magicshot](https://magicshot.ai/blog/why-your-ai-images-still-look-fake-12-tells-and-how-to-kill-them)) **thin**; slop is "the model's defaults showing through because the operator never overrode them" ([oakgen](https://oakgen.ai/blog/ai-slop-backlash-make-content-that-doesnt-look-ai)) **thin**. For this video add (**unverified**): even, sourceless glow everywhere; everything new and spotless; knitwear that looks like smooth rubber instead of yarn with stitches and fuzz; centred symmetrical poses; sepia filter standing in for period; teal-and-orange grade; gold on everything. Glossy reflects light from a visible source; slop glows from nowhere. Glossy has wear (scuffed brogues, a chipped marble step); slop is uniformly perfect.

## 4. Stage-rap gestures, as body movement

Sources: research exists on rap performance movement (Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics project on "typical body movements and verbal phrases in rap performance", [MPG](https://www.aesthetics.mpg.de/en/research/department-of-music/aesthetic-experience-contexts/live-performances/rappin-and-rocking-the-house-typical-body-movements-and-verbal-phrases-in-rap-performance.html); a multimodal battle-rap study aligning bars to gesture, gaze and crowd reaction, [Global Hip Hop Studies](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/from-bars-to-bodies-multimodal-power-plays-in-trex-vs-charlie-clips/); a Jagiellonian catalogue of rap gestures and their functions, [RUJ](https://ruj.uj.edu.pl/entities/publication/154ff246-fcc1-4f6a-99a4-adbfce616a71)) **solid** that the field exists; page contents could not be fetched, so the list below is **unverified** synthesis. Gestures being tied to the beat and to lyric content recurs across sources **thin**.

Vocabulary:
- **Beat chop:** flat hand slicing down on each downbeat, forearm like a metronome.
- **Point-out:** arm fully extended, index finger at the lens or a named person; or a double-hand point.
- **Palm press / bounce:** both palms facing down pumping on the beat ("hold it down").
- **Raise-the-roof / hands up:** both palms up pushing at the ceiling; crowd-work "put your hands up".
- **Finger count / list:** counting off points on the fingers, close to camera.
- **Fist to chest / tap the heart:** sincerity beat.
- **Throw-away flick:** back of the hand flicking away, dismissive.
- **Wave across:** arm sweeping side to side over the head (crowd wave).
- **Mic hand:** mic held tight with the elbow up and out (horizontal forearm), or the cupped grip; free hand does all the talking. A period swap: a big chrome ribbon mic on a stand or a megaphone.
- **Stance:** wide, knees soft, leaning forward into the lens; one foot on a monitor (period: a crate of gin, a bank counter, a car running board).
- **Head and shoulders:** head nod on the beat, chin up/out on an emphasis line, shoulder shrug-bounce, head tilt.
- **Lean-in:** sudden lunge to a lens-close position on a punchline; back away on the next bar.
- **Group work:** the hype men behind ad-libbing with their own points, all hands up together on the hook, synchronized bounce.

How they read in a still frame (**unverified**): pick the extreme of a move, not the middle; full arm extension, open fingers, foreshortening toward a wide lens close to camera; asymmetric pose (one arm up, one down); weight off-centre. Without a face, the hands and head angle do all the acting, so gloves should be a contrasting colour to the suit (e.g. white or cream gloves on dark suits) so the gestures read.

Making it sarcastic / mock-serious (**unverified**): overdo it (a fist-to-heart held far too long; a point that swings round to the man next to him); mismatch the grand gesture to the trivial line; dead-still deadpan stance while one hand does a tiny limp "raise the roof"; slow-clap; a faux-solemn bow; checking a pocket watch mid-flow; air-quotes with gloved fingers; one man doing a big crowd-work gesture to a vault with no one in it; the gang performing to a single terrified bank teller as if to a stadium.

## 5. Fast action-movie editing cut to music

- Classical Hollywood averaged about 8 to 11 seconds per shot (1930–60); action films cut faster; examples around 2.7 s average shot length in late-1990s films ([Bordwell, "Intensified Continuity", Film Quarterly 2002, via davidbordwell.net](https://davidbordwell.net/books/figures_intro.php?ss=2)) **solid**.
- Frames per beat = (60 / BPM) × fps ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/music-video-pacing-emotional-montage-rhythm), [cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/beat-based-editing-in-capcut-syncing-cuts-transitions-and-motion-to-music)) **solid** (arithmetic).
- Don't cut on every beat: it strobes; vary shot lengths and use the song's structure (verse/chorus/bridge) ([Larry Jordan via No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/larry-jordan-teaches-us-how-create-video-montage-set-music), [CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/music-video-pacing-emotional-montage-rhythm)) **thin** (several agree).
- Build to a climax by shortening shot length, then give a beat of rest after it ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/music-video-pacing-emotional-montage-rhythm)) **thin**.
- Frame accuracy matters more than exact beat accuracy ([Vegas](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.com/in/post-production/how-to-edit-video-footage-to-the-beats-of-music)) **thin**. The editor decides when to break the beat ([invideo](https://invideo.io/faq/can-ai-automatically-cut-a-video-to-the-beat-of-music/)) **thin**.
- Speed ramp: 100% down to 10–20% over about 0.5–1 s, hold 1–2 s at the key moment, back to 100%; used in music videos to sync action peaks to beats ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/ai-glossary/speed-ramping)) **thin**. Speeding up an incoming hit near impact adds force ([filmeditingpro](https://www.filmeditingpro.com/?p=30439)) **thin**. Fake slow motion from normal-speed footage looks choppy; optical flow / frame blending helps at a quality cost ([Motion Array](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/premiere-pro/premiere-pro-speed-ramping-tutorial/)) **solid**. Important for 6-second AI clips: ramps down will smear; ramp *up* (speed up) is safer.

92 BPM arithmetic (**solid**, arithmetic): one beat = 0.652 s; half-beat (eighth) = 0.326 s; one bar (4/4) = 2.61 s; half bar = 1.30 s. At 24 fps a beat is 15.65 frames, at 25 fps 16.3, at 30 fps 19.6. Frames don't divide evenly, so lay markers from the waveform rather than stepping a fixed frame count, or the cuts drift.

Suggested ratios for an action-pace rap video (**unverified**, craft convention): verse base at 2 beats per shot (≈1.3 s); fills and action bursts at 1 beat (≈0.65 s) with occasional half-beat flurries of 3–4 shots; hook or punchline held a full bar (2.6 s) on the performer; one long breath (2 bars) before a drop. Cut *on* the snare/kick for impacts; cut *on action* (mid-swing, mid-leap, the door opening) so the motion carries across the cut and hides it; cutting slightly off the beat (a frame or two early) reads as tighter to many viewers.

Readability at high cut rates (**unverified**): keep the performer in the same screen position and eye-line across a run of cuts; alternate wide/close so each shot is new information; give every lyric-carrying close-up at least a beat; keep a consistent screen direction in chases; a single-frame white flash or a 2–4 frame freeze ("impact frame") on a hit (no source found for impact-frame specifics); return to a "home" performance shot every bar or two so the audience has an anchor; let colour contrast (her vs the gang) do identification so faces are not needed.

## 6. Nano Banana 2 prompting

Model: Nano Banana 2 runs on Gemini 3.1 Flash Image and is the Gemini app default ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/google-nano-banana-2-cheat-sheet/)) **thin**.

Google's own tips (Gemini app blog, read via mirror: [blog.google](https://blog.google/products/gemini/image-generation-prompting-tips)) **solid**:
- Prompt elements: Subject (be specific), Composition (extreme close-up, wide, low angle), Action, Location, Style, and for edits, direct specific instructions.
- Character consistency: define the character with concrete details in the first prompt and reuse it in follow-ups in the same conversation; the model keeps key features and clothing across poses and settings but "may not always get it right."
- Combine images: generate pieces separately, upload them and ask for a composition; style transfer keeps the subject and re-renders the style.
- Limits: stylisation can be inconsistent; text may misspell; aspect ratios may not be honoured.

Consistent character / references (**thin** unless marked):

# More parodied action: 40 new set pieces for the masked gang (sealed web research, r3)

Sealed researcher, web only, 2026-11-04. No repo files were read.

**Brief:** six masked robbers in sharp coloured suits and full-head knitted balaclavas, Prohibition America, a glossy high-budget look. Each set piece plays an action cliché straight and too cool, with one deadpan or absurd touch. Only property gets hurt: nobody is harmed and guns point up or away. The 16 already done are left out. Where a new idea sits close to one of them, the entry says so.

**How the period facts are marked:**
- **solid**: two or more agreeing sources, or one strong one.
- **thin**: one source, or the sources disagree on details.
- **unverified**: general knowledge I did not check this session. Check it before it goes on screen as fact.

**Tropes:** each trope is described generically. Films are never named in a description. Silent-era stunts are cited for their date only.

## A. Silent-era stunt comedy (period-correct and already parody-shaped)

### 1. Hanging off the clock face
- **Trope:** a man dangles from the hands of a giant clock high on a building, high above the street.
- **1920s version:** a bank's clock tower, or the clock on a downtown office block, above Model T traffic. One robber hangs from the minute hand. A cash bag hangs from his other hand.
- **Deadpan touch:** with the hand that holds the bag, he checks his wristwatch against the clock, frowns, and nudges the minute hand forward.
- **In the song:** inflation and time ("every minute it's worth less"), or "running out the clock on the working man".
- **Period:** **solid.** The stunt is from a 1923 silent comedy, shot on a set built on a rooftop with the street below. https://silentlocations.com/2012/02/29/how-harold-lloyd-filmed-safety-last/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_Last!
- **AI risk:** it will put numbers on the clock face and give a wrong time. Use Roman numerals, or a plain face with tick marks only. Keep the hands simple, and write "hands at a quarter to three".

### 2. The falling building front
- **Trope:** a whole house front falls flat on a man. He survives untouched because he is standing exactly where the open window lands.
- **1920s version:** the false front of a bank, or a two-storey timber storefront, falls into a muddy street in a storm. One robber stands in the window gap.
- **Deadpan touch:** he doesn't blink. He straightens his tie, then steps forward through the window frame as if it were a door.
- **In the song:** landlords and the housing crash ("the house came down and I'm still standing"), or a bank failing around the gang.
- **Period:** **solid.** A 1928 silent comedy did it with a real facade of about two tons, with three inches to spare. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat_Bill,_Jr. and https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/buster-keatons-narrow-escape-steamboat-bill-jr/
- **AI risk:** it will draw the window in the wrong place, or the man cut through by the wall. Stage it **after** the fall: the facade lies flat and the man stands in the rectangular hole. The motion clip can be a dust settle and a tie straighten.

### 3. Lunch on the girder
- **Trope:** the crew in a row on a steel beam hundreds of feet up, legs dangling, eating lunch.
- **1920s version:** six robbers on an I-beam of a skyscraper going up in Manhattan, rivet buckets beside them. They eat sandwiches and count banknotes.
- **Deadpan touch:** one of them is passing round the stolen cash, and another is passing round sandwiches. Nobody can tell which is which, so one hands back a banknote with a bite out of it.
- **In the song:** the working man built the city. Wages against wealth ("we built the tower, they own the view").
- **Period:** **solid.** The famous photo was taken on 20 Sept 1932 at the RCA Building, staged as publicity. 1932 is still Prohibition. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunch_atop_a_Skyscraper and https://www.history.com/news/lunch-atop-a-skyscraper-photo-1932
- **AI risk:** six figures in a row is fine, because a row is easy. Hands holding small objects go wrong, so keep it to sandwiches and stacks, not single notes. Don't ask for the copyright image itself. Say "a steel beam high over 1930s Manhattan".

### 4. The bonnet slide that takes forever
- **Trope:** the hero slides across the car bonnet to reach the driver's door.
- **1920s version:** the bonnet of a 1920s luxury tourer is enormous, longer than a man is tall (a Duesenberg or Packard type, **unverified** as to exact length).
- **Deadpan touch:** he slides, stops halfway, and has to shuffle the rest of the way on his backside, still too cool, while the others wait in the car.
- **In the song:** the long slow slide of the currency, or "the long way down".
- **Period:** long-bonnet luxury cars of the late 1920s are **unverified** (general knowledge).
- **AI risk:** low. One figure and one car. Avoid badge names or a readable plate.

### 5. Rolling under the closing vault door
- **Trope:** a heavy door grinds down and the hero rolls under at the last second, then reaches back for the hat.
- **1920s version:** a round bank-vault door is the wrong shape for this, so use a descending steel shutter or portcullis gate in a marble banking hall.
- **Deadpan touch:** he rolls through. The shutter stops a hand's width from the floor. His hat is on the other side. He slides an arm under, retrieves it, and puts it back on over the balaclava.
- **In the song:** a bailout arriving just in time ("the door came down, we got our hat back").
- **Period:** bank shutters as such are **unverified**. The trope is general.
- **AI risk:** it will mangle a figure that is half under a door. Stage it as the hand and hat under the shutter, at floor level and close up.

### 6. The wing walk and the plane-to-plane transfer
- **Trope:** a man walks the wing of a flying biplane and steps across to a second plane.
- **1920s version:** two barnstorming biplanes over cornfields or a county fair. A robber in a pinstripe suit steps from one top wing to the other, carrying a cash bag. This is **not** a rope-ladder escape: no ladder, just the two wings side by side.
- **Deadpan touch:** his hat stays perfectly on in the gale, and so does a fresh banknote in his breast pocket.
- **In the song:** passing money between banks, the inter-bank shuffle ("from one hand to the other").
- **Period:** **solid.** 1920s barnstorming wing walkers made plane-to-plane transfers. The first is usually credited to Ormer Locklear. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_walking and https://www.centennialofflight.net/essay/Explorers_Record_Setters_and_Daredevils/wingwalkers/EX13.htm
- **AI risk:** two biplanes plus a man is busy, and struts and wires come out muddled. Do one plane in the frame with the man on its top wing, and the second plane's wingtip only at the edge.

## B. Prohibition-era incidents and kit

### 7. The flip-bar
- **Trope:** a hidden mechanism transforms the room at the press of a lever.
- **1920s version:** a speakeasy is raided. The bartender pulls a lever, and the back-bar shelves flip and send every bottle sliding down a chute. In our version, the shelves flip round to reveal a gleaming money-printing press, already running.
- **Deadpan touch:** the robber pulls the lever without looking up from polishing a glass.
- **In the song:** "they can print it, so can we". Secret money creation.
- **Period:** **solid.** 21 Club, New York, after a 1930 raid: quick-release shelves dropped the liquor into a chute and the sewer. Sources differ on whether it was a lever or a button. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21_Club and https://abcnews.go.com/Travel/Vacation/story?id=6122261
- **AI risk:** a half-flipped shelf is impossible to draw. Do two stills, before (bottles) and after (the press), and cut between them.

### 8. Money overboard
- **Trope:** the smugglers dump the cargo overboard to escape.
- **1920s version:** a rum-runner speedboat off the New Jersey coast at dawn, with a Coast Guard patrol boat far behind. The robbers tip crates over the stern. The crates are full of banknotes, and they leave a wake of floating money for a mile.
- **Deadpan touch:** one robber licks his finger and counts each note before it goes over.
- **In the song:** flooding the economy with money. Inflation as "a sea of paper".
- **Period:** **solid.** Rum-runners dumped liquor to avoid arrest. Rum Row lay beyond the 12-mile limit set in 1924. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum-running and https://prohibition.themobmuseum.org/the-history/the-rise-of-organized-crime/rum-running/
- **Note:** this sits next to the speedboat chase that is already done. Keep it a daylight, wide shot of the wake, not a chase.
- **AI risk:** floating banknotes can carry fake readable text. Say "pale green paper notes, no legible print".

### 9. The oil-smoke vanish
- **Trope:** a pursued vehicle disappears into a smoke screen and is gone.
- **1920s version:** a rum-runner pours used oil on the hot exhausts and the boat vanishes into a black cloud. For land, the same gag on a getaway car on a coast road.
- **Deadpan touch:** when the smoke clears, the six robbers are standing on the dock, dry, in a neat row. The boat is gone and nobody knows where.
- **In the song:** where did the money go? The bailout vanishes ("it went up in smoke").
- **Period:** **thin.** Oil on the exhaust, smoke screens and sudden reversals were rum-runner tactics, but the sources are popular history. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum-running and https://capecodlife.com/cape-cod-rum-runners/
- **AI risk:** low. Smoke hides everything.

### 10. Under the El
- **Trope:** a car chase weaving between the steel pillars of an elevated railway while a train thunders overhead.
- **1920s version:** Chicago's Loop or New York's Third Avenue El, with wooden or early steel cars overhead and Model Ts and a horse cart in the street below.
- **Deadpan touch:** the getaway car stops at every red signal, perfectly legally. The pursuing police car has to stop too.
- **In the song:** "running on rails". Debt you can't get off.
- **Period:** **solid.** In the 1920s about 120 trains an hour entered the Loop. Ridership was 229 million in 1926. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Loop
- **AI risk:** rows of pillars and the train above are fine. It will invent readable station signs and ads. Say "no readable signs or lettering".

### 11. Hanging off the El
- **Trope:** a man hangs off the outside of a moving train car.
- **1920s version:** one robber stands on the outside step of an El car as it rounds a curve above the street, holding the grab rail with one hand and a cash bag with the other.
- **Deadpan touch:** a commuter inside, reading the paper, hands him a ticket through the window. He punches it.
- **In the song:** commuters and wages, the daily grind.
- **Period:** **solid** (as in #10).
- **Note:** close to the train-roof run that is already done. This one is the side of the car, at street height, in a city curve.
- **AI risk:** low.

### 12. The airship drop onto the tower
- **Trope:** the crew drops from an airship onto a skyscraper.
- **1920s version:** a blimp nudges the mooring mast on top of a Manhattan skyscraper. Six robbers slide down a single line onto the tower's observation deck.
- **Deadpan touch:** the last one tips the airship pilot.
- **In the song:** the balloon is inflation: the airship swells as the song says "inflation".
- **Period:** **solid** that the mast was built (Empire State Building, 1931). **Thin** that anything docked: the sources disagree, and the Met says no airship ever docked. A Goodyear blimp dropped newspapers to the building in Sept 1931. https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/docking-on-the-empire-state-building-12525534/ and https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/294832 . Goodyear's first blimp flew in 1925: https://www.airships.net/goodyear-blimp/goodyear-blimp-pilgrim
- **AI risk:** six men on one rope will merge. Show one man on the line and the others already standing on the deck. Don't put a readable name on the blimp's envelope.

### 13. Ballast is money
- **Trope:** a balloon is sinking, so they throw things overboard to climb.
- **1920s version:** a gas balloon or small blimp over the city. The robbers throw out sacks of banknotes to rise.
- **Deadpan touch:** every sack they throw out lifts them higher. When the money is gone they are above the clouds, delighted and broke.
- **In the song:** this is the point. The less money is worth, the "higher" the numbers ("we went up because it went down").
- **Period:** balloon ballast is general knowledge, **unverified**. Airships are as in #12.
- **AI risk:** low.

### 14. Opening the instrument case
- **Trope:** the musician's case that hides a submachine gun.
- **1920s version:** a robber walks into the bank lobby and opens a violin case.
- **Deadpan touch:** there is a violin in it. He plays it. Meanwhile the others walk out behind him with the cash. Or the case holds a printing plate.
- **In the song:** misdirection, a cue for "look over here while they rob you".
- **Period:** **solid** that the Thompson dates from 1921 and was notorious with gangsters. **Thin, mostly myth:** that gangsters carried it in violin cases. A Thompson is longer than a 31-inch violin case. Popular culture made it. https://www.classicfm.com/discover-music/tommy-guns-violin-cases-gangster-films-history/ and https://www.forgottenweapons.com/thompson-smg-cases-police-fbi-and-secret-service/ . The myth makes it better parody.
- **AI risk:** low.

### 15. The axe through the door
- **Trope:** the raid. Axes splinter the door, then the wall.
- **1920s version:** Prohibition agents raiding with axes. Inside, the gang sit at a card table and keep playing as axe heads come through the walls around them, wood only.
- **Deadpan touch:** one robber raises a finger, "one moment", and finishes his hand.
- **In the song:** the regulator arriving too late, "the watchdog showed up after".
- **Period:** **solid.** 1921 photos show agents smashing barrels with axes and pouring liquor into sewers. https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/61386/15-pictures-people-wasting-perfectly-good-alcohol-during-prohibition and https://lcweb2.loc.gov/resource/cph.3c23257/
- **AI risk:** an axe head through a door is easy. Agents' faces aren't needed: just the axe blades.

### 16. The armoured truck lifted away
- **Trope:** instead of cracking the armoured truck, take the whole thing.
- **1920s version:** an early armoured truck (riveted steel plates on a truck chassis) is parked outside a bank. A steam crane from the building site next door hooks it and lifts it into the sky.
- **Deadpan touch:** the guards are still inside, playing cards, unaware. The truck swings gently.
- **In the song:** "they lifted the whole economy", or a bailout as a lift.
- **Period:** **thin.** Brink's school-bus conversions came in 1917, an enclosed truck in 1918, a fully armoured car by 1923, but the dates conflict. https://www.curbsideclassic.com/blog/vintage-ads-and-brochures/vintage-ad-1926-international-brinks-armored-truck-back-when-everyone-got-paid-in-cash . The first armoured-car robbery was 1927 near Pittsburgh, with dynamite under the road. **Do not reference that one:** people died. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Jaworski
- **AI risk:** the crane cable and hook come out fine. Avoid lettering on the truck.

### 17. The gold-vault glow
- **Trope:** the vault door swings open and golden light floods out over the faces.
- **1920s version:** a vault five storeys below Wall Street on bedrock: steel cages and stacked bars.
- **Deadpan touch:** inside is no gold, only a man in a green eyeshade at a printing press, who looks up, startled.
- **In the song:** the central bank. "There is no gold, there's a man with a press."
- **Period:** **solid.** The NY Federal Reserve building opened in 1924, with its vault about 80 ft down on bedrock (the depth figure comes from secondary sources). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank_of_New_York_Building and https://www.bullionstar.com/gold-university/new-york-fed-gold-vaults
- **AI risk:** a round vault door is drawn well. Keep the inside simple: one man, one press.

### 18. The note storm
- **Trope:** money rains down and the hero stands in it, arms out.
- **1920s version:** fresh-cut notes pour off a rotary press in a government printing hall. The robbers stand in the storm in slow motion, unhurried.
- **Deadpan touch:** one opens an umbrella.
- **In the song:** the chorus. "Print it, print it."
- **Period:** **solid.** In 1929 the US switched to small-size notes printed 12 to a sheet, partly to save paper and ink as the bureau's output grew. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Engraving_and_Printing and https://deanelawlibrary.blogspot.com/2013/07/normal-0-false-false-false-en-us-x-none.html
- **AI risk:** high for readable fake currency text. Ask for "blank green paper", "notes blurred by motion". Keep it out of close-up.

### 19. Hot money through the pneumatic tube
- **Trope:** using the building's own system to carry the loot.
- **1920s version:** a bank or department store with brass pneumatic tubes. The robbers feed in canisters of cash, which shoot across the building to a man waiting with a sack.
- **Deadpan touch:** the canisters arrive with a little receipt, which he files.
- **In the song:** money moving at speed between banks while the wages stand still.
- **Period:** **thin.** Department stores had them from about 1880. Banks are named only in general terms. https://cashrailway.co.uk/pneumatictube.htm and https://weburbanist.com/2009/04/11/a-series-of-tubes-pneumatic-networks-past-present-futurama/
- **AI risk:** the brass tube with its hatch is drawable. One figure only.

### 20. Riding the roller coaster
- **Trope:** a fight or chase on a moving roller coaster.
- **1920s version:** a wooden coaster at a seaside amusement park in 1927. Six robbers sit in the front car with cash bags on their laps.
- **Deadpan touch:** at the top of the drop all six raise their hands, guns pointed at the sky, faces still.
- **In the song:** boom and bust, the business cycle.
- **Period:** **solid.** The Coney Island Cyclone opened on 26 June 1927 (25 cents). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coney_Island_Cyclone
- **AI risk:** the coaster car with six riders is fine in a front view. The track structure gets muddled in a wide shot.

### 21. The motorcycle with a sidecar
- **Trope:** the bike chase, one man driving and one in the sidecar.
- **1920s version:** an Indian or Harley-type motorcycle with a sidecar on a country road. Behind it, police on motorcycles.
- **Deadpan touch:** the man in the sidecar is reading the financial pages, with his reading glasses over the balaclava.
- **In the song:** the market reports, the stock tickers.
- **Period:** **solid** that 1920s police used motorcycles to enforce Prohibition. **Thin** on sidecars in the 1920s. https://www.rideapart.com/articles/harley-davidson-police-history and https://police1.com/motorcycle-patrol/articles/police-history-the-evolution-of-motor-patrol-in-american-policing-h2Jv6FjbXrUN3stk
- **AI risk:** a sidecar rig is fine. Don't put a brand on the tank.

### 22. The motorcycle through the billboard
- **Trope:** the bike bursts through a billboard or paper banner.
- **1920s version:** a painted wooden billboard by a highway with a picture only (a smiling family, a car).
- **Deadpan touch:** he leaves a hole the exact shape of the bike and rider, like a cartoon.
- **In the song:** breaking the advertised dream.
- **Period:** **unverified** (roadside billboards, general knowledge).
- **AI risk:** it will write on the billboard. Say "a painted picture, no words".

## C. Heist-film clichés

### 23. The bells on threads (the laser grid, in the 1920s)
- **Trope:** a hallway of alarm beams with gaps the hero contorts through.
- **1920s version:** threads strung across the vault corridor, each with a small brass bell.
- **Deadpan touch:** he goes through like a ballet dancer, perfect. The sixth man just walks straight through, and the bells jingle softly.
- **In the song:** red tape, regulation the rich slip through.
- **Period:** the bell threads are a **parody invention**, not a period fact.
- **AI risk:** it will draw too many threads in a tangle. Write "five taut threads, each with one bell".

### 24. Cut the red wire
- **Trope:** two men argue over which wire to cut while the timer runs down.
- **1920s version:** a bundle of dynamite with an alarm clock strapped on. Alternatively, a bank alarm bell box on the wall.
- **Deadpan touch:** they argue. The third walks past and simply stops the alarm clock with his finger.
- **In the song:** the "ticking debt bomb", "nobody knows which wire".
- **Period:** **unverified** (dynamite with an alarm clock timer is a stock image, not a period fact).
- **AI risk:** it can draw the clock face with numbers, but keep the wires simple. "Three wires: red, blue, white."
- **Rule:** keep it plainly a prop, with nothing aimed at people.

### 25. The cut circle in the floor
- **Trope:** cut a circle in the floor and drop through.
- **1920s version:** the gang saw a circle in the floor of the bank manager's office, and the whole circle drops into the vault below.
- **Deadpan touch:** they are sitting on chairs on the circle when it drops, and land still seated, legs crossed.
- **In the song:** "the floor dropped out of the market".
- **Period:** a cartoon trope; **no period fact claimed**.
- **AI risk:** mid-fall is hard. Stage it as a looking-up shot, the men seated on a disc of floor in the vault, plaster dust falling around them.

### 26. The safecracker's stethoscope
- **Trope:** a man listening to a safe dial with a stethoscope.
- **1920s version:** a black steel safe with gold scrollwork.
- **Deadpan touch:** he listens like a doctor, then turns to the others and shakes his head gravely: the patient is dead (empty).
- **In the song:** the economy's heartbeat, or "the bank is broke".
- **Period:** **unverified** (combination safes of the 1920s, general knowledge).
- **AI risk:** low.

### 27. The tools in a velvet-lined case
- **Trope:** the case opens on gleaming tools in fitted foam.
- **1920s version:** a crocodile leather case lined in red velvet.
- **Deadpan touch:** the "tools" are an adding machine, a rubber stamp and a ham sandwich.
- **In the song:** accountants as robbers, the stamp that makes money.
- **Period:** **unverified** (adding machines, rubber stamps, general knowledge).
- **AI risk:** low. Close-up.

### 28. The model of the bank (the plan)
- **Trope:** the plan over a scale model with little cars and men.
- **1920s version:** a pool table under a green lamp in a back room. A cardboard model of the bank.
- **Deadpan touch:** the little men in the model are wearing tiny balaclavas.
- **In the song:** the intro or verse one, "here's how it works".
- **Period:** **no period fact needed**.
- **AI risk:** it adds labels. Say "no writing".

### 29. The crew montage
- **Trope:** recruiting each specialist at their day job.
- **1920s version:** a barber, a typist pool, a docker, a soda jerk, a hotel lift operator, a bandleader.
- **Deadpan touch:** every one of them is already wearing the balaclava at work, and nobody around them notices.
- **In the song:** the working man, ordinary workers as the gang.
- **Period:** **unverified** (jobs, general knowledge).
- **AI risk:** low. One per still.

### 30. The mask under the mask
- **Trope:** the disguise peeled off to reveal the real face.
- **1920s version:** a robber disguised as a policeman or a banker peels off a rubber face mask.
- **Deadpan touch:** underneath is the balaclava. He peels that off, and underneath is another balaclava.
- **In the song:** who is really robbing whom: "the banker's mask is a robber's mask".
- **Period:** **unverified.** Disguised Prohibition agents (the famous New York pair) were not confirmed this session. Their 1922 photo exists: https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/joe-fleming/25-photos-of-prohibition-era-raids
- **AI risk:** a face half peeled is creepy and hard. Show only the balaclava under it, with the rubber mask in his hand.

### 31. The van that swallows the car
- **Trope:** the getaway car drives up a ramp into the back of a moving truck, the doors close, and it is gone.
- **1920s version:** a furniture removal van with a wooden body, driving slowly. The car vanishes into the back.
- **Deadpan touch:** a removals man on the tailgate ticks it off on his clipboard.
- **In the song:** off the books, hidden debt.

## What an AI image model will probably draw badly, and how to stage it simply

- **Too many figures.** Six men in a line, a row or a car seat is fine. Six men doing six things is not. Crowds, corridors of guards and ranks of police turn to mush. Keep to one to three figures in action, and show the others still or out of focus.
- **Mid-motion physics.** A falling facade, a flipping shelf, a car up a ramp, a man under a door and a safe in the air all come out wrong. Stage before and after as two stills, or stage the moment just after, when everything is at rest. Let the 6-second clip carry the small motion: dust, smoke, a tie straightened.
- **Readable signs.** Banknotes, billboards, clock faces, station signs, the blimp's envelope, armoured truck lettering and newspaper headlines all come out with nonsense letters. Ask for "no legible text", "blank green paper" and "plain faces".
- **Knitted balaclavas.** Over a hat, spectacles or a stethoscope, the knit can melt into the object. Check and re-roll.
- **Hands holding small things.** Hands holding coins, single notes, cards or cups go wrong. Prefer large props: bags, stacks, umbrellas.
- **Wires, ropes, struts.** Keep counts low and write the number.
