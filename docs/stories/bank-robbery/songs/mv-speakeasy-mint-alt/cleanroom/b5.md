=== SCENE 18 ===
A full-bleed frame from a 35mm feature film, Chicago, 1928, early evening, inside a cramped radio studio above a furniture showroom. We look in from the engineer's booth through two thick panes of soundproof glass set a hand's width apart, so the room arrives doubled and trebled: each pane throws back its own faint reflection of the booth's rack of glowing valves, a stack of orange and violet ghosts floating over the studio. Through them, off-centre to the right and seen from below desk height, the mayor, a tall, skinny man in a bowler hat with a heavy gold chain of office round his neck, a long narrow face, hollow cheeks, a pencil-thin grey moustache, grins into a big chrome ribbon microphone, a typed script in his fingers turned edge-on. The chrome bulges his grin back at him, stretched. Above the far door a red glass lamp glows, blank. In that doorway a gangster in a chalk-stripe suit and fedora, pockmarked, toothpick in his teeth, thumbs through a fold of banknotes. Horsehair acoustic panels sag and stain; cigarette burns on the desk felt. The only hard light is one hanging bulb over the microphone; everything else falls to deep black. All paper, dials and lamps are blank, with no lettering anywhere.
WHY: The propaganda scene is shot through layered booth glass, so the mayor's one voice literally multiplies into ghosts, a physical psychedelic layer nobody else uses.

=== SCENE 19 ===
A full-bleed frame from a 35mm feature film, Chicago, 1928, late at night, a tenement kitchen two floors above the elevated railway. The camera sits low on the oilcloth table, close, peering past a heavy cut-glass water jug standing in front of the lens. The jug sits between us and a wooden cathedral radio set whose dial glows warm amber, and the water bends that glow into rippling caustics, crawling nets of gold and green light that spill across the table, up the couple's faces and over the peeling wallpaper, warped as if the room is underwater. A couple lean in to the radio, listening: a stocky Black woman in her forties in a darned cardigan, hair tied in a rag, and a thin white man with a week of stubble and a cracked collar stud, both very still, eyes unfocused. A red scarf hangs over the back of a bentwood chair. A coal range ticks in the dark, a chipped enamel kettle, a wedge of grey bread on a board. Through the sash window, far off, other kitchen windows hold the same small amber glow. One light source only, the radio dial, with soot-dark corners and grain. The radio face and everything in the room carry no lettering.
WHY: The broadcast's voice becomes light refracted through a water jug, an intimate, liquid distortion that makes the listeners look hypnotised without any pasted effect.

=== SCENE 20 ===
A full-bleed frame from a 35mm feature film, Chicago, 1928, four in the morning, a vast brick vault dug under the city, its arched ceiling black with coal soot. The camera hangs high in a corner behind a rusted iron railing, tilted hard so the floor runs downhill across the frame. A burst steam pipe from the furnace below hisses along one wall, and its steam fills the vault in slow rolling banks; two lamps cut solid shafts through it, a green-shaded work lamp on the left and a caged amber bulb on the right, so the air itself becomes glowing slabs of emerald and honey that overlap into strange turquoise where they cross. Through the steam, gangsters in chalk-stripe suits and fedoras, one scarred, one with a toothpick, shirtsleeves rolled, strain to push wooden wheelbarrows piled with rubber-banded bundles of cash up a sagging scaffold plank onto a mountain of money that fills the right of the frame and rises out of sight. Bundles have tumbled into puddles of condensation on the cracked brick floor; a barrow wheel is wrapped in tape. Deep black beyond the beams. All banknotes, crates and walls carry no readable lettering.
WHY: Coloured lamps cutting through furnace steam turn the air into solid colour, a wide, industrial psychedelic layer that ties the vault to the burning below.

=== SCENE 21 ===
A full-bleed frame from a 35mm feature film, Chicago, 1928, midday, the top of a mountain of cash inside a brick vault that runs under the pavement. The ceiling is a grid of old pavement vault-lights, hundreds of round glass lenses set in iron, turned amethyst by years of sun; noon daylight pours through them in a rain of violet, lilac and pale gold discs that dapples the whole pile, shifting tone where the glass is chipped or clouded. The camera looks straight down from just under the ceiling, rotated off-square so the body lies diagonally across the frame. The mayor, a tall, skinny man in a bowler hat with a heavy gold chain of office round his neck, a long narrow face, hollow cheeks, a pencil-thin grey moustache, sprawls on his back on the rubber-banded bundles, arms flung wide, eyes glazed and half-open, the gold chain sliding on his chest, a single banknote laid over the lower half of his face like a newspaper on a beach. A shoe has come off; his sock has a hole at the toe. Bootprints and footsteps in the dust on the lenses cast faint shadows. No other light; the vault edges fall to black. The banknotes show only blurred engraving, no readable words.
WHY: Purple-sun pavement glass, a real Chicago object, scatters daylight like a drug haze over the mayor, matching "don't get high off your own supply" with a physical, period-true effect.
