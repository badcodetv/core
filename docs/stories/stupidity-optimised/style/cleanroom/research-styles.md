## 1. The Square Close-Up

**Family:** social realism.

**References**
- Fish Tank (2009, Andrea Arnold; cinematographer Robbie Ryan).
- Wasp (2003, Andrea Arnold short; Robbie Ryan). Ryan compares the way the camera follows the lead in both films.
- Mommy (2014, Xavier Dolan; André Turpin), a Canadian relative in a 1:1 frame, handheld.

**Physical look**
- **Frame:** 4:3, tight on one person. The camera moves when they move. Fish Tank's camera follows its lead closely and takes her point of view.
- **Movement:** handheld and physically close. Ryan's most athletic shot rode a pick-up truck to keep pace with a running girl.
- **Light and colour:** naturalistic, in a blues-and-yellows palette on Fish Tank. Natural light only, the film stock and the lenses are all **[unverified]**.
- **Bodies and casting:** real skin, bad posture, no vanity. Fish Tank's lead had no acting experience and was spotted arguing at a railway station.
- **This film:** window light in the attic, strip lights in the gym, and the camera at Brandon's shoulder as he lifts.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** the camera loves Brandon without judging him, so each injury lands on a body we are pressed against. When Gary drops the weight, the frame cannot back away.

**Honest risk:** it asks for sympathy, and skit logic asks for distance. A gag every ninety seconds may fight the intimacy. Handheld closeness also leans hardest on faces and hands staying consistent between generated shots.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** pale, spotty, underfed, in a supermarket hoodie and new protein-brand shorts.
- **Gary:** grey stubble, sun-damaged neck, faded charity-run T-shirt.
- **Giles:** soft well-fed face, navy gilet with a small logo, seen only on a laptop camera.

## 2. Kitchen Sink Monochrome

**Family:** British New Wave, 1960-61.

**References**
- Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960, Karel Reisz; Freddie Francis), shot largely on location in Nottingham.
- A Taste of Honey (1961, Tony Richardson; Walter Lassally), shot on location in Salford, Manchester and Blackpool.

**Physical look**
- **Format:** black and white, both films being catalogued as such. Grain, lens choices and the shape of the frame are **[unverified]**.
- **Framing:** real streets with the town behind the person. My proposal is wider shots than modern drama, so the town's rooflines and terraces sit over Brandon's head.
- **Light:** my proposal is overcast daylight outside and hard practical lamps inside.
- **Bodies:** ordinary faces, with young leads who look like they work for a living.
- **This film:** phones, the app and the data centre appear in a look that belongs to 1960. The anachronism is left unexplained.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** it gives a protein-shake idiot the dignity of an angry young man. The form promises a working-class hero who sees through everything, and Brandon sees through nothing. Screens glowing in monochrome also look properly alien.

**Honest risk:** it can read as a pastiche, or a film-school joke, within a minute. Black and white may also make the water and data centre argument feel historical when it needs to feel current. The audience may hold it at a respectful distance.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** big ears, bad haircut, a tracksuit top zipped to the neck, like a boy in his dad's idea of smart.
- **Gary:** heavy brow, broken nose, donkey jacket over gym kit.
- **Giles:** smooth and pink-looking even in grey, a quilted gilet worn like a county squire's.

**Sources:**
[Saturday Night and Sunday Morning, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturday_Night_and_Sunday_Morning_(film)) ·
[A Taste of Honey, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Taste_of_Honey_(film))

## 3. Morgue Tableau

**Family:** deadpan formalism.

**References**
- Songs from the Second Floor (2000, Roy Andersson; István Borbás, Jesper Klevenas, Robert Komarek).
- A Pigeon Sat on a Branch Reflecting on Existence (2014, Roy Andersson; István Borbás, Gergely Pálos).
- Paradise: Love (2012, Ulrich Seidl; Wolfgang Thaler, Ed Lachman), a location-shot and improvised relative. I could not verify Seidl's frontal framing in a source **[unverified]**.

**Physical look**
- **Camera:** a perfectly static camera, one position per set, one long take per skit, deep focus.
- **Sets:** built in a studio, with handmade miniatures and trompe l'oeil depth.
- **Light and colour:** interiors "lit like morgues", a "jaundiced palette", chequered floors, oblique décor.
- **Bodies:** "pallid everymen". Andersson cast non-actors from the streets of Stockholm. Pale make-up is widely reported, but my sources did not confirm it **[unverified]**.
- **This film:** every room is one flat, overlit, sickly composition, with a background figure quietly doing something awful in the depth.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** this is the closest real-world form to skit-after-skit, because each shot already is a skit. The injury happens far from the lens and nobody reacts. A tiny surveillance detail in the background of an earlier scene pays off later, so the audience becomes the spy.

**Honest risk:** it is slow, and the South Park energy needs speed. The look also has its own strong authorship, so it may read as an Andersson imitation rather than a British film. Stillness suits a generator, but one fixed wide frame exposes every background error.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** waxy, damp-looking, in a too-tight compression top, arms held away from his sides.
- **Gary:** grey-yellow skin, heavy eye bags, a towel round his neck that has lost all its colour.
- **Giles:** the same pallor, a beige gilet, sitting perfectly still at a very long table.

**Sources:**
[Senses of Cinema](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2026/cteq/human-poems-roy-anderssons-songs-from-the-second-floor/) ·
[Songs from the Second Floor, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songs_from_the_Second_Floor) ·
[A Pigeon Sat on a Branch, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pigeon_Sat_on_a_Branch_Reflecting_on_Existence) ·
[Paradise: Love, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise:_Love)

## 4. Dated Primaries

**Family:** deadpan, heightened colour.

**References**
- Fallen Leaves (2023, Aki Kaurismäki; Timo Salminen). Salminen has shot every Kaurismäki film.
- Kaurismäki's wider work, as described by Little White Lies. Specific second titles are not separately verified here.

**Physical look**
- **Framing:** simple, uncluttered, with a quiet lyricism critics compare to Edward Hopper.
- **Colour:** pale green, flesh pinks and pillar-box reds, or rich reds, creams, greens and browns. Blue-green walls set off faces. It reads as painted rooms, not a colour grade.
- **Light:** key overhead lighting, sometimes natural light. A "dusky hue" holds across locations. Whether Fallen Leaves was shot on 35mm is **[unverified]**.
- **Bodies:** flat, deliberate performances, lived-in faces, slightly out-of-time clothes. The era of Fallen Leaves is deliberately unclear.
- **This film:** a red gym bench against a green wall, a yellow ambulance blanket, a community hall the colour of old mint.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** people say terrible things flatly in beautiful rooms. Brandon's gurus and Gary's betrayal are both funnier if nobody raises their voice. The tenderness also means that when the weight falls, it is a loss.

**Honest risk:** this style is gentle and forgiving, and may turn a frightening film wistful. The surveillance plot needs dread that this look does not naturally produce. It also needs real walls with real paint, since a generator may flatten this into "quirky" colour.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** long sad face, too-short brown trousers, a red zip-up from no recent decade.
- **Gary:** jowly and moustached, slicked hair, a powder-blue short-sleeved shirt over gym shorts.
- **Giles:** neat side parting, cream shirt, mustard gilet, and no visible pleasure in anything.

**Sources:**
[Little White Lies](https://lwlies.com/articles/the-strange-and-beautiful-world-of-aki-kaurismaki/) ·

## 5. Crew in the Room

**Family:** mockumentary.

**References**
- The Office (UK, 2001-03; Ricky Gervais and Stephen Merchant; Andy Hollis named as cinematographer). Single camera, no laugh track, an office built at Teddington Studios.
- This Country (2017-20; Daisy May and Charlie Cooper; directed by Tom George), shot on location around Northleach in the Cotswolds. No cinematographer is named in my source.

**Physical look**
- **Camera:** one operator, reacting a beat late. Snap zooms, focus hunting and shooting through doorways are genre habits I could not confirm for these titles **[unverified]**.
- **Light:** whatever the room has, including ceiling panels, a cold window and the fridge.
- **Colour:** unflattering and unstyled, the colour of real Britain on a Tuesday.
- **Bodies and wardrobe:** ordinary, a bit shapeless, clothes from where you would actually buy them.
- **This film:** talking heads to camera from Brandon, the neighbours and Giles. The camera catches Gary glancing at it.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** the look-to-camera is the great British comic device, and here it can turn. Who commissioned this documentary? If the crew turn out to be the contractor, the form itself is the surveillance, and every earlier interview becomes a debrief.

**Honest risk:** it is the most familiar option here, and it may feel like a thousand sketch shows. Viewers are also highly attuned to real faces in this form, so generated people may fail fastest here. And it needs improvised-feeling speech, which is hard to fake.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** eager and blinking, with a home-bleach fringe and a lanyard from a course he paid for.
- **Gary:** avoids the lens, thinning hair, fleece with a defunct company's logo.
- **Giles:** tanned, perfectly at ease on camera, grey gilet over a pale blue shirt with a visible pass.

**Sources:**
[The Office, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Office_(British_TV_series)) ·
[This Country, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_Country)

## 6. The Rig and the Feed

**Family:** surveillance, found footage.

**References**
- 24 Hours in A&E (Channel 4 documentary from The Garden): 70 fixed cameras running around the clock for 28 days, intercut with later interviews.
- Red Road (2006, Andrea Arnold; Robbie Ryan): a Glasgow CCTV operator, washed-out CCTV imagery, 4:3.
- Caché (2005, Michael Haneke; Christian Berger): long stationary takes on HD video, so tape and film become impossible to separate.
- Searching (2018, Aneesh Chaganty; Juan Sebastian Baron): told almost entirely through screens, shot on GoPros, webcams, mini DV and an iPhone.

**Physical look**
- **Camera:** high fixed corners and lenses with no operator. Nothing reframes for the joke.
- **Light:** only what the room offers. Night-mode grain in the attic, ambulance strip light, webcam blown-out windows.
- **Colour:** washed out and compressed, with timestamps, battery icons and notification banners.
- **Bodies:** seen from above and small. Faces mostly matter on video calls.
- **This film:** every setting has a plausible camera, including the gym, phone, doorbell, ambulance rig, council webcast and laptop. Who owns each feed is itself the plot.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** an injury in a fixed wide shot with no reaction cut is the purest slapstick there is. Once the plot turns, the audience realises that it has been watching the contractor's own footage. The coroner's office can open as the file being reviewed.

**Honest risk:** the look gets monotonous over feature length, and it is a thin way to love Brandon. It also hides performance, so a joke that needs a face must find a phone. Low resolution will flatter a generator, which may make this a lazy choice as well as a smart one.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** seen first as a shape in an oversized hoodie, then a raw-looking face lit by his phone.
- **Gary:** a bald patch from the ceiling camera, grey vest, always checking the door.
- **Giles:** a badly compressed face with a bookcase behind, and a navy gilet collar.

**Sources:**

## 7. Long Lens Paranoia

**Family:** 1970s paranoia thriller.

**References**
- The Parallax View (1974, Alan J. Pakula; Gordon Willis): anamorphic, long lens, unconventional framing, shallow focus, shadow-etched compositions. The hero is often filmed from great distances, suggesting he is being watched.
- The Conversation (1974, Francis Ford Coppola; Bill Butler, with Haskell Wexler uncredited). Wexler's footage of the Union Square surveillance scene was retained.
- Burn After Reading (2008, Joel and Ethan Coen; Emmanuel Lubezki), as the comic relative. Its plot of gym idiots caught up in intelligence work, and a satellite-style zoom, are from my memory **[unverified]**.

**Physical look**
- **Lens and frame:** wide-screen and long-lens, with figures small in big architecture and large blocks of empty wall or shadow.
- **Movement:** slow and patient. My proposal is a held shot, then a very slow zoom on an innocent conversation.
- **Light:** my proposal is one source and deep shadow in the corporate office, with the fluorescent gym going dim at the edges.
- **Colour and texture:** my proposal is muted browns, greys and institutional greens, with film grain. This grade is not sourced.
- **Bodies:** tired, ordinary people in unremarkable clothes who look like they are hiding something.
- **This film:** a planning meeting shot like an assassination, and a back-garden chat seen from far off through a hedge.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** it takes the idiot completely seriously. Brandon trying a cold shower, framed like a man being watched by the state, is funny for an hour. Later the audience learns he really is being watched, and the same frames stop being funny.

**Honest risk:** this is the obvious genre for the plot, and it can tip toward moody prestige darkness. It also gives the twist away if the first frame looks like a conspiracy. Very long-lens depth and big-space composition can look artificial in a generator.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** narrow shoulders lost in a brown anorak, thin moustache that isn't working.
- **Gary:** heavy tired eyes, grey car coat over a tracksuit, carrying a flask.
- **Giles:** long pale face in deep shadow, charcoal gilet, a silver pen he doesn't use.

**Sources:**
[The Parallax View, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Parallax_View) ·
[Criterion Channel](https://www.criterionchannel.com/videos/the-parallax-view) ·

## 8. Public Information Film

**Family:** British state-sponsored 16mm, 1970s-80s.

**References**
- Apaches (1977, John Mackenzie; Phil Méheux): 26 minutes on 16mm, for the Health and Safety Executive through the Central Office of Information. Children die one by one on a farm, a dead child keeps narrating calmly, and the credits list real child deaths.
- Threads (1984, Mick Jackson; Andrew Dunn, Paul Morris): BBC pseudo-documentary with a calm narrator, real Sheffield locations and about 1,100 candidates at open auditions. The capture format is **[unverified]**, though its surviving prints are 16mm.

**Physical look**
- **Format:** my proposal is soft 16mm colour, with visible grain and dull greens and browns, shot in flat daylight. The print-wear detail is mine.
- **Camera:** plain, functional and unshowy, placed to show what is about to go wrong.
- **Light:** English daylight, overcast, and fluorescent tubes indoors.
- **Graphics:** bald captions and a measured official voice stating facts over the action.
- **Bodies:** unknown faces and ordinary clothes. Threads went for relative unknowns on purpose.
- **This film:** each injury plays as its own safety film, with a warning caption and a calm voice. "Brandon did not check the bar."

**Why funnier or more frightening:** this one is built for the structure. A British safety film is already skit after skit of people being hurt for their own stupidity, narrated without pity. It also carries a real generational memory of being frightened by the state, which then gets very sinister when the "safety" voice turns out to be the contractor, or the app.

**Honest risk:** the audience that remembers these films is older than the audience following gurus, so the joke may land only for some. Modern gym and phone details may fight the period texture. Apaches lists real dead children, so borrowing its form must never touch that device.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** pudding-bowl hair, nylon tracksuit, freckled and cheerful, unaware he is the example.
- **Gary:** a council-worker face, sideburns, a short-sleeved shirt and tie under a gym towel.
- **Giles:** a Ministry man gone private, thin tie replaced by a bottle-green gilet, reading from a card.

**Sources:**

## 9. Archers Technicolor

**Family:** against the grain. Nobody would expect a wartime British romance look for a film about a gym idiot.

**References**
- A Matter of Life and Death (1946, Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger; Jack Cardiff). Earth is in full colour. The Other World was shot in three-strip Technicolor and printed without colour, giving a pearly monochrome. It has a 25-minute celestial trial on a vast set. Cardiff fogged the lens with his breath on the beach scene.
- Black Narcissus (1947, Powell and Pressburger; Jack Cardiff, Academy Award). Mostly shot at Pinewood, with the Himalayas built from landscape paintings, mattes and models.

**Physical look**
- **Colour:** full-blooded Technicolor reds, greens and flesh tones, on my reading of its reputation rather than a quoted source.
- **Sets:** openly constructed and painted. My proposal is a small English town with a painted sky and a hand-made data centre on the horizon.
- **Light:** my proposal is hard, sculpted studio key light on faces, with coloured gels in the attic at dusk.
- **Camera:** composed, graceful, unhurried, with dissolves between worlds.
- **Bodies:** my proposal is that everyone is groomed and glowing, even when bleeding.
- **This film:** the town is full colour, and the coroner's office is pearly monochrome, in direct echo of the Other World's trial.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** the gap between the image and the idiot is enormous. A cracked rib in a glorious sunset is very funny. It also suits the ending: an inquest staged as a cosmic court, with paperwork where the stairway to heaven should be. Corporate evil in such a beautiful town is properly unnerving.

**Honest risk:** the joke might be spent in ten minutes. The look also fights any feeling of present-day Britain, and the real water argument could become a fairy tale. It is the furthest from "realistic" here, though it is still photographic. Generators may slide toward generic retro glow.

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** rosy, brilliantined, with a wide innocent face and a crisp primary-red training vest.
- **Gary:** weathered and handsome in a ruined way, in a cream cable-knit sweater with a whistle.
- **Giles:** silver temples, an immaculate tweed gilet, a pocket square, a voice from another century.

**Sources:**
[A Matter of Life and Death, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Matter_of_Life_and_Death_(1946_film)) ·
[Black Narcissus, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Narcissus)

## 10. The Optimised Image

**Family:** hybrid invented for this film. The name and concept are mine. No existing film does exactly this, as far as I could establish **[unverified]**.

**Concept:** the picture gets better as the app takes over. It starts as harsh on-camera flash and ends as a bright, cheerful commercial. The audience can watch the film being optimised before Brandon notices anything.

**References**
- Martin Parr, The Last Resort (photobook, New Brighton, 1983-85, published 1986): close-up, saturated colour with flash, satirical, leaving the viewer unsure whether to laugh or cry. This is the start of the film.
- Nosedive (Black Mirror, 2016, Joe Wright; Seamus McGarvey): duck-egg blue, "peppermint green" and "strange peach", a "sickly pastel feel", and a different look for characters at different ratings. This is the middle.
- The Truman Show (1998, Peter Weir; Peter Biziou): a look shaped by TV commercials, with characters leaning into the lens with wide eyes and interiors heavily lit, plus shots informed by surveillance research. This is the end.
- Mommy (2014, André Turpin) is the precedent for a format carrying meaning. The frame changing size partway through is from my memory **[unverified]**.

**Physical look**
- **Stage one, before the app:** direct flash on skin, close, saturated, with visible sweat and pores. The attic is ugly and crowded, and the frame is square or nearly so.
- **Stage two, the app works:** the frame slowly widens, the flash softens, and pastels leak into his clothes and walls.
- **Stage three, full optimisation:** bright, even commercial light, no shadows on Brandon's face, characters leaning into the lens, and the town looking like a brochure.
- **The wobble:** Gary's face stays in stage one throughout, the one thing the system cannot retouch. My proposal is that the coroner's office returns to raw flash with a single hard click.

**Why funnier or more frightening:** the look itself is a character and a threat. Viewers will enjoy Brandon's improvement, and feel uneasy without knowing why. The brighter it gets, the more frightening it gets, so the film runs against the usual dark-means-danger grammar. The last hard flash then hits like a crime-scene photograph.

**Honest risk:** it is the hardest option to keep consistent, since three looks need three sets of rules and the steps between them must feel gradual. It may read as a clever idea that the audience notices once and then ignores. And Nosedive is close enough that some viewers will just say "Black Mirror".

**Characters**
- **Brandon:** starts blotchy, with a cold sore and grey joggers, and ends poreless, tanned and in peach athleisure.
- **Gary:** the same broken-veined face and the same washed-out grey T-shirt from first shot to last.
- **Giles:** always stage three, with even light, peppermint gilet and bright teeth, even on a bad video call.

[Nosedive, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nosedive_(Black_Mirror)) ·
[The Truman Show, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Truman_Show) ·
[Mommy, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mommy_(2014_film))
