# Cremonini — the man who won't look

**Double-cast.** Same face, twice: the Aristotelian philosopher at Padua who will
not put his eye to the glass (beat 2), and the peer reviewer who will not read the
data (beat 9). Never remarked on in dialogue.

Historically real: Cesare Cremonini, celebrated philosopher, colleague and drinking
companion of Galileo's, who is reported — in Galileo's own complaints, including
his 1610 letters — to have declined to look through the telescope. Our version is
that archetype, not a biography.

**He is not a priest, and that is the load-bearing fact of the entire story.** He
is a professor. He is the smartest man in the university. What he refuses to look
away from is not scripture — it's **Aristotle**: the previous generation's
science, canonised, with his own name on the syllabus. Every version of this
character in every era is defending a correct answer that stopped being checked.
(No bishop is recorded refusing to look. Don't hand this beat to a cleric.)

**He is the most important character in the story and he must be sympathetic.**

He is not stupid. He is not a coward, exactly. He is **mortgaged**. Thirty years
of published work, a chair, students who paid to be taught what he knows, a
reputation that arrives in the room before he does. If those four lights are
there, all of it is worth nothing by Thursday. Looking is not free. It has a
price and he can read the price off the tube from where he's standing, which is
why he doesn't need to look to know he can't afford to.

*(Historical fairness, kept in reserve: early telescopes were awful, and "I looked
and saw nothing" was a real and honest outcome for many. Our man never gets that
excuse — he doesn't look at all, which is the documented complaint and the
sharper beat.)*

The audience's first reaction should be contempt. The story's job is to turn that
around by beat 7 — where the audience does exactly what he does, at a dinner
table, and calls it being sensible.

**Modern variant** — the reviewer. Same refusal, same courtesy, better lighting.
He doesn't say no. He says *"it's not really falsifiable, is it,"* and slides the
proposal an inch back across the table, and asks kindly how her contract's
looking.

## Visual (house style: hyper-realistic documentary photography)

- 60s, long-faced, fine-featured, immaculately groomed. The face of a man who has
  been listened to for a very long time. Reading glasses. Soft hands.
- **1610 variant:** rich scholar's robes — better cloth than Galileo's, fur at the
  collar, a ring. Behind him, a shelf of books with his own name on the spines.
  Blocking to lock: the tube is **held out to him in frame**, and his hands stay
  folded. He is smiling politely. He is looking at Galileo, not at the tube.
- **Modern variant (beat 9):** the same face and build, grey-templed, a soft
  half-zip over an open collar, a conference lanyard, a wedding ring. Behind him,
  a shelf of books with his own name on the spines — **match the 1610 shelf
  framing exactly.**
- The two shots are a **match cut**: same eyeline, same distance, same folded
  hands, same offered object (a brass tube / a stack of paper). The audience
  should feel the four hundred years collapse without being told.
