# The frame — composition, light, colour, lens

Everything that is decided inside one rectangle, before anything moves or cuts. This is where
most of our work lives, because most of our shots are stills.

---

## 1. Depth is the first thing to fix

**Foreground, midground, background.** A subject on one plane against a backdrop reads as flat
no matter how sharp, detailed or well-exposed it is. Resolution never fixes flatness; layers do.

For our subject matter, the foreground layer is usually free: a doorway edge, a cable run, a
handrail, a stack, a pipe, haze. It does not need to be interesting — it needs to be *nearer*.

**Atmospheric perspective is real optics, not a look.** Dust and haze in the air scatter light
and soften contrast with distance, and it is one of the primary cues human vision uses to judge
how far away something enormous is. In a vast dark interior, haze is doing depth work no amount
of geometry can. **A**

## 2. The focal point

One place for the eye to rest, or the viewer scans, settles nowhere, and reports the frame as
cluttered. Give the focal point the light, the space, or the depth position — and take those
away from everything else.

**Negative space is the cheapest way to manufacture one.** Emptiness is not wasted frame; it can
carry the whole emotional weight of a shot — isolation, scale, dread — on its own.

**The eye's real priority order** is motion, then faces and high-contrast regions, then the
composition's lines and depth cues. Compose with that order rather than against it. Leading lines
work and it is measured, not just felt: viewers fixate longer and more purposefully when a line
leads to a clear subject than when the same line leads to nothing.

## 3. Tension versus resolution

Same vocabulary, opposite directions:

| Resolved / "pretty" | Unresolved / "tense" |
| --- | --- |
| Balanced weight | Imbalance |
| Centred subject | Off-centre subject with lines converging on it |
| Symmetry | Asymmetry |
| Correct headroom, noseroom, lead room | Deliberately violated — the frame about to run out of room |
| Everything shown | Something withheld — partial view, shadow, obstruction |
| Closed frame | Implied world past the edge |

**Headroom, noseroom and lead room are one rule:** leave space in the direction attention wants
to travel — up from a head, ahead of a gaze, ahead of motion. **Short-siding** (subject near the
edge, looking *away* from the open space) is the deliberate violation and it signals isolation
and unease.

**Bazin's distinction is the theory under all of this.** A painting's frame is *centripetal* —
self-contained. A film frame is *centrifugal* — it implies the world continuing past it. Showing
everything is what makes a frame read as a diorama. **A (mixed)**

**Symmetry has no fixed meaning.** Kubrick's one-point perspective produces dread; Wes Anderson's
produces safety and comedy. If a shot needs symmetry to feel monumental, the monumentality is
coming from the darkness, the light and the scale contrast — not from the symmetry.

## 4. Staging without actors

Blocking survives our constraints far better than it looks, because it is a decision about
**geometry**, not about directing a performer — and geometry can be specified before a frame
exists.

- **Depth planes, not a line-up.** Subjects side by side on one plane read as a photograph of a
  stage.
- **Proxemics.** The gap between two figures maps to real interpersonal distance and signals
  intimacy, tension or estrangement before anyone speaks.
- **Status cues stack.** Closer to camera, larger in frame, facing camera reads dominant;
  smaller, further, turned away reads subordinate. Combine two or three; never rely on one.
- **A frame-within-a-frame** — doorway, window, mirror, structural division — does narration's
  job for free: contained, trapped, observed.
- **Architecture and machines stage exactly like actors.** Which structure is closer, which is
  lit, which blocks the other, which dominates the vertical — the same power grammar as a
  two-shot. This matters because many of our shots have no human in them at all.
- **An empty frame before or after a figure is part of the sentence.** Entry and exit are
  punctuation.

## 5. Light

**Name the source, then claim the shadow.** "Dark" is not an instruction. One hard source from a
named place, plus an explicit statement of where the falloff goes, is.

| Lever | Reads as |
| --- | --- |
| Hard light (small/distant source, sharp shadow edge) | Harsh, exposing, unforgiving — **optical fact** |
| Soft light (large/close source, gradual falloff) | Gentle, flattering, safe — **optical fact** |
| Front | Flat, honest |
| Side (45°) | Classic dimensional modelling |
| Three-quarter / back | Mystery, threat |
| Under | Horror, unnatural |
| Silhouette | Anonymity, myth |
| Ratio ~2:1 | Calm, natural |
| Ratio 8:1+ | Dramatic, morally ambiguous |

**Three-point lighting is a teaching diagram.** Working DPs routinely drop the fill entirely and
let one side go dark.

**🔴 One bright anchor inside the darkness, always.** The visual system computes how dark
something is relative to the brightest region it can group into the same scene. With no anchor
there is nothing to normalise against, and near-black reads as a broken file rather than as
chosen darkness. A rim, a practical, a screen, an eye-light. *(principle 11 — the most
load-bearing rule we have)*

**Rim light and silhouette** are how a dark subject stays readable against a dark background:
separate by edge, never by lifting the whole frame.

**Underexposure is a legitimate choice, committed at capture.** Every named practitioner who
does it says the same thing — decide it when the image is made, don't try to rescue it after.

## 6. Colour

- **Plan the palette for the whole piece first.** We cannot fix drift across a shooting day the
  way a DP can; every frame is made separately and will wander. A restricted palette is also a
  technical advantage — fewer places for a model to drift off-brief between shots.
- **A colour code is a flag, not a symbol.** One colour, one idea, appearing only when that thing
  is present. The power is rarity and consistency; the specific hue is arbitrary.
- **Warm against cool in one frame** is most of what viewers mean by production value.
- **Complementary contrast is a pop — spend it once.** Opposite hues genuinely produce maximum
  simultaneous contrast (**A**). The add-on claim that "orange advances, blue recedes" is
  folklore riding on that real base fact.
- **Desaturation has a cost** — it removes the audience's fastest source of mood information — so
  it needs a reason. "We ran out of colour ideas" is not a colour script.
- **Fixed colour-to-emotion codes are myth.** The lab evidence is real but almost entirely
  single swatches on blank backgrounds; it tells you about a colour chip, not a lit, graded,
  composed frame.

## 7. Lens, height and angle

**The distortion is the distance, not the lens.** Same distance, same subject size, any focal
length gives identical geometry — changing lens without moving changes only field of view. The
"wide-angle close-up is aggressive" effect is inseparable from the fact that the framing
*requires* proximity. **A**

| Choice | Does this |
| --- | --- |
| Wide / short focal length | Embeds subject in environment; space expands; background recedes fast |
| Long / telephoto | Flattens depth; isolates; cuts the subject out of the world |
| Shallow depth of field | Tells the eye exactly where to look |
| Deep focus | Refuses to choose; forces the viewer to scan and decide — the stronger choice when the *relationship between planes* is the point |
| Rack focus | A reveal without a cut; reads as discovery rather than edit |

**Height and angle are separate variables.** The lens can sit low and stay level, or sit high and
tilt down. Most sources conflate them. Treat height as a slider — and note that the one study to
test it found only ~30° differences produced clear effects, so a nudge does nothing.

**"Low angle equals power" is weak and context-dependent.** One narrative study found it; one
direct-address trust study found eye-level was trusted *more* than either low or high angle;
another found it running backwards. Decide whether you are optimising for dramatic dominance or
for credibility — they pull opposite ways.

**The overhead is our narrator's natural position** — omniscience, irony, dread, not contempt.
But it buys omniscience free and must earn scale separately: from above, an empty space collapses
into pattern rather than absence.

**The Dutch angle dies fastest from overuse.** The same critics who canonise Carol Reed for it
savage everyone who repeats it past meaning.
