# Motion and cutting — movement, sequence, and re-cutting what already exists

Where the excitement actually lives. Our shots are stills and 8-second clips, so almost none of
our energy comes from within a shot — it comes from **which shots, in what order, held how
long.** This file owns that.

---

## 1. Movement

**The one question.** *What does this movement mean that stillness would not?* No answer, no
move. **P**

**🔴 House ruling, 2026-08-26 — ask it twice, because there are two movements.** *Does the world
move?* and *does the camera move?* are separate questions with separate answers and separate tools:
Veo animates the world with its camera locked, post moves the camera over the finished clip. The
motion budget below governs **camera** movement only — a locked frame full of working machinery
spends none of it. Nor does an 8-second generation cap set a beat's length; clips chain frame to
frame. How: [`../video-fx/hybrid-method.md`](../video-fx/hybrid-method.md). **H**

**Dolly and zoom are not interchangeable.** A dolly physically moves the camera, so the
background parallax shifts and depth feels real. A zoom magnifies a flat image. If you want the
viewer to feel they are *approaching* something, only one of the two does it. **A**

**The push-in is the workhorse, and it means one specific thing** — growing psychological
attention, narrowing toward a realisation. It is also the one move an 8-second generated clip
executes cleanly. Two facts that happen to agree: use it, but put something at the end of the
push worth arriving at. **P** / **p**

**The dolly zoom is a cliché now** — not because the effect is worn out but because directors
reach for it to announce "big reveal" instead of to encode a specific rupture in a character's
reality. **P**

**Stillness is a discipline, not a budget.** Ozu locked the camera down entirely; Haneke uses
the static frame to trap the viewer inside a character's logic. Neither is doing less work.
**P**

**🔴 The economy that governs our whole form:** in a body of work built from static or
near-static shots, **a single moving shot lands with outsized force — and only if the stillness
around it is real rather than cheap.** *La Jetée* spends roughly 26 minutes as photographs and
cashes its one moving shot exactly once. Budget motion like a scarce resource: motivated
stillness is free and always available; motivated movement is worth spending maybe once per
sequence. **P**

**Handheld is not truth.** It is a style that has been criticised as lazy shorthand for
authenticity. Plenty of directors get more subjective urgency from a rock-steady camera. *(ruling
R7)* **P**

**The long take buys immersion and is also, by named practitioners, showing off.** Both readings
are legitimate and depend entirely on whether the story needed it. **P**

## 2. Designing a sequence

**A scene is a small argument.** Every shot is a step in it, or it is decoration. This is the
single most useful sentence in the sweep for judging a cut. **P**

The method, in order:

1. **Name the argument.** What does this scene assert that the one before it did not? One
   sentence.
2. **Establish geography once, early.** One wide, one time. Every closer shot afterwards then
   spends its whole budget on feeling instead of re-explaining where we are. **P**
3. **Pick a direction for shot size and hold it.** Wide→close is the default shape and reads as
   *moving toward the truth*. Inversions are deliberate craft. Random size-jumping reads as chaos
   because the viewer cannot tell what the change means. **P**
4. **Decide what is withheld, and for how long.** A reveal is a withheld shot, not an effect.
   And suspense beats surprise — tell the audience the bomb is under the table. *(This is already
   our narrator's structural advantage: it knows how it ends. `story-craft` calls it free
   dramatic irony.)* **P**
5. **Set a pattern so you can break it once.** A repeating rhythm, size or tempo exists so the
   break reads as a break. **P**
6. **End on a button.** One shot, often small, closing the transaction the scene opened.
   Punctuation, not whatever frame was left over. **P**

**A montage is a scene, not a pile of clips** — it has setup, escalation and a resolution shot of
its own. **P**

**Coverage is not our problem and not our method.** Shoot-everything-decide-later is the opposite
philosophy to a designed shot list, we cannot afford it, and we do not need it. *(ruling R5)*

## 3. The cut

**Emotion first, continuity last.** When you cannot have everything, protect the audience's read
of the *feeling* and the *story point*. Screen direction and axis geometry are what you sacrifice
first, not last. **P**

**A hard cut between two unrelated images is never neutral.** The viewer's brain invents a
relationship. Use it deliberately or it uses you — this is the entire toolkit of satire and
political documentary, and it is the mechanism our form runs on. The narrow version is
well-evidenced (and has 2024 fMRI support); the famous "one face, three emotions" story failed a
controlled replication and is myth in its popular form. *(ruling R4)* **A**

**Cut on movement.** A hand starting to move, a head starting to turn — the eye tracks motion,
not frame boundaries, so the cut hides inside it. **P**

**There is no correct shot length.** A still held two seconds too long dies; held exactly long
enough it becomes the moment people remember. Content sets the length, never a stopwatch. A
talking head with new information every second earns 6–8s; a wide with nothing left to read past
2s should cut at 2s. **p**

**Fast is not exciting and slow is not boring.** Each produces a different kind of tension.
Contrast between them is what makes either land. Average shot length has genuinely shortened
across a century of film — that is a measured trend, not a law. **A** (the measurement) / **P**
(the craft reading)

**Sound leading or lagging the picture (J-cut / L-cut)** makes two unrelated images feel like one
continuous thought. Cheapest transition upgrade available to us, and it costs nothing but edit
time. **P**

**🔴 Know both readings of the Adam Curtis method before using it.** Practitioners praise the
same juxtaposition technique that documentary theorists call intellectually dishonest. We run two
tracks knowingly; `story-craft` gate G3 is the tripwire — a juxtaposition that lets the
beneficiary go ambiguous has become the thing his critics name. **Contested, both sides real**

## 4. 🔴 Re-cutting footage that already exists

The common BadCode situation: the clips are made, they are on a timeline, and the cut is not
working. This is a different job from designing a scene, because the shot inventory is fixed.

**Do this in order. Do not start by generating more clips.**

1. **State the argument the scene is supposed to make.** One sentence. If you cannot, the
   problem is upstream in the canon, not in the edit — go to `story-craft`.
2. **List what you have as jobs, not as clips.** For each: what does this shot *do*? Establish,
   escalate, reveal, dwell, punctuate, or nothing. Shots that do nothing are the answer to
   "why is this flat" more often than any framing note.
3. **Find the direction.** Lay the shot sizes out in order. Is there a shape, or is it random?
   Imposing a direction on existing material is free and is usually the single biggest
   improvement available.
4. **Test the button.** Does the last shot close what the first one opened? If not, one of the
   shots you already have is probably the real ending and is currently sitting in the middle.
5. **Cut the shots that repeat.** Two shots doing the same job means one of them is padding, and
   the weaker one is diluting the stronger. This is where most length comes out.
6. **Re-time before you re-shoot.** Length is the cheapest variable you own. Most "this drags"
   notes are solved by cutting each shot to the moment it stops earning, not by new footage.
7. **Then, and only then, name the gaps.** What job is missing that no existing shot does? That
   is your generation list — usually one or two shots, not a re-shoot.

**What you can add without generating anything:** order, duration, a J-cut or L-cut, a held beat
of black, a caption carrying the beneficiary, a bookend made by repeating an existing frame at
the end.

## 5. Continuity — what actually applies to us

**Audiences track actions and goals, not camera geometry.** A cut breaking spatial or temporal
continuity is barely noticed if the *action* flows; a cut breaking the action is noticed even
when space and time are fine. Viewers only clock an error where they are already looking —
usually a face. **A**

**Most continuity machinery is not our problem.** The 180-degree rule, eyeline matching and the
triangle govern cutting between multiple angles of one continuous action. We do not shoot
coverage, so they do not apply. *(ruling R3)*

**What does apply, in a scene with no people:** consistent light direction, consistent scale and
proportion, and object permanence. That is what a viewer uses to build a mental model of a place.
**P**

**The 30-degree rule is worth keeping.** Two shots of the same subject from near-identical
angles read as a position jump, not a new viewpoint. Cut wide-to-close or change the axis; never
nudge. **P**

**When you must break a convention, the fix is not to obey it** — it is to give the eye an
unambiguous anchor: a moving element, a strong graphic line, a beat of black, a caption. **P**

**An establishing shot is a convenience, not a requirement.** Anything that answers *where and
when* will do — a sound cue, a caption, a graphic match, one unambiguous prop. **P**
