title: Education
line: Keep the bottom rung on the ladder, and let trainees learn the job before they mark a machine's.
---
## Position

How do you check a machine's homework if nobody ever let you do the homework? Who decided a graduate's first job would be marking it? And what happens to the next layer up in ten years, when nobody underneath it learned the work?

The BBC this week met new graduates who spend much of their working day supervising AI agents and checking what they produce. The worry is simple: they learn to manage the work before they learn to do it. That isn't the graduates' fault, and it isn't the universities'. An employer designed that first job. It can design it differently.

So we propose learning time. Where an employer puts AI agents into entry-level roles, part of each trainee's week goes on doing the underlying work themselves, with someone senior checking it, before they are asked to sign off a machine's version.

The test. A one-year pilot with volunteer employers and their local colleges in a few towns, with a comparison group that works without it. At the end, both groups do the job unaided. If the learning-time trainees aren't measurably better at it, or employers walk away because it costs too much, we publish why and drop it.

Who it leaves out: everyone who never got through the door. The pilot helps people with a first job. Young people with no first job are the bigger number, and they are counted alongside it, every quarter, so nobody mistakes a good pilot for a fixed ladder.
