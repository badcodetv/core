A sensor can tell you she fell. It can't pick her up. No council swaps her visit for a screen
without her say-so.

Her carer drives between visits. One home care firm in Mansfield and Northampton paid its carers
5p a mile for that drive, until the Fair Work Agency found it: £107,935.96 owed, and 40p a mile now.

Who decided. Councils buy home care, under rules ministers set. A report for the Casey Commission
finds nobody in charge: private providers came to dominate in a largely uncontrolled way, and people
paying for their own care pay on average 40% more than people their council supports. On 12
November ministers asked councils to buy home care by neighbourhood, one or two main providers in
each. The Bristol study they cite found the same care with 35% less staff time and 65% less
driving. Guidance comes in the spring. Nobody has said where the saved time goes.

The rule. Volunteer councils buy home care by the hour and by neighbourhood, not by the task. The
hours the road gives back stay in care in that neighbourhood, written into the contract: first to
people assessed and waiting there, then into longer visits. Counted from the council's visit
records, published. No visit is replaced by a sensor or a screen unless the person, or whoever
speaks for them, agrees in writing. If it stops, nobody loses an hour on the way out: each person
keeps the care they had.

The ledger. Councils keep paying for the same hours; the time the road gave back goes to people,
not off the rota. A provider that banks the saving as fewer hours loses the contract. Medium: £100m
to £1bn a year, once national.
