A village in North Yorkshire has learned the Home Office may put up to 1,200 asylum seekers in the
old RAF base down the road. Nobody asked it. Who decided, who pays, and where's the extra GP?

Migration has a cost, and it lands at the bottom. The government's own advisers found EU
migration from 1993 to 2017 went with nominal pay about 5% lower for the lowest-paid UK-born
workers and about 4% higher for the best-paid: a short-run effect they expect to fade. Over the same
years real pay at the bottom rose 55%. We print the size with every official release, beside the
homes built.

So the roof comes with the arrival. Work visas and their families don't grow faster than the homes,
GP and school places funded for them, paid for first from what the closing asylum hotels cost.
Where the homes aren't built, the number falls. Refugees aren't capped; their arrival brings the
roof money.

The hotel money is the first payment, not the last: 16,021 people were in asylum hotels at the end
of June 2026, half the year before, so it shrinks as they close. After it, MPs vote the roof money with
the number. No money voted, no rise in the number.

The cap is one national number, so it can't be tried in two towns; the trial tests the roof it
rests on.

Who decided. Yes, it's a cost. Whitehall sets it alone today. Under us, MPs vote on it alone, once
a year, in public.

Nothing is retrospective.

The ledger. What the closing hotels cost pays first, then the Treasury by the yearly vote;
councils, GPs and schools in arrival towns gain. Large: over £1bn a year once national.
