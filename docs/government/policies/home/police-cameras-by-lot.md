Police cameras. On 4 November the Home Office promised a regulator, bias tests and £11 million to
triple the live facial recognition vans. Right rules, wrong order.

The wrong. A shop camera catches a face. An officer runs the still against the custody pictures and
the computer offers a name. The police inspectorate found some officers treating that match as
evidence, not a lead, and relying on it without enough else. It called that deeply
concerning. It also found forces keeping custody pictures that should have been deleted.

Who decided. Ministers, by leaving it to each force. No law is written for this, and each force's
rules differ. The inspectorate wants a statutory code in the coming Police Reform Bill.

The rule. One code in that Bill, for live cameras and old footage alike. Two things wait for no
lot: deleting what the law says must go, and the right to a human at the stop. Every force has
both from day one, and neither ends with the trial. The rest goes first to volunteer forces drawn
by lot, all at once. A match is a lead, not a fact about you: an officer decides, and says so, at
the stop. Nobody is arrested on a match alone; the arrest record says what else the officer had,
and someone outside the force reads it. Each arrest found resting on the match alone is a breach:
the person is told, and the force answers for it to the watchdog, in public. So is each picture
found held past its date, and it goes that day.

The ledger. Small, and the Home Office pays: deletions, records, the inspectorate's reading,
an officer's time to check. Who gains: anyone the computer gets wrong.

Each force wrote its own rules. The Bill can write one.
