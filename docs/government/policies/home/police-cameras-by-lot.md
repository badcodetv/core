Police cameras. On 4 November the Home Office promised a regulator, bias tests and £11 million to
triple the live facial recognition vans. Right rules, wrong order.

The wrong. A shop camera catches a face. An officer runs the still against the custody pictures and
the computer offers a name. The police inspectorate found some officers treating that match as
evidence rather than a lead, and relying on it without enough else. It called that deeply
concerning. It also found forces keeping custody pictures that should have been deleted.

Who decided. Ministers, by leaving it to each force. There is no law written for this, and each sets its own
rules, which differ. The inspectorate wants a statutory code in the coming Police Reform Bill.

The rule. One code in that Bill, for live cameras and for searches of old footage alike. It goes
first to volunteer forces drawn by lot, all of it at once: the vans, the old-footage searches, the
deletions. A match is a lead, not a fact about you: an officer decides, and says so, at the stop.
Nobody is arrested on a match alone; the arrest record says what else the officer had, and someone
outside the force reads it. Each arrest found resting on the match alone is a breach: the person is
told, and the force answers for it to the watchdog, in public. A custody picture the law says must
go is deleted, and each force publishes the count.

The ledger. Small, and the Home Office pays: the deletions, the records, the inspectorate's reading,
an officer's time to check. Who gains: anyone the computer gets wrong.

Each force wrote its own rules. The Bill can write one.
