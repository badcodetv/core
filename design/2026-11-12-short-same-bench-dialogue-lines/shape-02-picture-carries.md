# Shape 02: The picture carries it

The pictures already say who is on the bench, what he did to them and that he never changes, so the words only do what a drawing cannot: the one shared line, and the button. Every line the image can say (war, wounds, thanks, pay, the same face) is cut. Pushed to the extreme, the whole short is two spoken words, repeated, and a button that is a silent action. The echo is built by the drawing: identical framing, identical pose, same bench, only the person next to him changes.

## Dialogue 1: Same Bench: the whole short

Shots relied on: **A1** wide locked frame of the 1919 bench, fat cat centre-left, veteran with crutch and one trouser leg pinned up, a faded "WELCOME HOME" bunting scrap at his feet; **B1** the identical frame today, same bench, same cat, same pose, nurse with a lanyard and a dropped clapping-hands poster in the gutter; **C1** hold on the cat's face in both eras, split as a diptych, identical.

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FAT CAT (1919, to the veteran) | [not looking up from his paper, flicks a hand] Forget it. | 1.2 |
| (A to B, picture only) | [bunting scrap on the ground; match cut, only the bench's paint and the passer-by's clothes change] | 0 |
| FAT CAT (today, to the nurse) | [not looking up from his phone, same flick of the hand] Forget it. | 1.2 |
| BUTTON (silent action, no words) | [C1: the two Forget-its play as a diptych, same hand flick in both; his top hat is the only thing that holds the same height in both panels; he lifts his paper to sneeze and it is the 1919 newspaper in his hand again] | 0 |
| CAPTION (optional, over the diptych) | Same bench. | 1.0 |
| Total | | 3.4 spoken, 14 s picture |

Button alternatives (pick one; all use the C1 diptych):
1. **Silent:** the cat checks a pocket watch, a 1919 one, then a smartwatch on the other wrist. Same face, both wrists. No words, no caption.
2. **Sound:** a single park-bench creak and a distant, thin clap that stops after one. Nothing said.
3. **CAPTION only:** "Same bench." (shown on the right panel only, the left panel stays blank).
4. **Spoken, one word:** FAT CAT: [to camera, bored] Next. (swaps the nurse out for the next person approaching, a figure in the park gates, unfinished, a sketch outline.)
5. **Spoken, two words:** NARRATOR (dry): Same man. (over the diptych, flat).

**Why it works.** The audience sees a veteran with a pinned trouser leg and a nurse with a lanyard and instantly knows who carries the cost; no line has to say so. Repeating "Forget it." in the identical flick, framed identically, makes the echo the entire joke, and because the cat is the only one who speaks, he is the decision-maker and the target. The soldier and nurse are silent, which makes them dignified rather than pitiable. The button is a shot, not a sentence: the unaged face next to itself reads as "same man, every time" without a moral being stated.

**Cuts from current.** No spoken button. No dialogue for the veteran or nurse. No explicit era cards (the bunting and the clothes date it). No "thanked then forgotten" line: the bunting scrap (A1) and the dropped clapping poster (B1) carry it. The 1919 caption's wording is nowhere.

**Risk.** It lives or dies on the drawing: if the two frames do not match at a glance, or the poster and bunting are too small to read at 9:16, the "thanked" half is lost and it is only a rude man twice. TTS "Forget it." must be dry, not angry; two identical reads can feel robotic unless the second is a hair more bored. A silent button can fall flat on autoplay-muted feeds, so the caption "Same bench." is the safety net. Gatekeeper check: the cat must not read as a stock caricature of a real person.

**Protected lines.** "Forget it." survives verbatim, twice, as the only spoken words.
