Museum of Old Tech

The voting system sits in a glass case next to the gas lamp and the telegraph, with a polite label saying it is still in daily use. The deck is a gallery tour of obsolete machines, and the joke is that one of them still runs the country.

Serves: the "older than the light bulb" hook, quietly and with total authority. Feels calm and trustworthy for a sceptical reader.
Undermines: pace. A museum is slow, and the memes have to bring all the energy.

Update Available

Britain is an old computer that has been clicking "remind me later" since 1872. Every slide is a desktop with a pop-up on it: error boxes, progress bars stuck at 3%, patch notes. Error-box jokes are already a meme language people know.

Serves: pillar 2 (laws that update and can be rolled back) and the "we have the technology" hook. Fast and very meme-native.
Undermines: it is a tech joke, and the reader rules say name the decision-maker, not the technology. The pop-ups must blame people, not the machine.

Bundle Deal

The corner shop and the big supermarket: yellow price stickers, shelf-edge labels and till receipts. A party is a multipack you have to buy whole, and the deck keeps asking why you can't just buy the one thing you came in for.

Serves: pillar 1 dead centre, in the reader's own everyday world. Loud, cheap-looking on purpose, and the funniest of the four.
Undermines: it can look like a discount ad. It needs the narrator's authority to stop it reading as a gimmick.

Building Site

From the October Future Proof microsite: daylight, blueprints, hi-vis and hoardings. Politics is under construction and the deck is the planning notice. This is the one you already agreed to in spirit (Future Proof is the first lit place in BadCode).

Serves: continuity with the microsite, and the idea that we are building something, not just moaning.
Undermines: it is the most "designed" and the least meme-like; building-site jokes are thinner than shop or computer jokes.
