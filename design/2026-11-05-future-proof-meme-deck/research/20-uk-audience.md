Searches used: 7 of 8. Source grades: PRIMARY (pollster/org itself), SECONDARY (press/campaign group reporting a poll), HEARSAY (forum, snippet, or my inference). Several figures came via search snippets, not opened pages; re-check before any number goes on a slide.

## Headline
The cross-party anger is real and polled. The *comedy-register* evidence is thin: no source I found tests which humour styles land with working-class or right-drifting UK audiences. Section 3 is therefore my judgment, labelled as such.

Trap from the PR data: do NOT make Pillar 1 about proportional representation or "the voting system". Reform voters' support has softened as Reform's odds rose, and PR is a party-fight. Unbundling a *choice* (vote on issues, not a party package) is a different, less loaded ask. Lead with the lived annoyance, never the electoral-reform label.

## 3. What humour lands (JUDGMENT, not sourced; test it)
- Parties already run memes deliberately: Labour's pre-2025-locals "shareables" against Reform, strategist logic "memes are cognitive shortcuts". SECONDARY: https://labourlist.org/2025/04/labour-memes-nigel-farage-reform-uk-local-elections-2025 . Reform pitches itself as digitally native (their own framing, GB News: https://www.gbnews.com/politics/nigel-farage-reform-uk-gen-z-labour-keir-starmer-democraphic ). So the format is not neutral ground: a meme deck that looks like party content will be read as party content. Show the target is a *mechanism*, not a team.
- Daily Mash: audience skew reads as office workers (Wikipedia mirror, HEARSAY); its satire hits all sides; Mash Report cancelled 2021. Takeaway only: equal-opportunity mockery is a known, accepted British register.
- No usable evidence found on Al Murray, Joe Lycett, Limmy, or pothole humour. Do not cite them as proof of what lands; at most use them as tonal references (deadpan, pub-voice, swearing-adjacent understatement, mock-incompetence) and say it's a hunch.
- Working hypotheses to test with the pub-argument rule (the-reader.md rule 8):
  1. Joke at the system's absurdity, never at the viewer ("hero, not pupil").
  2. Domestic, concrete objects (kettle, bill, bin, menu, pothole) beat abstractions (governance, mandate).
  3. A joke the viewer can repeat to a mate in one sentence.
  4. Second-person blame and "wake up" phrasing are out (the-reader.md rules 1-2).
  5. Name the chooser (water boards, whips, a rule from 1832-era Westminster), not "politics" or "AI".

## 4. Entry ramps per pillar
Pillar 1, composition over inheritance (vote per issue, not per party bundle):
- Best ramp: **the bundle** - you want the water policy from one party and the border policy from another and the box makes you pick a whole package. Frame as a shopping/menu joke ("meal deal you can't unbundle"), not electoral reform. Corroborated by Small Ideas itself: the public gets to pick individual small fixes, 50 of them, no party attached.
- Second ramp: MPs' second jobs (bundle of duties vs. a bundle of paymasters) - weaker, only a generic poll behind it.
- Avoid: "PR". Reform support is falling and the term triggers party loyalty.

Pillar 2, continuous integration (testable, continuous governance):
- Best ramp: **water**. A decade of promised fixes, bills up, nobody checking the build. "Imagine shipping this and never running a test." Cross-party anger, both Reform and Green voters rank it high, and a decision-maker is nameable (regulators, owners, the ministers who approved it).
- Second ramp: potholes (visible, daily, local; no polling, treat as instinct) - a bug report that sits unfixed for years.

Pillar 3, data-driven decisions (no proposal without its experiment):
- Best ramp: **energy bills / "Britain is broken" promises**: someone promised it would get cheaper, show the test they ran. Not polled in this pass.
- Second ramp: Small Ideas - a rare case of ideas gathered *before* policy. Use it as the positive example: "this is what proposing, then testing, looks like".
- Mind the trap: "the data says" can sound like technocrats overriding the reader. Pair with a person's consequence (rule 7, first-person account).
