# Critique of synthesis.md (adversarial pass, 2026-11-05)

Checked against: the-reader.md, voice.md, research 10-21. Ranked worst first. Slide numbers are the synthesis's.

## Ranked problems

### 1. The deck tells the reader how the system works; it rarely tells a story (lecture, whole spine)
Pillar 1 and 3 are "mechanism, mechanism, citation". Slides 11 (Ostrogorski), 19 (post-legislative review), 20 (FCA sandbox), 24-27 (four evidence slides in a row) are essay beats in slide form. Voice.md: "Don't lecture." Reader rule 1: hero, not pupil. There is no protagonist: nobody in the deck is wronged and then fixed. The only person is Kai.
**Fix:** run one person through all three pillars (one invented character, a Bob from a town with a bad bus, a closed A&E, whatever the story picks). Pillar 1 is their ballot. Pillar 2 is the rule that hurt them being "tested" on them for five years. Pillar 3 is the letter they got. Cut 11, 19, 20 or fold each into one line spoken over a character slide.

### 2. Slide 13 and slide 12 walk into Brexit without naming it
"Not referendums" plus a Swiss-referendum slide (332 votes) will make this reader hear one thing: the 2016 vote. Slide 13 pre-empts the objection by stating it, which is rule 10 (never repeat the lie to refute it) in reverse: it raises the loaded word. Slide 12 also says nothing about Swiss results that this reader may like (immigration-quota votes) or hate; "can go either way" is a trap the synthesis flags and then ignores in the slide.
**Fix:** drop slide 13 as a standalone. Reframe 12 as "a Swiss ballot has several boxes; you tick each one", visual only, no count, no outcome. Handle "so, referendums?" in Q&A notes, not on a slide.

### 3. Slide 31 sets Kai against Martin Lewis (trusted by this reader, and a trap legally)
Expanding Brain with "Panel picks 50" at the bottom and "vote per idea" at the top ranks MSE's project as the dumb brain. Lewis is among the few figures this audience trusts; mocking a well-meant effort reads as smug and as punching at someone doing it for free. It also implies a relationship and rests on nothing verified: research 10 never opened the live site, the deadline year is assumed, panel names are one outlet. Naming him on the slide is also near the "no impersonation / no implied endorsement" line.
**Fix:** reframe as "yes, and": "This is the instinct. Here is what the next step would look like." No ladder of brains. Do not name the site on a slide until Kai has opened it and read who runs it and how it ends. Ship the topical version only if verified, with no logos, colours or face. Otherwise cut 31 and keep 32-33.

### 4. The word "experts" in the CTA (5, 36) and the self-undermining "Probably wrong. Posted on purpose."
This reader trusts almost nobody and "ten thousand experts will turn up to correct you" tells them the deck's audience is a crowd of credentialed correctors. Slide 5's "Probably wrong" also breaks voice dial 3 (total authority) just before three claims we then lean on. It is Cunningham's law, an internet-native joke that this reader does not use.
**Fix:** CTA = "Write one rule. Say how we'd know. Show it to the person it would hit." Drop "experts". Keep humility in the construction-site slide 34, where it is in character; keep the narrator certain everywhere else.

### 5. Shaky or invented numbers on slides (synthesis flags some, misses others)
- **Slide 4:** "Bank: 10 questions for £50" is unsourced and sounds exact. Use "a bank asks you to confirm it is you" with no number, or find a source.
- **Slide 24:** "a pill on 500 people" is invented. Trial sizes vary by phase; say "years of trials" or cite a real phase table.
- **Slide 25:** "67.5% -> 79.0%" compares the control letter to the best variant of 11, and research 14 says the sample sizes differ by summary and the postcode-norm line was 79.0 while the "national norm" line was 72.5. The spoken line says "one line changed"; the best letter added a particular line, fine, but say "best of 11 versions", not "one sentence", or a hostile fact-checker has it.
- **Slide 27:** "~99% of 120,000 turned around" is fine as primary-adjacent, but the caption "Counted success vs measured success" quietly accuses a government of lying while claiming it does not; the narrator must name who counted (the department) or it reads as "they all lie", which feeds the cynicism this reader already has.
- **Slide 2:** "Parliament: 760" rests on a contested "first parliament" label (research 15 itself says contested). Use "Parliament is about 700 years old" or say "since the 1200s".
- **Slide 3:** a "firmware patch for the kettle" is true of almost no kettle. Use the phone or the bank app.
- **Slide 12:** 332 flagged CHECK by the synthesis. Do not print it.
- **Slide 21/22:** Wikipedia only; no hard numbers for sewage. Grade everything or cut.
**Fix:** a hard gate: no slide leaves draft with a number lacking `source URL + grade` in spine.json; the synthesis already says so, so enforce it in the build step, not in a to-do.

### 6. Slide 22 (sewage) raises anger with nothing to aim it at
"Sewage: a top priority across parties" ends on "the one thing voters on all sides keep saying". No decision-maker is named (the water companies' owners, the regulator, the ministers who set the rules); the slide is anger with no flame direction (reader rule 6, now advisory, still a good test) and the Pillar 2 caption "shipped, never ran a test" blames nobody and is a stretch on facts.
**Fix:** either name the chooser plainly (who signed off, who regulates, who was paid) or cut it. If kept: this is the "problem with no stop condition" example and it ends on the undo button, slide 23.

### 7. Slides 11 and 8: "a party can win with policies most people don't want" tells this reader their vote is meaningless
Ostrogorski is correct, but to a reader with low institutional trust it reads as "the system is rigged, and only unbundling saves you", i.e. cynicism without direction. And slide 8's caption "Sweating over two sets of 200 promises" is the Two Buttons gag with voters as the sweating man: the butt of the joke is the voter.
**Fix:** put the butt of the joke on the bundle ("Both buttons are labelled 'Yes'"), not the sweating man. For 11, make the example concrete, tiny and local (three people in a pub, three issues). No bar charts of abstract voters.

### 8. Pacing: the middle sags, and the same joke is told three times
- Slides 3, 15, 16 are all "government updates slowly". 15 is the best; cut 3 and 16 (CD-ROM gag also dates the audience upward; a 30-year-old has never seen one).
- Pillar 2 runs 9 slides (15-23) versus 5 for the one the audience came for (Pillar 1 is 6-14, 9 slides too). Pillar 3 has four evidence slides in a row (24-27) with no laugh between.
- 36 slides at 5-6 min fits a talk, not a share. The two-minute cut is 10 slides; the 36 should be the research deck, not the product.
**Fix:** a 20-slide main deck (cold open 4, pillars 4 each, bridge 2, close 2), with the other slides as a "deeper" appendix. Never more than two evidence slides in a row without a visual laugh.

### 9. Memes as look: "no house look" is stated, then quietly set
- Slide 1 (blinking cursor on dark screen, then a daylight cut) is GPOM's look carried over; slide 5 puts "FUTURE PROOF" on a building-site hoarding; slide 34 is a "daylight construction site, empty plot labelled Yours"; synthesis mentions a "blueprint palette". That is a full visual identity (site, hoarding, plots, blueprint) chosen inside the synthesis, not by `story-style`. CLAUDE.md says the daylight rule is settled (Kai 2026-10-17) but nothing else is.
- Drake/Two Buttons/Expanding Brain/This Is Fine as the grammar of the whole deck is itself a house style, and by 2026 these templates are tired and will date the deck.
- Slide 29 as a reusable "nutrition label" and the three.js "bricks" metaphor risk becoming the BadCode explainer template across stories.
**Fix:** keep the metaphors (bricks, tests, towns) as content, and send hoarding/blueprint/cursor/etc. through `story-style` for this deck only; state in the deck's `look.md` that nothing here is a BadCode default. Cap borrowed templates at three and make the rest original formats.

### 10. Legal: "own redraw" of famous memes is not as green as the synthesis says
Research 19 itself says UK fair dealing for parody is narrow and Shazam v Only Fools [2022] rejected it. "Own redraw" of This Is Fine (KC Green's dog), Drake (a real person's likeness, the cartoon-style redraw still evokes the photo) and Distracted Boyfriend (a stock photo) are derivative works of identifiable works, not original art. They also put Drake's name and image in a political deck. "Meme grammar" (the layout) is fine; the specific character, pose or photo is not.
**Fix:** use the layouts only (two panels, two buttons, a ladder) with original characters. Drop Drake by name and by pose. Drop the This Is Fine dog; use an original character in an original burning room. Keep slides 15/18-style fake UIs, which are safe.
Also: slide 30 (Billionaire Coin, "cap hoarding at $1bn, the rest builds hospitals") sits in a deck that will be seen as financial promotion in a coin-adjacent way; CLAUDE.md says the coin's trust statement is never said as one. Cut 30 from the main deck; the claim ledger must be read first.
Also: naming Martin Lewis, Nesta, Involve and HMRC letter text on slides is factual use, but a fake government update popup (15) styled as gov.uk could be read as impersonating a real organisation: no GOV.UK crown, typeface or green/black.

### 11. Voice and register slips
- Slide 35 ("That one's from the canon. It's a straight line. I mean it.") and slide 4 ("You are the sane one") are the right care register, but "I mean it" breaks the narrator, and slide 1 has Kai say the narrator "wrote the postmortem" (the narrator wiped out humanity in canon; telling a UK reader that on slide 1 is a bleak non sequitur before the hook).
- Slide 26 (Scared Straight) targets the "common sense" belief this reader most holds (tough on crime). It runs the third-party trick correctly, but "everybody was sure it'd work" aims at the viewer's instincts. Make the "everybody" explicit (US states, 1970s officials) and the people who were sure named.
- Slide 21 poll tax is a good target but is the one slide where a viewer who hates the Tories could cheer, so keep it as "no stop condition", not partisan: say who set it up and who scrapped it.
**Fix:** Kai's opener is one line, no postmortem: "I'm Kai. Here's an idea from my day job." Narrator comes in only for the diagnosis beats; Kai plays the re-aim straight. Remove "I mean it".

### 12. The AI hook is missing where it matters for this reader
Kai's pitch says "since then we have had the internet and AI". The deck mentions AI only at slide 32 as a summariser. The reader's machine fear is being judged or defrauded by an algorithm. The deck never says who decides what AI is for in politics, and never mentions the welfare algorithm at all.
**Fix:** one slide in Pillar 3 naming the decision: "Who decided a computer should judge your benefits claim, and what test did it pass?" That is the AI slide that earns its place and aims the anger.

## Smaller catches
- Slide 17 spoken line "Left: ... Right: ..." is a description of the screen, not a joke or story: let the picture speak.
- The 9:16 short list (3, 6, 9, 15, 18, 24, 26) includes the two weakest slides for this reader (3, 24). Use 4, 6, 15, 25.
- Open question 3 (a topical small-ideas slide before 12 Nov): ship it only verified; a stale hook after the 12th is worse than none.
- `deck.json`/`packages/deck` pre-commits an architecture before the picking page; fine but note: do not build the renderer before three slides pass a cold read.

## The 5 strongest slides to keep
1. **Slide 4 (bank app vs ballot paper)** (after removing the invented "10 questions"): hero not pupil, "you are the sane one", no scapegoat.
2. **Slide 6 (till receipt, can't take the pickles off)**: a joke anyone in a queue gets in two seconds; pillar 1 in one object.
3. **Slide 10 (party slab cracks into bricks)**: the best explainer in the deck, one variable, no lecture.
4. **Slide 25 (HMRC letter, best of 11 versions)**: measurement as a small, human, almost cheerful story; names the department that ran it; "nobody changed a law".
5. **Slide 17 (green-tick brick tower with a revert arm vs a crane-dropped monolith)**: makes "small, tested, undoable" visible, and answers the "move fast and break things" trap by design. Runner-up: slide 23 (the undo button) and slide 34 (the empty plot, once look is picked by `story-style`).
