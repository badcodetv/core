# The Cantillon effect and the real numbers behind ENC

**Question:** Is the real-world economics behind "print money → asset prices rise, wages don't" defensible, what are the actual numbers, and where does an informed critic break the story?

## What I found

1. **Cantillon's original claim is about non-neutrality and sequence, not "printing helps the rich" per se.** Richard Cantillon's *Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général* (written 1730s, published posthumously 1755) broke with the then-orthodox view that money is neutral (more money → proportionate price rises, no real effects). He argued new money's effects depend entirely on *who receives it first* — early receivers spend at old prices and capture real purchasing power before prices catch up; late receivers face higher prices with no compensating income. In his 18th-century example, proximity to the court/state determined who benefited. ([FEE](https://fee.org/articles/the-cantillon-effect-because-of-inflation-we-re-financing-the-financiers/), [Medium/S. Roy summary](https://medium.com/@a.roy.research/the-cantillon-effect-who-gets-new-money-first-and-does-it-matter-a23ea122ebd9))

2. **The modern transmission mechanism is NOT one pipe — this is where the popular "money printer" story oversimplifies.** Three channels exist with different distributional footprints: (a) **QE** — the central bank buys government bonds/MBS *from financial institutions*, raising asset prices; gains concentrate among existing asset holders; (b) **bank lending** — banks create new deposits when they extend credit; distribution depends on who can borrow; (c) **fiscal transfers** — stimulus checks, UI, PPP loans go straight to households first. The 2020–21 M2 surge was QE *plus* unprecedented direct fiscal transfers (CARES Act, ARPA), not classic QE alone. Conflating "the Fed printed it" with "it went to Wall Street" is the single biggest way this satire could be attacked as sloppy. (This is a reasoned synthesis of the BoE/BIS literature below, not a single quoted source.)

3. **The Bank of England's own 2012 report is the strongest, most citable admission of Cantillon-effect-style skew.** "The Distributional Effects of Asset Purchases" (BoE Quarterly Bulletin, July 2012) found that QE-driven asset price increases boosted household financial wealth held outside pensions, but **the top 5% of households held 40% of these financial assets** — so the wealth gains were heavily concentrated among the already-wealthy. Crucially, the same report argues the counterfactual (no QE) would have been worse for *almost everyone* via higher unemployment and more business failures — it's a "most people better off, unequally so" finding, not "only the rich benefited." ([BoE paper PDF](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/2012/the-distributional-effects-of-asset-purchases.pdf), [BoE QE page](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy/quantitative-easing))

4. **The academic literature on monetary policy and inequality is genuinely split — not a consensus for the thesis.** Studies finding expansionary policy *increases* inequality: Mumtaz & Theophilopoulou (2017, UK wealth inequality), Inui et al. (2017), Andersen et al. (2023). Studies finding it *reduces* inequality via employment effects: Saiki & Frost (2014), Coibion et al. (2017), Furceri et al. (2018), Guerello (2018). Mumtaz & Theophilopoulou's own work finds both: looser monetary policy **decreases** earnings/consumption inequality (employment channel helps the bottom) while **increasing** wealth inequality (asset-price channel helps the top). ([CEPR/VoxEU summary](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/monetary-policy-and-inequality), [ScienceDirect: Mumtaz & Theophilopoulou 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0014292120302282))

5. **The COVID-era numbers are dramatic and real, and so is the reversal — which matters for ENC's burn mechanic.** US M2 grew roughly 26.9% year-over-year by February 2021 — the fastest expansion since WWII, exceeding both 1970s inflation and 2008–15 QE ([source](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2023/may/the-rise-and-fall-of-m2)). M2 then peaked near $21.72T in April 2022 and fell to roughly $20.66–20.84T by late 2023/early 2024 — a peak decline of about **$1.06 trillion, or 4.74%**, the first M2 contraction of that magnitude since the Great Depression era (only five such episodes in ~150 years: 1878, 1893, 1921, 1931–33, 2022–23). M2 has since resumed growth, reaching new all-time highs by 2026. ([Motley Fool / St. Louis Fed synthesis](https://www.fool.com/investing/2023/09/03/money-supply-great-depression-ominous-sign-stocks/))

6. **The strongest evidence AGAINST the simple thesis: real wages at the bottom grew fastest during the exact window the thesis predicts they'd be flat.** From 2019–2024, low-wage US workers saw real wage growth of about **+15.3%** — historically fast, breaking decades of stagnation (EPI, [SWA report](https://www.epi.org/publication/swa-wages-2023/)). The bottom 50% of earners were reportedly the *only* group with real gains through the 2021–2023 inflation peak, even as average real hourly earnings for the whole workforce fell year-over-year for 25 straight months (April 2021–April 2023). The driver is a tight post-COVID labor market — a labor-market story, not a monetary-non-neutrality one — and needs to be bracketed explicitly in ENC's copy.

7. **Wealth concentration is real, large, and current.** Per the Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts, as of Q4 2024 the **top 1% of US households held ~33% of household net worth**, while the **bottom 50% held ~2.5%**. ([FRED DFA release](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/release?rid=453), [CompoundLadder synthesis of Fed data](https://www.compoundladder.com/guides/55-us-net-worth-statistics-2026))

## What this means for ENC

**→ The strongest, safest claim ENC can make:** "Since 2000, US home prices have roughly tripled (Case-Shiller, 100→332.7) while the money supply that measures dollars in the system has grown far faster than real wages." That's citable, precise, and doesn't require picking a side in the QE-inequality debate.

**→ Do not say "the Fed prints money and gives it to Wall Street."** That conflates QE (buys assets from institutions), bank lending (creates deposits for borrowers), and fiscal transfers (goes to households) — three different pipes with three different distributional footprints. An economist will nail this immediately. Say "when the money supply expands, asset owners benefit disproportionately and first" instead — defensible; the mechanism-specific claim isn't.

**→ The single best-armored citation for the thesis is the BoE's own number: top 5% of UK households held 40% of the financial-asset gains from QE.** It's an official central-bank admission, not an outside critique — use it as the anchor stat in any public-facing explainer.

**→ The 2022–2023 M2 contraction is a gift for the ENC mechanic (burn on M2 decline) but also a genuine counter-example to watch:** money supply fell nearly 5% and it was the *worst* stretch for real wages (real hourly earnings fell YoY for 25 straight months), not the best — this actually supports "less money = harder for ordinary people," which cuts against a simple "printing bad, ordinary people good" framing. Flag this nuance rather than hide it.

**→ The sharpest attack an informed critic will make:** 2021–2024 is the empirical embarrassment. This is exactly the window of the fastest M2 growth in modern history, and real wages for the bottom half of earners grew *faster* than at almost any point in decades (+15.3%, EPI), driven by a tight labor market — a channel that has nothing to do with Cantillon-style asset-price transmission. If a critic points to this period, "money printing keeps wages flat" is simply false for the specific years ENC is most likely to reference. The honest response is to root the joke in the *asset-price* side of the story (assets outpacing wages **on average, over the long run, and overwhelmingly for wealth held as assets vs. wages**) rather than a strict "prints ↑ = wages flat" causal claim for any given year.

**→ The academic literature does not unanimously support the thesis.** Genuinely split (Mumtaz & Theophilopoulou 2017 vs. Saiki & Frost 2014, Coibion et al. 2017, etc.) — some serious researchers find loose monetary policy *reduces* earnings inequality via employment, even while *increasing* wealth inequality. ENC's copy should target wealth/asset inequality specifically, where the evidence is much stronger, not earnings inequality generally, where it's contested.

**→ Wealth concentration (33% top 1% / 2.5% bottom 50%, Fed DFA Q4 2024) is rock solid and unrelated to the causal debate** — it's simply descriptive fact and safe to cite standalone as "this is what asset ownership looks like today," separate from any claim about what caused it.

## Open questions

- Should ENC's public copy explicitly caveat the 2021–2024 real-wage counter-example, or stay silent and risk a "gotcha" from a critic? (Recommend: pre-empt it — a single honest footnote defuses the strongest attack.)
- Does the project want to make any *causal* claim ("printing money causes X") or stick to *correlational/structural* claims ("asset ownership beats cash-holding when the money supply grows")? The latter is far more defensible.
