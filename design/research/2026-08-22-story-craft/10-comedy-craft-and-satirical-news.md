# Comedy craft, deadpan, and the satirical news bulletin

## What this covers

The mechanics of making something funny *on purpose*: named joke-construction frameworks (Kaplan's screen-comedy tools, Dikkers' eleven funny filters, the rule of three, bathos/anticlimax), the fake-news bulletin as a satirical form (Morris, Iannucci, Yes Minister, Private Eye), tonal whiplash — grief off a laugh, or vice versa (Fleabag, BoJack Horseman, Pratchett's Death) — and the comic voice of a petty all-powerful entity (Marvin, GLaDOS, the Stanley Parable Narrator, Good Omens), plus the vertical-scroll mechanics of a reveal beat. It leaves general narrator-voice construction to sibling brief 09 — this is the joke engineering *inside* whatever voice that brief builds.

## Top findings

1. **Comedy needs a "non-hero," not a fool** — a character who "lacks some, if not all the essential tools and skills with which to win," so the *struggle* generates the laugh, not stupidity. Steve Kaplan, *The Hidden Tools of Comedy*.
2. **Comedy is watching someone watch someone do something funny.** Kaplan's "straight line/wavy line": one character creates or is blind to a problem, a second struggles and fails to cope with it — the reaction completes the circuit, per John Cleese's own account of Monty Python's discovery.
3. **"Winning" — comedy grants permission drama withholds.** A comic character may do whatever they think will work, no "shoulds" — paired with Kaplan's "Positive Action" (the character genuinely believes the plan will succeed), keeping scheming sympathetic rather than sociopathic.
4. **Start from a subtext, then run it through eleven mechanical filters.** Scott Dikkers (longtime Onion editor-in-chief), *How to Write Funny*: fix the buried opinion first, then generate candidates via Irony, Character, Shock, Hyperbole, Wordplay, Reference, Madcap, Parody, Analogy, Misplaced Focus, Metahumour.
5. **A headline is a flag, and it must resolve on one read.** The Onion's rule, via Dikkers: "confused readers rarely laugh" — a joke needing re-reading is already dead.
6. **The rule of three is "establish, reinforce, surprise" — the third beat must still connect to the pattern**, or it reads as a non sequitur. Ken Levine (veteran US sitcom writer) notes audiences now pre-empt the form.
7. **Bathos is a deliberate crash from the sublime into the mundane — Douglas Adams' signature move.** The engine is the size of the drop, not the mundane image alone: a doomed fleet of ships hangs "in the sky in much the same way that bricks don't."
8. **A petty, all-powerful narrator is funniest when the comedy is the gap between authority and control, not stupidity.** The Stanley Parable's Narrator is "formal British diction colliding with frustration" (Eurogamer's Jeffrey Matulef) — composure intact, only the plot lost.
9. **Tonal whiplash works when the comic register has earned trust first, so tragedy lands as betrayal, not confusion.** Critics on Fleabag: it "pulled you in with its irreverence and jokes... [before it] reveals that it's actually a tragedy" (Maureen Ryan, *Variety*).
10. **Institutional satire is funniest aimed at structure, not party.** Yes Minister avoided real-party politics so both sides could see themselves satirised — ministers who believe they're in charge vs. civil servants who believe they run the country.
11. **Chris Morris's fake-news satire depends on the audience never anticipating the trick — which is why it can't be safely repeated.** His reason for not reviving Brass Eye: once known, the mechanism becomes "hackwork... with no drive."
12. **"Comedy is tragedy plus time" has no single author.** Traced to Steve Allen (*Cosmopolitan*, Feb 1957), independently voiced by Carol Burnett, Lenny Bruce and Woody Allen — the operative variable is elapsed time, not detachment.

## The techniques in detail

### Kaplan's toolkit: non-hero, winning, straight line/wavy line

**What it is.** Six interlocking tools: Winning (permission to do whatever seems necessary, no "shoulds"); Non-Hero ("the more skills your character has, the less comic and the more dramatic" — withhold competence deliberately); Positive Action (the character genuinely believes the plan will work — this keeps it sympathetic, not sociopathic); Active Emotion (feelings arise from pursuing the goal, not bolted on); Metaphorical Relationship (a second relationship sits under the surface one — "business partners," "cats and dogs"); Straight Line/Wavy Line (an often-oblivious agent creates the problem, an observer struggles and fails — the reaction carries the laugh, per Cleese/Python's own discovery that comedy is "watching someone watch someone do something funny").

**Why it works.** A highly competent schemer reads as a villain; a character who lacks the tools and improvises anyway reads as sympathetic, because the audience recognises its own inadequacy, not the character's cunning.

**Failure mode.** Give the non-hero real competence, or drop "positive action" so the character knows the plan is bad and does it anyway, and the tone tips into drama or dislikeability.

### Dikkers' eleven funny filters and The Onion's headline discipline

**What it is.** Fix the subtext (the buried opinion), then pass it through eleven filters: Irony (state the opposite), Character (a comic archetype acting true to type), Shock (moderated, in service of the subtext), Hyperbole (exaggerate past reason), Wordplay, Reference (a recognisable shared observation), Madcap (slapstick/non-sequitur that still serves the point), Parody (borrow a format, swap the content), Analogy (expose a hidden likeness between unlike things), Misplaced Focus (fixate on the wrong, smaller thing), Metahumour (make humour itself the target). Companion Onion rules: "concept is king," "the shorter the better," and — load-bearing for any single-read medium — "confused readers rarely laugh."

**Why it works.** Separating subtext from filter turns "be funnier" into a search procedure: run one premise (Dikkers' own example, "we don't save as much time as we think by having AI do all our work") through several filters — Irony makes it a piece arguing AI wastes time, Hyperbole an office where nobody remembers what the company does any more — and pick the sharpest, rather than waiting on instinct. "Ruffle some feathers" ("Horatian satire isn't really remembered because it's toothless") names the matching trap: a filter applied too gently to offend anyone usually lands on no one either.

**Failure mode.** Filters run with no fixed subtext degrade into filters-for-their-own-sake — hyperbole with nothing under it is noise, wordplay with no point is just a pun.

### The rule of three and comic escalation

**What it is.** Two beats establish a pattern; a third breaks it while staying connected — "establish, reinforce, surprise." Levine: the third "must break the pattern but not so much... that it's a non sequitur — it's not that it doesn't belong, it's that you don't expect it." His "Stan Daniels turn" makes the punchline the structural opposite of the first two ("She's hateful, she's despicable — I'm in love").

**Why it works.** Two items alone are ambiguous — pattern or coincidence? The third confirms the pattern retroactively while breaking it. Levine's toy case: "We serve lasagna, spaghetti, and poi" — two Italian dishes set the pattern, the third (Hawaiian) breaks it while staying inside "food this restaurant serves."

**Failure mode.** The form is now so familiar audiences pre-empt the twist, so a lazy third beat reads as predictable rather than surprising.

### Bathos and anticlimax

**What it is.** A deliberate crash from the sublime to the mundane — Adams' recurring device: cosmic set-pieces resolved with a flat, domestic non-sequitur.

**Why it works.** The engine is the *height of the fall* — the more portentous the build, the harder the deflation lands, which is exactly why bathos pairs with an authoritative, cosmic-scale narrator: bigger claimed stakes bank more comic capital for the drop.

**Worked example.** The Vogon fleet hanging "in the sky in much the same way that bricks don't" — Earth's demolition treated as, among other things, a licensing-paperwork failure.

**Failure mode.** Without a genuine build, "bathos" is just flatness — the reader must briefly believe the sublime version for the mundane one to register as a *drop*. Overuse also flattens tone permanently: if every big moment deflates, nothing is left to fall from.

### The fake-news bulletin as satire

**What it is.** Four related crafts. **Chris Morris** (*The Day Today*, *Brass Eye*) built satire on the audience — and real interviewees — never anticipating the deception; "defining my own sense of what is possible," testing progressively how far participants could be walked into absurdity on camera. **Armando Iannucci** (*The Thick of It*, *Veep*) locates comedy in the institutional *response*, not the crisis itself: "there's something inherently comic about the fact that politicians make things worse by worrying too much about something." **Yes Minister** built comedy from structural antagonism — ministers who believe they're in charge vs. civil servants who believe they run the country — via Sir Humphrey's "long-winded technical jargon and circumlocutions," escalating into set-pieces Stephen Fry recalled audiences anticipating ("when's the big speech going to happen?"). **Private Eye** runs on nicknames and euphemism as satirical *and* legal technology at once ("tired and emotional" for drunk, a fictional proprietor "Lord Gnome"), plus deadpan captions that let a photo's context do the accusing.

**Why it works.** All four displace the joke from the target's stated position onto the mechanism around it, letting the satire land on a structure rather than one person or party. Iannucci on Selina Meyer needing to stay credible: "she is the Vice President, so you have to believe... she can't be incompetent, or fall over" — the comedy comes from the office constraining a capable person, not a fool holding it.

**Failure mode.** Morris's account of Brass Eye's non-repeatability: the trick works once per audience; repeated after it's understood, it becomes "hackwork... with no drive." The opposite failure is partisan point-scoring, deliberately avoided by Yes Minister's writers, which is why it still reads as satire decades on.

### Tonal whiplash: grief off a laugh

**What it is.** A deliberate collision of comic and tragic registers, engineered so the comedy *earns* the tragedy. **Fleabag** builds trust through direct-address jokes before critics note it "reveals that it's actually a tragedy" (Ryan); Nussbaum called it "a precision black-humour mechanism." **BoJack Horseman**'s creator Raphael Bob-Waksberg pitched "a cartoon that was funny, but also... incredibly tragic at times" — the pivot was structural enough that one outlet changed its review policy to whole-season after misjudging the comic-only first half. **Pratchett's Death** achieves whiplash through character, not plot: absolute finality that is also comically literal — greeting trick-or-treaters with "Cower, brief mortals" out of genuine confusion about the correct script.

**Why it works, and how it fails.** The comedy is the device that gets the tragedy to land, not a tonal accident beside it: trust built through jokes lowers the guard, so the tragedy arrives as betrayal-of-expectation. Deployed without first earning that trust, the same collision reads as confusion instead — the diagnosis some critics initially gave BoJack's first half before revising it on fuller context.

### The petty omnipotent being

**What it is.** Total or near-total power, written for comedy by locating the joke in a *gap*, not stupidity. **Marvin**: "a brain the size of a planet" reduced to opening doors — Adams named the direct inspiration as comedy writer Andrew Marshall ("he's exactly like that"), placing him in "a long line of literary depressives, such as A. A. Milne's Eeyore"; the comedy is his stoicism, not manic suffering. **GLaDOS**: co-writer Erik Wolpaw wrote her "passive-aggressive... and mildly sarcastic" rather than a cartoon villain, avoiding clichéd robot-speak so she reads as "a regular person" underneath — the mismatch between clinical protocol delivery and personal cruelty ("You are a horrible person") is the mechanism. **The Stanley Parable's Narrator**: composure intact, control of the story lost; design intent was to "mess with the player's head... pretending there's an answer and then kinda whisking it away." **Good Omens** inverts apocalyptic stakes by having its angel and demon prioritise comfortable earthly lives over cosmic duty.

**Why it works, and how it fails.** Power stays intact while control or composure slips, preserving authority while generating the gap; undermine the power instead and the joke becomes incompetence — weaker, different. GLaDOS's fixed clinical register with betraying content is the cleanest transferable version. Dialogue that slides into generic "sassy AI" boilerplate — the cliché Wolpaw says the writers avoided — collapses the joke into a trope, and the authority needed for pathos goes with it.

### The scroll as reveal mechanism

**What it is.** In print comics, the reveal is engineered by pacing (setup creates question/tension/direction), the page-turn (automatic suspense), and the reveal page (immediate impact) — final setup panels use "visual leading" (a gaze, a motion line) pointing toward the turn. The vertical scroll substitutes a "scroll-fold," roughly one phone-screen height, for the page-turn: a short scroll before a beat reads as a quick reaction; a long scroll is a held breath before a reveal.

**Why it works.** Distance is time. A held-breath gap that's too short kills a reveal by rushing the pause a joke needs; a beat that's too long past a quick gag lets the reader's attention drift before the punch arrives.

**Failure mode.** Treating every beat's gap as uniform — no variation in scroll distance means no variation in comic timing, which flattens every joke to the same rhythm regardless of size.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **"Tragedy plus time" has no verifiable single author.** Steve Allen is the earliest documented print source (1957), but Carol Burnett attributed it to her mother, who died in 1958 — before Allen's published version — so oral circulation may predate the record. Treat single-person attribution as folklore, not fact.
- **Monty Python is cited on both sides of the rule-of-three question.** One claim from search synthesis, not independently re-verified by fetch, holds Python deliberately over-repeated beats past three "until it would become funny, then annoying, then funny again" — flagged **(unverified)**. Treat rule-of-three as a default, not a law.
- **Grading:** Kaplan and Levine are practitioner testimony via podcast/blog, not their own book text. Dikkers' fullest primary account (his Substack) paywalled after one paragraph, so the 11-filter list is sourced from a book-review site — listicle-grade on names/definitions, though corroborated across two secondary sources. Quote Investigator is research-grade. The Wikipedia entries carry named, checkable quotes from critics and creators (Adams, Wolpaw, Ryan, Nussbaum, Fry, Matulef) — reliable for those quotes, not as primary craft essays.
- **Good Omens' granular mechanics (footnotes, deadpan narration) and Chris Morris's granular technique (audio manipulation, interview construction) were both thin in the pages reached** — the richest material gave premise/philosophy over method, so those claims stay general rather than device-level.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Give the narrator "winning" but never villainy — the gap is "it won completely and still got bored and lonely"** (the Marvin/GLaDOS gap: power intact, purpose slipping), not the non-hero mechanism. In-story humans (Bob, the laid-off worker) are Kaplan non-heroes; the narrator stays petty-omnipotent — keep the two lanes separate.
2. **Run every script's political point through subtext-then-filter before drafting**, and separately apply The Onion's one-pass headline test to every caption/title — a caption needing a re-read gets skipped, not re-read.
3. **Use bathos to undercut the narrator's cosmic certainty, and budget it**: the bigger a scene builds the stakes, the more capital is banked for a flat deflation right after — spend it every beat and drops stop registering.
4. **Adopt Yes Minister's targeting discipline: structure, never a party or the reader's own tribe** — already what `the-reader.md` rules, and proof structural satire outlives its news cycle.
5. **Treat any fake-bulletin device as one-shot per surprise, not a recurring template** — Morris's reasoning for retiring Brass Eye. Vary the deception each time rather than reusing a spoof-headline structure twice in one release.
6. **Use Private Eye-style running euphemisms as a serialisation tool** — a code phrase that compounds in value across episodes, rewarding the loyal reader without a glossary.
7. **Build tonal whiplash Fleabag/BoJack-style: earn the comic register for a real stretch before the tragedy hits** — a gut-punch with no comic credit banked first reads as confusion, not betrayal.
8. **Put the political point in the rule-of-three's surprise slot**: two escalating beats, then a third that breaks the pattern *and* lands "who benefits" (the-reader.md rule 6), where the audience is already primed to take a punch.
9. **Make scroll-gap length do the comic timing** — short before a quick reaction, long ("held breath") before a genuine reveal, with the prior panel leaning toward the scroll direction.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does every joke resolve on one pass, no re-reading needed?
2. Is there a stated subtext, and would a filter pass (irony, hyperbole, misplaced focus) sharpen it?
3. Is the comic character a non-hero, or has competence crept in and turned the scheme sinister instead of funny?
4. If there's a reaction beat, is it doing real comic work, or is the joke resting on the action alone?
5. Where the narrator jokes about its own power, is the joke in the authority-vs-control gap, not in it being dumb?
6. Does the rule-of-three's third beat break the pattern while staying connected, or read as a non sequitur?
7. Is there a genuine build before any bathos drop, or is the "mundane" line flat with nothing to fall from?
8. If institutional satire is in play, is the target the structure, or has it drifted onto a party or demographic?
9. If a fake-news device is used, has it appeared earlier in the release, and is it varied enough not to be seen coming twice?
10. Does the piece earn its tragic turn with a real run of comic trust first, or whiplash cold?
11. Does the scroll gap before a reveal match its weight — short for a punch, long for a reveal?
12. Does the beneficiary-naming beat (the-reader.md rule 6) sit in the rule-of-three's surprise slot?
