# Theme as argument; character as engine

## What this covers

How a story makes a political or economic point *through what a character does* rather than
narration — the mechanics that let theme drive plot instead of sitting on top of it as commentary.
It covers Egri's premise, McKee's controlling idea, Mazin's central dramatic argument, McDonald's
armature, Truby's moral argument and four-corner opposition, Cron's misbelief, the ghost/lie/want/
need cluster, Weiland's flat arc, Arndt's three-stakes endings, Pixar's rule that theme is
discovered not imposed, and — because BadCode's stories have no personified villain — how to build
real opposition against a systemic or impersonal antagonist. It leaves the non-human/godlike
narrator to sibling brief 17 and the science of what actually changes minds to sibling brief 08.
This is the internal architecture of a single story, not who's telling it or whether it lands.

## Top findings

1. **A premise is a causal sentence, not a topic.** Egri's premise names "the dominant trait of the
   lead character, the conflict and the ending" in one line — "poverty encourages crime," not
   "poverty." A one-word theme gives a writer nothing to build scenes from. (Egri, via Neil
   Oseman's review.)
2. **Theme has to be debatable or there's nothing to dramatise.** Mazin: a theme "could not be
   brotherhood... because there's nothing to argue about there" — it needs to be a proposition like
   "if you love someone, set them free." (Craig Mazin, Scriptnotes ep. 403.)
3. **Structure is a symptom of theme, not a container for it.** Mazin: "structure isn't something
   you write well. It's something that happens because you wrote well... it is a symptom." Plotting
   problems are usually theme problems in disguise. (Same source.)
4. **The protagonist starts as the living anti-theme.** Mazin: the story takes them "from a place of
   ignorance of the truth... all the way to the point where they become the very embodiment of that
   argument... through action." McKee's version: idea and counter-idea "argue back and forth" scene
   by scene until one wins at the climax. (Mazin, ibid.; McKee's *Story*, via Shortform.)
5. **A controlling idea is a value change plus its cause.** McKee: "a specific, truthful statement
   about the world that expresses cause and effect" — *Finding Nemo*'s is "children live fulfilling
   lives when parents allow them to take risks," with the counter-idea running underneath the whole
   film. (McKee, via Shortform.)
6. **A misbelief, not a flaw, is what plot exists to shatter.** Cron avoids "fatal flaw" (pejorative)
   and "wound" (makes the protagonist a victim); a misbelief "made them smart" once and "began to
   undermine them" since, without their noticing. (Lisa Cron, *Story Genius*, via Writers Helping
   Writers.)
7. **Want is the anti-theme in physical form; need is the theme in physical form.** Ghost (a
   wounding past event) produces the Lie, which produces the Want; the Need is the Truth that
   disproves it. "Often the want taps into the anti-thematic statement and the need taps into the
   thematic statement." Example: *A Christmas Carol* — Want "make as much money as possible," Need
   "remember that true wealth is the love of his fellow human beings." (K.M. Weiland, Helping
   Writers Become Authors.)
8. **A flat-arc protagonist enforces the theme rather than learning it.** Weiland: "the character is
   the one changing the world around him," truth "tested to the breaking point" but never
   abandoned — the shape for a character who is right from scene one. (Weiland, "Flat Character
   Arc, Pt. 1.")
9. **An ending pays off three stakes at once, not one.** Arndt: external (the physical outcome),
   internal (a relationship or self-respect), and philosophical — "a contest between two sets of
   values" (*Star Wars*: "empire and dominance versus democracy and cooperation"). An "insanely
   great" ending flips all three within seconds. (Michael Arndt, "Endings," transcript.)
10. **Name the armature as a full sentence or it does no work.** McDonald: a single word like
    "competition" "is like saying your theme is 'Red.' It really says nothing at all" — an armature
    must be a complete claim, e.g. "Competition is sometimes a necessary evil." (Brian McDonald,
    *Invisible Ink*.)
11. **Real moral complexity needs four corners, not two.** Truby: two-part opposition (good vs.
    evil) is "always simplistic"; four-corner opposition pushes a hero and at least three opponents
    to distinct value-corners so the story argues values against values. Applied to *The Hunger
    Games*: radical challenger, subtle challenger, extreme endorser, protagonist. (Truby, *The
    Anatomy of Story*, via a four-corner-opposition explainer.)
12. **An antagonist doesn't have to be a person to be real** — a wound, an institution, a deadline,
    an accumulating consequence all work, provided the abstraction still generates concrete,
    escalating scene pressure. Example: the Nazi Army in *Saving Private Ryan*, rendered as
    "faceless troops." (Helping Writers Become Authors, citing Weiland's "antagonistic force.")

## The techniques in detail

### Theme as a testable sentence (Egri, McKee, Mazin, McDonald)

**What it is.** Four practitioners, across eight decades, independently converge on the same rule:
state theme as a full causal sentence, never a subject. Egri's premise names character trait +
conflict + ending ("poverty encourages crime"). McKee's controlling idea states value + cause
("children live fulfilling lives when parents allow them to take risks"). Mazin's central dramatic
argument is a debatable proposition the protagonist embodies first in ignorance, then in proof
("if you love someone, set them free"). McDonald's armature is the same discipline applied to any
medium: a single word "is like saying your theme is 'Red.'" **Why it works.** A sentence can be
checked against every scene; a word can't. Mazin folds structure itself into this: "structure is
not a tool, it is a symptom" of the character's relationship to the argument — plotting trouble is
usually an unresolved-theme problem. **Worked example.** *Finding Nemo* serves all four writers as
the same case study: Marlin embodies the anti-theme (overprotection) from scene one; the sharks,
Dory, and the jellyfish forest attack it in turn; the climax — letting Nemo act alone against his
own terror — makes the argument physical. **Failure mode.** Egri: the premise "must be the very DNA
of the script"; a premise stapled on after the plot is written reads as a moral rather than an
argument, and a theme that's really a topic ("brotherhood," "loss") gives structure nothing to be a
symptom of.

### Truby's four-corner opposition

**What it is.** Push a hero, a main opponent, and at least two secondary characters to four
distinct corners of one moral question, each holding an incompatible value. **Why it works.**
Two-part opposition can only produce good-vs-evil, which Truby calls "always simplistic"; four
corners forces each position's internal logic to be stated well enough that the audience feels the
pull of more than one before the story resolves it. **Worked example.** In a *Hunger Games* reading:
Katniss holds the belief under test; Gale is the radical challenger (reverse it entirely); Peeta
is the subtle challenger (expose its gaps); Effie is the extreme endorser (the belief taken past
comfort). **Failure mode.** Four characters who want the same thing for the same reason but
disagree on tactics isn't four-corner opposition — the structure requires *values* in conflict, not
plans.

### The ghost/lie/want/need chain, and Cron's misbelief

**What it is.** A causal chain: a wounding past event (Ghost) produces a false, self-protective
belief (Lie, or Cron's "Misbelief"), which produces an external goal serving the Lie (Want), which
the plot strips away until the character can accept the Truth (Need). **Why it works.** It pushes
McKee's and Mazin's story-level argument down into a single psychology, so the reader experiences
theme as character change, not commentary. Cron insists the misbelief isn't shameful: it "made them
smart" once. **Worked example.** *A Christmas Carol*: Want "make as much money as possible" sits on
a Lie about what security requires; Need "remember that true wealth is the love of his fellow human
beings" is the theme, delivered as realisation. **Failure mode.** A want and a need pointing the
same direction removes the internal argument entirely; Cron's specific choice to reject "fatal
flaw" (pejorative) and "wound" (victim-making) is itself a caution about how framing changes whether
a misbelief reads as pitiable, contemptible, or simply understandable — only the third invites the
reader to recognise themselves rather than judge.

### Weiland's flat arc

**What it is.** The protagonist already holds the story's truth and does not change; instead "the
character is the one changing the world around him." They're tested near to breaking but never
abandon the truth. **Why it works.** It's the right shape whenever a story's engine is "watch what
happens when someone who already knows better meets a world that doesn't" — a narrator or crusader
role, not a learner role. **Worked example.** Katniss already believes society should run on trust
rather than fear; Maximus already believes in what Rome should be. **Failure mode.** A flat-arc
character with nothing genuinely tested reads as smug or inert; the belief still needs real
external pressure or "unchanging" curdles into "static."

### Arndt's three stakes

**What it is.** Every ending resolves an external stake (the physical outcome), an internal stake
(a bond or self-respect), and a philosophical stake (which of two value systems wins) — ideally in
the same beat. **Why it works.** A story can nail plot and still feel hollow if the values contest
was never made explicit, or state its values clearly and still feel unearned if nothing is
externally or emotionally at risk. **Worked example.** *Star Wars*: external — the rebellion
survives; internal — Luke's sense of being called to greatness; philosophical — "empire and
dominance versus democracy and cooperation." *Little Miss Sunshine*: external — win the pageant;
philosophical — "winning/status" versus "having fun/private life." **Failure mode.** Arndt says he
wrote *Little Miss Sunshine* "intuitively" and only recognised the pattern later, studying *Star
Wars* — this is a revision-stage diagnostic, not a first-draft checklist; forcing all three from a
blank page tends to produce mechanical endings.

### Pixar's discovered theme

**What it is.** Emma Coats' rule 3: "Trying for theme is important, but you won't see what the
story is actually about til you're at the end of it. Now rewrite." **Why it works.** It checks the
frameworks above: a premise imposed before the writer has discovered what characters actually do
under pressure tends to illustrate the theme rather than argue it honestly. Paired with rule 22
("what's the essence of your story? most economical telling of it?") and rule 13 ("give your
characters opinions... passive/malleable... is poison to the audience"). **Failure mode.**
Treating "discover it in rewrite" as permission never to state the argument at any stage — the rule
assumes a rewrite pass exists in which the now-discovered theme gets pushed back into every earlier
scene.

### Antagonist without a villain

**What it is.** A system, a wound, an institution, a deadline, or an accumulating consequence
functioning as the opposing force, instead of a personified villain. **Why it works.** Real
institutional antagonists behave this way in life — nobody "villains" a supply chain — so a
systemic antagonist can be more honest, provided the abstraction still generates concrete,
escalating, scene-level pressure. **Worked example.** The Nazi Army in *Saving Private Ryan*,
rendered as "faceless troops" rather than a named villain — an abstract, systemic antagonist made
dramatically concrete. **Failure mode, load-bearing for BadCode specifically:** a purely abstract
antagonist ("the system," unqualified) gives the reader nothing to watch push back in a scene. The
craft fix across these sources (Weiland's "antagonistic force," Truby's four-corner web) is the
same: **the system still needs a face making one specific choice on screen**, even when no single
person is "the villain."

## Disagreements and caveats

- **McKee's three controlling-idea types (idealistic/pessimistic/ironic) could not be verified from
  a fetched source.** An early search summary mentioned them; the page actually fetched did not.
  Treat the taxonomy as (unverified); the value+cause definition and *Finding Nemo* example are
  confirmed.
- **Cron's and Weiland's vocabulary is adjacent, not synonymous.** Cron rejects "wound" as
  victim-making; Weiland uses "Ghost"/"wound" without objection. Don't flatten "misbelief" and
  "ghost" together in craft notes — Weiland's Ghost is the causal backstory event, Cron's Misbelief
  sits closer to Weiland's Lie.
- **Everything here is practitioner craft, not peer-reviewed narratology.** Egri (1946) and McKee
  (1997) are the closest to canonical texts and widely cited, but are personal theories of craft;
  Mazin, Truby, Cron, Weiland, Arndt and McDonald are all working writers/consultants teaching from
  experience. Several sources here (Shortform, Helping Writers Become Authors, Medium/Substack
  posts) are third-party summaries of the primary books rather than the books themselves — flagged
  per-entry below.
- **Truby and McKee disagree on how many sides an argument needs.** McKee's idea/counter-idea is
  fundamentally two-sided; Truby argues two sides is "always simplistic." Not fully reconcilable as
  stated — McKee describes the idea-level contest (often genuinely binary), Truby the cast needed
  to dramatise it without two mouthpieces debating. A two-sided idea can still want a four-cornered
  cast.
- **The Pixar list is a widely circulated document, not studio-published doctrine.** Coats has
  reportedly said (per search summary, not independently fetched) she wishes she'd called them
  "guidelines." The exact wording was stable across the sources checked; its status as official
  Pixar policy is not.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Write the controlling idea as one causal sentence before scripting anything** — not
   "automation," but "automation displaces workers when the gains are hoarded" (anti-theme:
   "automation is just progress and the losses are nobody's fault"). Check every scene against it.
2. **Give the counter-idea real screen time — don't strawman the drift-right reader.**
   `the-reader.md` already says their diagnosis is right and only the culprit list is contested;
   dramatise the counter-idea as genuinely persuasive for most of the runtime, the way Marlin's
   fear is justified by what the ocean actually does, before the controlling idea wins.
3. **Give the AI narrator a flat arc, not a change arc.** It already has the truth; its job each
   episode is to test that truth against a fresh human situation, not to learn anything itself.
4. **Build the antagonist as "the mistake," concretised, not "the system" unqualified.**
   the-reader.md's rule 5 ("name the decision, not the technology") is the same fix as Weiland's
   antagonistic-force concept: give the systemic antagonist one on-screen face making one specific
   choice, even when no single person is the villain.
5. **Reach for four-corner opposition against "worker good, boss bad."** Push a displaced worker
   (protagonist), the executive who chose the algorithm (main opponent), a colleague who took the
   severance gratefully (extreme endorser of "just adapt"), and a union organiser demanding total
   reversal (radical challenger) into four true corners of one automation argument.
6. **Check every episode's final beat against Arndt's three stakes** in the same closing image:
   what changes in the world, what changes for the character followed, and which value system won.
7. **Keep the misbelief sympathetic, per Cron's word choice.** Never "duped," never a wound-as-
   victim — a character who blames immigration should have a misbelief that "made them smart" once.
   This is the same ban `the-reader.md` rule 2 states, arrived at from craft theory instead of
   audience research.
8. **Write the armature once per story and hand it to both `suno-prompt` and `make-comic`** so
   lyrics and captions argue the same sentence, per McDonald's insistence that the armature — not a
   one-word theme — must be legible in every scene.
9. **Draft intuitively, then run the sentence test in revision**, per Pixar's rule 3 and Arndt's own
   account of writing *Little Miss Sunshine* before recognising its pattern — don't force the
   controlling-idea sentence before a single beat exists.
10. **Diagnose stuck plotting as a theme problem first**, per Mazin's "structure is a symptom":
    when a beat sheet feels inert, ask what the character's relationship to the argument is right
    now, before asking what scene is missing.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Can the theme be stated as one causal sentence, not a word or topic?
2. Does a genuine counter-idea get persuasive screen time before the controlling idea wins?
3. Does the protagonist begin embodying the anti-theme and end embodying the theme, through action?
4. Is the Want (external, serves the Lie) distinct from the Need (internal, serves the Truth)?
5. If the arc is flat, is the held truth tested to near-breaking, or does it coast unchallenged?
6. Where the antagonist is a system, is there one on-screen face making one specific named choice?
7. Do secondary opposing characters hold genuinely different values, not just different tactics?
8. Does the ending resolve external, internal and philosophical stakes, ideally in one beat?
9. Is any character's misbelief framed as once-protective rather than stupid or villainous?
10. Would removing the theme sentence leave any scene's presence unexplained?
11. Is the armature a sentence events could in principle disprove, or is it too vague to fail?
12. Does the piece ever state the theme directly instead of letting the climax's action be the argument?
13. Does this episode's controlling idea actually plug into the master arc's, or is it swappable?
