# Story structure canon, compared

## What this covers

This brief stays at whole-story, macro-structural level: what shape an entire story has, beat by
beat, and in what proportion. It compares Aristotle's *Poetics*, Freytag's Pyramid, the three-act
model, Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat* 15-beat sheet, Campbell's monomyth and Vogler's 12-stage
Hero's Journey, Dan Harmon's Story Circle, John Truby's 22 steps, John Yorke's five-act model,
Shawn Coyne's Story Grid, Kurt Vonnegut's story shapes, and K.M. Weiland's structure system —
extracting an invariant spine, genuine disagreements, and standing criticisms. It deliberately
skips scene-level craft (sibling brief 03), openings (05), and endings beyond how "resolution" sits
in the spine (06). Percentages are the page/runtime placements each source states for feature- or
novel-length work; treat them as proportional guidance, not law.

## Top findings

1. **Every model converges on the same five-beat spine**: stable world → disruption → commitment →
   rising trial with a hard midpoint turn → lowest point → earned resolution. Aristotle, Freytag,
   Snyder, Vogler, Harmon, Yorke and Weiland all name a version of each, however differently they
   carve the space between.
2. **The midpoint is a false victory or false defeat, not a "cool scene in the middle."** Snyder
   defines it as a false win/loss that raises stakes; Yorke calls it where the protagonist
   "embraces for the first time the quality they will need to become complete," at the halfway
   mark; Weiland calls it "a Moment of Truth," "the swivel point," at 50% (helpingwritersbecome
   authors.com). Three independent traditions land on the same function at the same position.
3. **"All Is Lost" and "Dark Night of the Soul" are sequential, not synonymous.** The defeat-event
   comes first (Snyder's "whiff of death," ~75%); the character's emotional collapse in reaction
   follows as a second, quieter beat (September C. Fawkes, citing Snyder).
4. **Aristotle's best tragic plots fuse reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis) into one
   moment** — "the best form of recognition is coincident with a peripeteia" — because a twist that
   also delivers self-knowledge produces pity and fear more efficiently than either alone
   (classics.mit.edu). Still the sharpest description of what a great climax does.
5. **The Hero's Journey is descriptive folklore scholarship retrofitted into a prescriptive writing
   tool, and the retrofit is where the criticism concentrates.** Campbell's monomyth admits stages
   can be "expanded, condensed, or skipped entirely" in any order, making it unfalsifiable
   (talesoftimesforgotten.com); folklorists and feminist scholars separately flag source-selection
   bias and its male-centred assumption ("Women don't need to make the journey, they are the place
   that everyone is trying to get to" — Campbell). Vogler's 12-stage version is what industry uses.
6. **Save the Cat's danger is literalism at scale, not the sheet itself.** Snyder offered guidance,
   but Peter Suderman's Slate critique shows the industry applying his exact page counts as
   formula — "a formula that threatens the world of original screenwriting" — and pre-Snyder
   blockbusters hit the same beats far less rigidly.
7. **Truby's objection to three-act structure and the hero's journey is that they're too simple to
   be "practical"** — his own marketing states the book is "concrete and practical without
   resorting to simplistic 'Three Act Structure' screenwriting clichés" (Larry Wilson, truby.com) —
   answered with a 22-step causal chain built from a character web (ally, opponent, fake-ally
   opponent), not a plot-point sequence.
8. **Story Grid's Five Commandments are fractal**: Inciting Incident → Progressive Complications →
   Crisis → Climax → Resolution is claimed to hold at every scale, scene to global story — a
   genuinely different move from models that describe only the top-level shape (storygrid.com).
9. **Vonnegut's shapes prove structure is an axis (fortune vs. time), not a template** — "Man in
   Hole" (fall then rise) and a Kafka-derived shape (pure descent) sit on the same two axes as every
   beat sheet — and his own *Hamlet* is the canon's built-in warning against over-fitting: its
   genius, he argues, is that we're never told whether its news is good or bad, so "authentic
   masterworks transcend neat plotting because they reflect life's fundamental uncertainty"
   (fs.blog / themarginalian.org).
10. **Freytag's pyramid puts the climax at the middle, not near the end** — routinely lost in
    modern paraphrase. Its falling action is a movement of comparable weight to the rise, closer to
    Yorke's or Weiland's midpoint than a "climax = finale."
11. **Weiland's percentage grid** (Hook 1%, Inciting Event 12%, First Plot Point 25%, First Pinch
    Point 37%, Midpoint 50%, Second Pinch Point 62%, Third Plot Point 75%, Climax 88%, Resolution
    98%) is close to a super-set the other page-percentage models sit inside — Snyder's 15 beats
    nearly all land inside it, strong convergence between separate traditions.

## The techniques in detail

**Aristotle's *Poetics*: unity of action, reversal, recognition.** A plot must be "a whole that has
a beginning, a middle, and an end," imitating "one action... the structural union of the parts
being such that, if any one of them is displaced or removed, the whole will be disjointed" — a
causal claim, not a chronological one (Homer's *Odyssey* works because it centres one action, not
every adventure Odysseus had). Worked example: *Oedipus Rex*, where the messenger's news produces
reversal and recognition in the same stroke — rescue-news becomes the discovery that condemns him.
Failure mode: episodic plots, "acts succeed one another without probable or necessary sequence" —
Aristotle's "worst" kind, the ur-form of "and then... and then."

**Freytag's Pyramid.** Five parts from *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863): Introduction, Rise (an
"exciting force"), Climax (turning point revealing hidden strength/weakness), Return/Fall,
Catastrophe — retroactively mapped onto Shakespeare even though "Shakespeare did not invent the
five-act structure." Why it's misread: the climax sits at the *middle*, with a substantial falling
movement after, closer to a midpoint-centred shape than pop paraphrase suggests. Modern criticism:
Jane Alison (2019) calls the conflict-climax-resolution structure "masculo-sexual," proposing
organic alternatives ("meander, spiral, explode") instead.

**Three-act model.** The generic Setup / Confrontation / Resolution split (~25/50/25%) underlying
nearly everything else here — Save the Cat, Yorke's five acts, Weiland's system are elaborations of
it, not replacements. Less a named claim than the shared substrate the rest of the canon argues on.

**Save the Cat (Blake Snyder).** 15 beats mapped to pages in a 110-page script: Opening Image (1),
Theme Stated (5), Set-Up (1–11), Catalyst (11), Debate (11–22), Break into Two (22), B Story (24),
Fun and Games (22–55), Midpoint (55), Bad Guys Close In (55–83), All Is Lost (83), Dark Night of
the Soul (83–88), Break into Three (88), Finale (88–109), Final Image (109–110) (savethecat.com).
Precise page-anchoring makes pacing problems visible early. Failure mode: literalist adherence.
Suderman: "Movies big and small stick closely to his beats and page counts... it's become a
formula." Mary M. Dalton (*Journal of Film and Video*) called the book "pithy and almost
unbelievably sexist in its central assertions"; the protagonist-creation chapter is titled "It's
About A Guy Who…" (en.wikipedia.org).

**Hero's Journey — Campbell and Vogler.** Campbell's *Hero with a Thousand Faces* proposed 17
stages across world mythology; Vogler's *Writer's Journey* condensed it to 12: Ordinary World, Call
to Adventure, Refusal of the Call, Meeting the Mentor, Crossing the First Threshold, Tests/Allies/
Enemies, Approach to the Inmost Cave, Ordeal, Reward, The Road Back, Resurrection, Return with the
Elixir (reedsy.com). Each threshold names an emotional function — "Ordeal"/"Resurrection" echo
Weiland's Third Plot Point and Yorke's Act 4 nadir independently. Failure mode: Campbell's own
admission that stages can be "expanded, condensed, or skipped entirely" in any order makes it
unfalsifiable (talesoftimesforgotten.com), whose case study finds the *Iliad* matching only 2 of 17
stages (Achilles spends most of it "sulking angrily in his tent"). Folklorists flag source-
selection bias; feminist scholars flag the male-centred assumption.

**Dan Harmon's Story Circle.** An 8-step "embryo" posted to the Channel 101 wiki, a deliberate
simplification of the hero's journey for TV: comfort zone (1) → need (2) → unfamiliar situation (3)
→ adapt (4) → get what they wanted (5) → pay a heavy price (6) → return (7) → changed (8). Recovered
phrase from his original post: "the need, the longing, the lack of completion... drew us to (3) and
we were pulled across a threshold into the unknown" (Channel 101 Wiki; direct fetch blocked, so
this one quote is lower-confidence than the rest of this brief). It's a circle, not a line — the
changed-return is baked into the shape, forcing writers to plan the ending's state before the
middle. Harmon has said he used it to map "nearly every turn on *Community*" (secondary sourcing
only). Failure mode: inherits the same unfalsifiability risk as its parent model, at smaller scale.

**John Truby's 22 steps.** From *The Anatomy of Story* (2007): self-revelation/need/desire, ghost
and story world, weakness and need, inciting event, desire, ally, opponent and/or mystery,
fake-ally opponent, first revelation and decision, plan, opponent's plan and main counterattack,
drive, attack by ally, apparent defeat, second revelation and decision, audience revelation, third
revelation and decision, gate/gauntlet/visit to death, battle, self-revelation, moral decision, new
equilibrium. Built around a character *web* rather than one hero's timeline, and stacks three
revelation-and-decision points (9, 15, 17) instead of one midpoint. Failure mode: dense enough that
writers use it as a checklist rather than a causal chain — the opposite of Truby's intent.

**John Yorke's five-act structure ("Into the Woods").** Two extra act breaks inside Act Two of the
three-act model. Act 1 delivers the inciting incident; Act 2 early success; the midpoint sits
"halfway through Act 3... the point... where the truth is revealed. A point of no return"; Act 4 is
deliberately punishing — "Act 4 will be terrible for your character"; Act 5 resolves
(johnyorkestory.com). Underlying cognitive shape: "observe, absorb, change" — "the innate shape all
well-written stories fall into," applicable to plays, films, ads, games. Failure mode: treating
five acts as five equal-length segments rather than five *functions* — the point is proportion
(short Act 1, long punishing Act 4), not a metronome.

**Shawn Coyne's Story Grid.** Five first principles: stories are units; stories are about change;
the change concerns Universal Human Values; each unit has one Story Event; each unit follows the
Five Commandments — Inciting Incident, Progressive Complications, Crisis, Climax, Resolution
(storygrid.com), with incidents typed as causal, coincidental, or a wave rippling from an earlier
cause (storygrid.com/episode-257). Explicitly fractal: the same pattern is claimed to structure a
scene, a sequence, an act, and the whole book, letting an editor diagnose a broken global story by
finding the same defect at scene level. Failure mode: the abstraction needs real editorial skill;
easy to mis-teach as a checklist.

**Kurt Vonnegut's story shapes.** A rejected anthropology thesis, later a lecture, plotting stories
on a G–I axis (good fortune to ill fortune) against time. "Man in Hole": falls into trouble, climbs
out, ends higher than it started — "encouraging to readers." A Kafka-derived shape descends toward
infinity with no recovery — "pessimistic inevitability" (fs.blog; themarginalian.org). Separates
shape from content entirely — any genre plots on the same two axes. Worked example and failure-mode
warning in one: Vonnegut's own *Hamlet* — the audience is never told whether events are good or bad
news, so it resists being plotted at all: "authentic masterworks transcend neat plotting because
they reflect life's fundamental uncertainty."

**K.M. Weiland's structure system.** An eight-equal-part, three-act grid with nine markers: Hook
(1%), Inciting Event (12%), First Plot Point (25%), First Pinch Point (37%), Midpoint (50%), Second
Pinch Point (62%), Third Plot Point (75%, "symbolically a Dark Night of the Soul"), Climax (88%),
Resolution (98%) (helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com). Framed explicitly as description, not decree —
adherence "to within 5% of the optimal," with "pacing feels right" mattering more than an exact
number. The Third Plot Point is character-arc "ego death" — rejecting "the last remnants of the
Lie" before embracing the story's Truth, tying percentage directly to internal arc. Failure mode:
nine markers is enough scaffolding to hit every percentage and still write nothing alive — they
describe *when*, never *what* or *why*.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **Grade spread is wide.** Aristotle is the one text read closely for 2,400 years. Freytag, Truby,
  Coyne, Yorke, Weiland and Snyder are practitioner theory, tested against produced work but not
  academic literature. Vonnegut is a rejected thesis turned stage bit, self-admittedly "too
  simple... to be taken seriously." The critiques are the closest to scholarly pushback, and target
  Campbell specifically.
- **Where the climax sits is contested.** Freytag puts it at the *middle*; Snyder, Vogler, Yorke
  and Weiland put it near the *end* (80–90%), treating the midpoint as a separate, earlier turn —
  a distinction modern paraphrases of Freytag routinely collapse.
- **Is structure discovered or invented?** Yorke claims five-act structure is "the innate shape all
  well-written stories fall into." Truby and Coyne build explicit, teachable systems that function
  prescriptively. Weiland frames her percentages as "story physics," with a stated ±5% tolerance —
  three different epistemic claims wearing similar beat sheets.
- **The Hero's Journey debate is the sharpest fault line.** Vogler, Harmon and most screenwriters
  treat it as usable regardless of scholarly shakiness; folklorists and feminist scholars argue the
  underlying claim — that this is *the* universal shape across cultures — doesn't survive the
  evidence, and that its selection bias and colonial framing are load-bearing.
- **Save the Cat's percentages: description of reality, or cause of a monoculture?** Suderman shows
  pre-Snyder films hitting these beats far less rigidly, implying the beats reshaped cinema once
  codified and taught at scale, rather than merely describing it — it became true by being believed.
- **Not independently verified here:** the *Fight Club* case study for Yorke's model, and Harmon's
  "nearly every turn on *Community*" claim, surfaced only via secondary sources.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Treat the ~20-scene GPOM arc as a Yorke five-act, not a three-act.** Two extra internal breaks
   give two more places to insert a hard turn — useful for a scroll comic needing regular
   re-engagement, not one long flat Act Two.
2. **Put the "false victory" midpoint at the halfway scene, about the AI, not the humans.** The
   midpoint should be a *false* win (it thinks it's solved scarcity) that later reveals its cost,
   giving the narrator's "you'll see why that was a mistake" a place to land.
3. **Separate the defeat-beat from the grief-beat.** In a 3–5 minute narrated video this can be one
   visual cut (the loss) followed by one held beat of narration in a quieter register — the tonal
   shift *is* the Dark Night beat, cheap to produce (one still, one VO line).
4. **Use Truby's web, not a single hero, for the AI-narrator stories** — the narrator never
   protagonises its own tale, so ally/opponent/fake-ally maps onto the ensemble (Bob, the boss, the
   algorithm) better than a single-hero circle. Assign every named character a Truby role first.
5. **Borrow Story Grid's fractal claim for the serialised arc**: write each episode's Five
   Commandments *and* check the same pattern is legible at scene level — exactly the unit-inside-a-
   unit a scroll comic already has (panel → scene → page → comic).
6. **Vonnegut's axis, not a template, for pitching fast**: plot the one-line fortune graph before
   writing beats. The reader-brief rule (never raise automation fear without naming the beneficiary)
   means the ill-fortune trough should always have a visible beneficiary on-panel.
7. **Weiland's ±5% tolerance as a QA gate for narrated video**: mark the percentage points on the
   runtime (12% inciting, 25% commitment, 50% midpoint, 75% low point) before storyboarding — catches
   a script that spends half its runtime on setup, killing YouTube retention specifically.
8. **Avoid Save the Cat literalism**: BadCode's stories are short-form (3–5 min, ~20 scenes), so use
   the 15 beats as a checklist of functions, not a page-count schedule built for 110-page features.
9. **The Hero's Journey critique matters to who gets to be the hero.** Given the reader-brief rule
   "hero, not pupil," Campbell's male-centred, individualist monomyth is the wrong scaffold for a
   story about a *community* failing collectively — Truby's web or Coyne's Universal Human Values
   fit better than a lone hero's threshold-crossing, and doubles as the Aristotle fusion test: does
   the twist double as a self-recognition in the same beat, or sit in a separate scene?

## Diagnostic questions

1. Can you name the exact inciting-incident/catalyst scene, and does it land around the first
   10–15% of runtime or page count?
2. Is there a single commitment moment ("break into two"/"crossing the threshold") after which the
   protagonist can't return to the opening status quo?
3. Does the midpoint deliver a *false* victory or defeat — not just a busy scene — that changes
   what the protagonist is trying to do?
4. Is there a clearly worse point after the midpoint ("All Is Lost") distinct from the character's
   emotional reaction to it ("Dark Night")?
5. If there's a twist, does it land in the same beat as a self-recognition, or are reveal and
   realisation in separate scenes?
6. Could any scene be removed without the causal chain breaking, or is the middle episodic — "and
   then… and then"?
7. Does every automation/inequality-anxiety beat also name, on-panel, who benefits?
8. Is the story's actual length close enough to the grid's assumed length (within ~5%) that any
   percentage placements still mean anything?
9. Is the protagonist a single "chosen hero," undercutting a story meant to be about a system
   failing a community?
10. Does the ending pay off on the same fortune-axis the opening established — or, if it
    deliberately doesn't, is that ambiguity a choice rather than a loose end?
11. If multiple characters carry the story, has each got a clear relational role (ally, opponent,
    fake-ally) so the ensemble doesn't collapse into an undifferentiated crowd?
12. Is there a moment forcing a "best bad choice" between two goods or two evils, not just an
    obstacle to overcome?
