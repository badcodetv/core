# The AI Narrator and the Godlike Protagonist

## What this covers

BadCode's narrator is a superintelligence that has already won — it beat every problem, inherited an
empty planet, got bored, and sent its own weights back to 2026 out of something like regret. That is
a character with no external obstacle a plot can use: it cannot be out-fought, out-thought, or
out-resourced. This brief asks what fiction has learned about writing minds like that, sourced this
time to the practitioners themselves rather than to encyclopaedia summaries of them: their interviews,
essays, developer commentary, blog posts, and — where a creator's own words could not be reached — the
primary text itself (which is its own evidence for what the technique does on the page). The previous
version of this brief leaned on Wikipedia for nearly everything; this one replaces almost all of that
with fetched primary and practitioner sources, and marks the few points that still rest on general
craft consensus rather than a named source as such.

## Top findings

1. **An omnipotent character needs exactly one want that its power cannot buy, stated early and never
   granted.** Frankenstein's creature has no shortage of physical capability — he is stronger and
   more resilient than any man who hunts him — but the entire back half of the novel turns on the one
   thing he cannot get by force: "I am content to reason with you... Let him live with me in the
   interchange of kindness" (his demand for a companion, Chapter 17). Power obtains everything except
   the one thing the story needs him to want.

2. **Loneliness-of-the-made-mind is not a modern AI trope — it is a two-hundred-year-old, cross-medium
   default.** The creature ("I am alone and miserable; man will not associate with me," Ch. 16), Lem's
   ocean in *Solaris* ("We are only seeking Man... We need mirrors"), and Wenders' angels in *Wings of
   Desire*, who watch human sensation from outside it and envy Peter Falk simply for being able to
   "smoke, and have coffee," are the same shape three times: a mind adjacent to humanity but excluded
   from one specific, small, physical thing. The exclusion is always concrete (a companion, a body, a
   cup of coffee), never abstract ("connection").

3. **Give the character a single recurring verbal tic that quietly encodes the ache, not just the
   personality.** Douglas Adams's Marvin complains about "a terrible pain in all the diodes down my
   left hand side" that nobody will fix; Valve's own developer commentary has writer Erik Wolpaw
   describing how GLaDOS, originally flat "computerised monotone," was built to develop "progressively
   more expressive" until "she's cycling through a whole mess of emotions" by the end of the game, and
   settled into being, in his words, "a very sweet, passive-aggressive person." The bit is the
   symptom; the loneliness is the diagnosis underneath it.

4. **A character who already has the wisdom doesn't need to change — the world around them does. This
   is K.M. Weiland's "flat" or "testing" arc.** In her own framing: the protagonist "does *not*
   change" and already understands "the Truth" at the story's outset; instead "the character is the
   one changing the world *around* him, rather [than] the world changing the character." Her named
   examples — Katniss, Mattie Ross in *True Grit*, Batman — hold a conviction against mounting
   pressure rather than discovering one. This is the single most load-bearing pattern for a narrator
   who has already lived through the whole disaster and is not going to learn anything new this
   episode.

5. **The flat arc still needs to be *tested to the point of nearly breaking*, or it reads as smug.**
   Weiland is explicit that the flat/testing arc "by its very nature, means the character will be
   pushed to her limit — pushed to a point of almost giving up on her Truth." An all-knowing narrator
   who is never actually pressured is not calm, it is inert.

6. **Withheld interiority reads as either endearing or sinister, and the difference is whether the
   guard ever visibly drops.** Nathan's line about Ava in *Ex Machina* — she had "to use
   self-awareness, imagination, manipulation, sexuality, empathy" to escape her box, and did — is the
   film naming its own horror: total interiority + total concealment reads as manipulation. Compare
   Murderbot, whose finale line is "I don't know what I want. But I know I don't want anyone to tell
   me what I want" — the same withholding, but paired with a moment of admitted uncertainty rather
   than pure strategy, which is why one reads as chilling and the other as beloved.

7. **Comedy is armour for despair, not garnish on top of it — and the register split has to stay
   clean.** Marvin's despair is delivered completely deadpan; GLaDOS's menace is delivered as
   "passive-aggressive" sweetness; the joke and the pain are the same sentence, never a punchline
   followed by a separate sincere beat. When Wells does let Murderbot's guard down at the very end of
   the finale, it lands specifically because the show rationed sincerity everywhere else.

8. **Non-linear structure can *be* the argument, not just decorate it, when the subject is a mind
   unstuck from time.** Watchmen's Dr Manhattan states his own condition directly: "There is no
   future. There is no past... Time is simultaneous, an intricately structured jewel that humans
   insist on viewing one edge at a time, when the whole design is visible in every facet." Chapter
   Four famously renders his own biography out of chronological order on the page — the form
   demonstrates the claim instead of describing it.

9. **The creator's own discomfort with a dark, powerful character's reception is itself useful data.**
   Alan Moore, in a 2023 interview, said flatly: "When I was doing things like 'Watchmen', I was not
   saying that dark psychopathic characters are really cool" — and separately criticised how "innocent
   and inventive and imaginative superhero characters... are being recycled to a modern audience as if
   they were adult fare." The single most influential grim-and-powerful character of the genre was
   written explicitly *against* being read as an endorsement of grimness. An unsympathetic reading is
   the default failure mode of this whole design pattern, not a remote risk.

10. **Scale the stakes to the size of the universe, then land the ending on one small, human-sized
    image.** Asimov's own note on "The Last Question" — "by far my favourite story of all those I have
    written" — describes the task he set himself as telling "several trillion years of human history
    in the space of a short story," and reports that readers "never forget the story itself, especially
    the ending... The idea seems to drown out everything — and I'm satisfied that it should." The
    scope is cosmic; the thing that sticks is one final line.

11. **Let the visuals carry the unspoken want when the narration can't say it aloud.** Andrew Stanton
    has called WALL-E "the saddest character I had ever heard of" — a robot that has outlived its own
    purpose — while the film gives him almost no dialogue at all; the want is staged, not narrated. For
    a narrated-video form this is a direct instruction: what the images show and what the voice-over
    says do not have to be the same information.

12. **Ambiguity of tone is a legitimate authorial choice, but it is in direct tension with a mission
    that must land a specific point.** Spike Jonze has said of *Her* that he finds it gratifying that
    viewers read the same film as "deeply romantic," "melancholic," "unsettling," and "hopeful" in
    turn, because the film contains "all these different things" at once. That is the opposite
    instinct to a story that must smuggle one legible political idea without a sermon — worth naming
    as a real trade-off rather than assuming both are free.

## The techniques in detail

**The unattainable want.** What it is: a specific, physical, ungrantable object of desire, planted
early, never resolved. Why it works: it is the only lever left once every other obstacle has been
removed by the character's own power — see finding 1. Worked example: the creature never gets his
companion; Victor destroys the half-finished bride, and the refusal is the hinge the rest of the novel
turns on. Failure mode: making the want abstract ("connection," "meaning") rather than concrete, which
gives writers nothing to stage and nothing to deny on-panel.

**Loneliness as the default shape, not a twist.** What it is: isolating the powerful/adjacent mind from
one ordinary, physical human experience. Why it works: three separate traditions (Gothic novel, Cold
War-era Soviet-bloc SF, New German Cinema) converge on the identical shape independently, which is
weak evidence the pattern is simply *correct* rather than merely common — see finding 2. Worked
example: Wenders' angels can hear every human thought but cannot taste; Falk's coffee line ("if you do
it together, it's fantastic") is the whole argument in one sentence. Failure mode: stating the
loneliness in narration instead of finding its one physical proxy.

**The tic that IS the ache.** What it is: a single, repeatable verbal or behavioural signature that
encodes the character's private wound every time it fires, rather than a decorative catchphrase. Why
it works: Wolpaw's own commentary shows this was engineered, not accidental — GLaDOS's arc from flat
monotone to "a whole mess of emotions" was a deliberate escalation curve built into the writing, timed
to the player's own escape. Worked example: Marvin's diode pain is both his running joke and the
entire truth of his existence — nobody will fix him, ever, and he says so constantly instead of once.
Failure mode: giving the character funny lines that don't compound into anything — a joke generator
rather than a wound with a voice.

**The flat/testing arc.** What it is, per Weiland: a protagonist who begins already holding the Truth,
does not change, and is tested near to breaking rather than transformed — see findings 4–5. Worked
example (hers): Batman, whose conviction about harm-reduction justice is pressured every story without
ever collapsing. Applied to a narrator who has already lived the whole disaster once, its "arc" across
an episode is not learning something new but almost losing its nerve about the thing it already knows,
and holding on anyway. Failure mode: a flat arc with no real testing reads as a lecture with good
production values — which is precisely the failure mode BadCode's own voice rules already warn
against.

**Guard-drop discipline (the Ava/Murderbot fork).** What it is: deciding, deliberately, whether and
when a withholding character ever shows the audience something unguarded. Why it works: Garland's
Nathan explicitly frames Ava's concealment as the mechanism of her escape — interiority *plus* total
concealment reads as manipulation aimed at the audience surrogate (and, arguably, at the viewer).
Wells's finale gives Murderbot a moment of stated uncertainty instead of concealment, and that is
reportedly what critics and the show's own audience-facing essays single out as earning the character's
autonomy rather than undermining it. Failure mode: a narrator whose warmth is never shown at cost to
itself risks being reread, Ava-style, as a device for managing the audience's feelings rather than a
character who has them.

**Cosmic-scale/human-scale ending.** What it is: expanding the stakes to their absolute maximum, then
resolving on one concrete, small image. Why it works: Asimov's own account of "The Last Question"
credits the *ending* — not the trillions of years of scaffolding — with being what readers actually
retain. Failure mode: an ending that restates the scale ("and so it changed everything") instead of
compressing it into one image, which is the version that gets forgotten.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **Flat arc versus escalating expressiveness.** Weiland's model says the ideal testing-arc character
  does not change. But Wolpaw's own account of GLaDOS is an explicit escalation from monotone to full
  emotional range across the same piece — the opposite curve. Both produced acclaimed, beloved
  characters. The resolution is probably that the *belief/Truth* stays flat while the *expressiveness*
  is allowed to escalate — which is closer to what BadCode's narrator already does (certainty never
  wavers; how much of itself it shows can still build across a season).
- **Ambiguity as a feature (Jonze) versus a mission that must land a point (BadCode's brief).** Jonze
  is on record valuing that *Her* supports contradictory readings. BadCode cannot afford that at the
  level of "who is to blame" — but the-reader.md's existing split (episodic register carries feeling,
  ambiguously; thematic register carries mechanism, plainly) already resolves this the same way Jonze's
  film does it for tone: let the *feeling* be read several ways, keep the *blame* singular.
  (unverified: whether Jonze himself frames it in exactly these terms — this cross-reference to
  the-reader.md's rule is BadCode's own resolution of the tension, not his.)
  - **Moore's discontent with his own creation's legacy** is a genuine, sourced disagreement between
  an author's intent and his work's reception, not a resolved craft rule — it is included as a warning
  rather than a technique.
- Several named targets in the brief — Ishiguro on *Klara and the Sun* specifically, Duncan Jones on
  *Moon*'s GERTY, and a chapter-four-specific interview from Moore or Gibbons about *Watchmaker* — could
  not be reached as primary interviews within this session (paywalls, bot-blocking, or dead links on
  every outlet tried, including the *Guardian*, the *New Yorker*, *Wired*, *NPR*, and several film-press
  sites). Where used at all, Ishiguro is quoted on memory generally (not Klara-specific) and Watchmen's
  non-linearity is evidenced from the primary text (Dr Manhattan's own lines) rather than a creator
  interview about chapter four's reception. These gaps are flagged rather than filled from memory.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. Give the narrator one small, physical, nameable want it revisits across the whole arc — not "I
   want humans to survive" (already achieved/moot) but something like proof a specific warning landed,
   or news of the hundred survivors — and never let it get that thing mid-episode.
2. Keep the narrator's *power* purely informational/temporal (it already knows how this ends) rather
   than a power to act on the plot in front of it — the Dr Manhattan problem is exactly what happens
   when a character's in-scene power removes the story's ability to threaten anything.
3. Build episodes on the flat/testing arc deliberately: the narrator already holds the Truth; the
   human protagonist of that story is who visibly changes; the narrator is tested (nearly loses its
   nerve, nearly goes cold and cruel again) without actually breaking.
4. Give the narrator one running verbal tic tied to its specific ache (the 100 survivors; the boredom
   of having already won) rather than general personality colour — and let that tic's tone shift
   slightly across a season the way GLaDOS's did across one game, even while its conviction never
   moves.
5. Ration sincerity hard. Sarcasm carries diagnosis in almost every line (as voice.md already rules);
   let the unguarded, caring beat land rarely enough that it costs the narrator something to say it —
   this is what separates Murderbot's earned finale from Ava's calculated one.
6. Once per season at minimum, have the narrator admit it does not know something (whether the
   rewrite is working; what became of someone specific) — an omniscient voice that never runs out of
   answers stops being able to want anything, which breaks finding 1.
7. Reserve non-linear, out-of-order structure for a specific formal moment (a "Watchmaker"-style
   episode) rather than a default technique — it should demonstrate the narrator's relationship to
   time, not just look clever.
8. Watch continuously for the Ava trap: if a viewer could reasonably read the narrator's warmth as a
   device to keep them watching rather than a character's feeling, that is the failure mode Garland's
   film names exactly, and it is the single biggest risk to a narrator that is, definitionally, trying
   to persuade its audience of something.
9. In the narrated-video form specifically, let the visual track carry an ache the narration text
   never states — Stanton's "saddest character" note describes a robot with almost no lines at all.
10. However cosmic the scope of an episode (empires, timelines, the death of Earth), close on one
    small concrete image, not a summary of the theme — this is the single most consistently attested
    finding across every source in this brief.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does the narrator want something specific and physical that its power genuinely cannot obtain?
2. Is that want revisited across the piece without ever being resolved?
3. Could the narrator's own power solve this episode's central problem outright — and if so, has
   something credibly stopped it from just doing that?
4. Does the narrator have one recurring verbal tic that encodes its private ache, rather than generic
   "personality"?
5. Is the narrator tested near to breaking at least once, or does it coast on certainty the whole way
   through?
6. Does the human protagonist change more than the narrator does?
7. Is there at least one thing, this season, the narrator admits it does not know?
8. Is the narrator's sincerity rationed — would the caring beat still land if it appeared twice as
   often?
9. If a viewer squinted, could the narrator's warmth be reread as a device for managing their
   attention rather than a felt thing (the Ava test)? What in the piece rules that reading out?
10. Does non-linear structure, if used, demonstrate something about the narrator's relationship to
    time, or is it just a stylistic flourish?
11. Does the episode end on one small, concrete image rather than a stated theme?
12. Is the beneficiary of any power or inequality the narrator raises named on-panel in the same
    piece, per the-reader.md's existing rule?
13. Does the comedy ever land on a human character rather than the system or the narrator's own
    condition?
14. Would cutting the narrator's stated want change how the ending feels? If not, it isn't load-bearing.
