# Word and Picture: Captions, Stills and the Narrated Image

## What this covers

Both of BadCode's primary forms hand a **still image** to a **narrating voice** — the scroll comic
pairs a panel with a caption, the 3–5 minute video pairs a still or clip with voice-over — and in
both, the two channels should do different jobs, not the same job twice. This brief covers the
taxonomy of what a word can do that a picture can't (and vice versa), the caption/balloon
distinction, the silent panel as deliberate absence, stills under narration (Ken Burns; McGuire's
*Here* as a still-frame timeline), and the specific trap of AI-generated stills — limited pose
range, drift across a sequence — that makes redundant captioning both more tempting and more
damaging. It leaves alone: gutter timing (brief 11, Eisner), the VO dramatic-earning test (brief
09, McKee), and lettering as visual typography rather than a word-count problem.

## Top findings

1. **A caption that repeats the picture is dead weight; the test is whether removing it destroys
   the meaning.** McCloud's word/picture taxonomy names the failure (duo-specific: both channels say
   the same thing) versus the target (interdependent: neither alone carries the idea) (unverified —
   taxonomy recalled from memory; the book itself is lending-restricted, see Disagreements).
2. **Moore's famously over-long panel descriptions are instructions, not draft caption copy.** Dave
   Gibbons recalled designing *Watchmen*'s characters from "the descriptions that Alan had
   provided," changing "little" from what Moore wrote — the prose briefs the artist; it never
   appears on the page (Wikipedia, *Dave Gibbons*).
3. **A caption should carry what the picture structurally cannot.** Blambot's script convention
   enforces this by keeping panel description and lettering in physically separate zones so a
   writer can't accidentally duplicate a visible action in dialogue: "the actions of characters
   should be here, (not in the lettering area)" (Blambot, *Comic Script Basics*).
4. **Caption and balloon are functionally different channels, not interchangeable boxes.** Captions
   narrate (place, time, editorial voice); balloons speak. Conventionally balloons are white and
   captions differently coloured, so the reader sorts "who's talking" before reading a word
   (Wikipedia, *Speech balloon*).
5. **A still doesn't need a caption to hold time — the frame can.** McGuire's *Here* layers decades
   into one fixed location with no narration; the 2014 book puts the room's corner "in the bound
   gutter of the open book" so the object *is* the timeline (Wikipedia, *Here*).
6. **McGuire's discipline for a wordless, multi-era panel: draw it "generic," "as easy to read as an
   instruction manual."** Ambition in the image demands plainness in the drawing, or the reader
   can't parse it without a caption bailing them out (Wikipedia, quoting McGuire).
7. **Ken Burns didn't invent stills-under-narration — he named the relationship it depends on.**
   The pan-and-zoom move dates to 1930s–50s newsreels and the 1957 Palme d'Or documentary *City of
   Gold*; Burns's own account: it's "our attempt to 'wake the dead,'" needing "a much more nuanced
   and complicated relationship to the photograph … as well as the soundtrack" (Wikipedia, *Ken
   Burns effect*, quoting a 2014 Burns Reddit AMA).
8. **Voice-over and image can run in deliberate counterpoint, not just sync.** The baseline film
   definition allows narration over images "that may or may not be related to the words being
   spoken" (Wikipedia, *Voice-over*) — mismatch is a tool, available by design.
9. **Broadcast narration has a measured pace, slower than conversation.** BBC English radio
   bulletins average 168 words per minute against Spanish RNE's 210 — "read it aloud and see if it
   fits" is a testable number, not a vibe (Wikipedia, *Speech tempo*).
10. **Script convention exists specifically to stop a writer over-directing the artist.** Panel
    description caps at staging necessity; everything expressive goes in dialogue/caption, and the
    two must never say the same thing (Blambot, *Comic Script Basics*).
11. **Gaiman's *Sandman* script time went from two weeks to a month** as the density each panel
    needed to carry increased once he found the series' own voice around issue eight (Wikipedia,
    *The Sandman (comic book)*).
12. **The generated still has a trustworthiness ceiling a drawn panel doesn't.** A drawn panel can
    be cheaply redrawn to add information a caption needs; a generated image is a locked
    photographic fact once accepted, with real pose/identity drift risk across a sequence
    (unverified — BadCode's own documented production constraint, not a web-sourced claim, but the
    load-bearing fact behind this brief's redundancy test).

## The techniques in detail

### McCloud's seven word/picture combinations

**What it is.** A taxonomy of how caption content relates to the image beside it: word-specific
(words carry it, picture illustrates), picture-specific (picture carries it, words just add
atmosphere/sound), duo-specific (both say the same thing), additive/intersecting (one elaborates or
reframes the other), parallel (word and image run side by side without touching), montage (words
become part of the image — signage, integrated typography), and interdependent (neither alone would
carry the idea; together they make a third) *(unverified — the primary text, in either*
Understanding Comics *or* Making Comics*, is lending-restricted and could not be quoted; Wikipedia's
*Panel (comics)* page confirms both books are the standard citation for this theory without
reproducing it — see Disagreements)*.

**Why it works.** It names the exact failure this brief chases: duo-specific redundancy costs the
reader's attention twice for one idea, and signals the writer didn't trust the image.

**Worked example.** Blambot's script convention enforces the interdependent end structurally: panel
description holds staging the artist needs and the reader never reads as prose; lettering holds
what only words carry. Duo-specific writing is prevented at the script stage.

**Failure mode.** Captioning every panel "to be safe" produces duo-specific writing throughout —
competent, over-explained, exhausting, because every beat gets told twice.

### The caption/balloon distinction as a reading contract

**What it is.** A caption is generally the storyteller's voice; a balloon is a character's, tied to
a mouth. The container (colour, shape) tells the reader how to hear a line before it's parsed
(Wikipedia, *Speech balloon*).

**Why it works.** Losing the distinction collapses two reading contracts into one; readers stop
trusting either channel.

**Worked example.** A sarcastic-future-narrator caption and an in-scene character's balloon are two
different speakers who must never be visually confusable — exactly what the convention protects.

**Failure mode.** Same box style for narrator and character speech, forcing readers to decode "who"
before they can process "what."

### The silent panel and the splash page

**What it is.** A wordless panel hands pacing and emotional interpretation entirely to the image. A
splash (full-page/spread single image) is the opposite scale extreme: one frame doing an
establishing shot and a tonal overture at once *(unverified — standard account of Eisner's
arguments in* Comics and Sequential Art *and* Graphic Storytelling and Visual Narrative*; Wikipedia
confirms both books exist and are "widely used by students of cartooning," based on Eisner's School
of Visual Arts lectures, but doesn't preserve his specific arguments, and the books couldn't be
fetched)*.

**Why it works.** Removing text is a communication choice — "nothing needs translating, just
look" — but only if the image alone can actually carry the beat.

**Worked example.** McGuire's *Here* is a book-length experiment in the silent-panel principle:
entire spreads carry decades of causality with zero narration, using recurring props (watches,
keys, a wallet) as the only continuity anchors needed (Wikipedia, *Here*).

**Failure mode.** A caption-free panel that needed one reads as a mistake, not a choice — readers
can't tell "trusts you" from "forgot," so the image must be unambiguous enough to earn the silence.

### Stills under narration: the Ken Burns relationship

**What it is.** A held photograph worked by camera movement while a voice narrates over it —
popularly named for Ken Burns, documented back to 1930s newsreel practice and 1957's *City of Gold*
(Wikipedia, *Ken Burns effect*).

**Why it works.** A photograph carries no argument alone — no causality, no "and then." Burns's own
words: the technique is "our attempt to 'wake the dead,'" depending on "a much more nuanced and
complicated relationship to the photograph … as well as the soundtrack" (Ken Burns, quoted on
Wikipedia).

**Worked example.** BadCode's narrated-video format is structurally identical: the still supplies
the fact, the narration supplies the argument.

**Failure mode.** Camera movement without narrational purpose is decoration — pan-and-zoom dresses
up a still but doesn't, alone, give it a claim.

### Voice-over as counterpoint, not just sync

**What it is.** The baseline definition allows narration over images "that may or may not be
related to the words being spoken" (Wikipedia, *Voice-over*) — sync is a choice, not the default.

**Why it works.** Counterpoint (ironic mismatch) only reads as intentional if the piece's default
relationship is legible sync, so the one break stands out.

**Worked example.** A narrator calling an outcome inevitable over a still of the preventable human
moment — lands only if most of the piece has already trained the viewer to expect agreement.

**Failure mode.** Constant irony trains the audience to distrust narration entirely, at which point
it can't state anything plainly — a real risk for a narrator whose whole voice is dry and knowing.

### Narration pace as measurable craft

**What it is.** Narration has a comparable words-per-minute rate; BBC English radio bulletins
average 168 wpm against Spanish RNE's 210 (Wikipedia, *Speech tempo*) — "read it aloud and see if
it fits" has a real number behind it.

**Why it works.** A caption/VO line must fit the time the image is on screen. Writing to a
words-per-second budget forces the cuts that duo-specific habits resist.

*(The "let the picture lead" discipline attributed to the BBC Natural History Unit and to Sheila
Curran Bernard's* Documentary Storytelling *— narration supplies only what camera and interview
cannot: motive, elapsed time, consequence — could not be sourced to a fetched page this pass. It is
standard documentary-craft knowledge (unverified), flagged rather than silently assumed.)*

## Disagreements and caveats

- **Grade honesty.** McCloud's exact taxonomy and its source book, Moore's specific caption dicta,
  Eisner's own silent-panel/splash arguments, Klein's and Piekos's numeric caption budgets, and
  Bernard's/BBC NHU's narration principles could not be verified against a fetched primary source
  this session — the relevant Internet Archive scans return HTTP 403 on search-inside, and every
  substitute search engine tried failed (see Sources). Marked (unverified) at point of use rather
  than dropped or smoothed over.
- **Wikipedia is tertiary, not practitioner.** Quotes it carries (Burns, Gibbons, McGuire, Gaiman,
  Moore) are attributable; the surrounding framing is editorial synthesis, a lower grade than the
  brief's stated preference for primary transcripts.
- **"Ken Burns effect" overstates his originality even by his own account** — Wikipedia is explicit
  that Burns credits Jerome Liebling and *City of Gold* as his influences, while his own quoted
  words describe something more specific than the mechanical zoom the term now means.
- **Errol Morris was a weaker fit than commissioned.** His documented work (*Believing Is Seeing*,
  the *Time* piece on photography) argues photography is unreliable as *evidence* — adjacent to,
  not the same as, the craft of pairing narration with stills. Included only for the point about
  what an image can't be trusted to prove, which bears on generated-still drift.
- **Todd Klein's current blog isn't a lettering resource.** Checked directly; recent posts are
  Disney-animation reviews, not comics craft — a genuine dead end.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Run a duo-specific check on every caption before it ships.** Delete it: does the reader lose an
   idea, or only lose confirmation of something already visible? If the latter, cut or replace.
2. **Reserve captions for what the still structurally cannot show**: foreknowledge ("it all went
   south pretty fast, in the end"), causality across time, and the named beneficiary of a decision —
   `the-reader.md` rule 5 ("name the decision, not the technology") is always a words-job.
3. **Use the silent panel deliberately.** At least one beat per story should carry zero caption — a
   full-bleed panel where the sarcasm goes quiet and the image alone lands the "care" dial from
   `voice.md`. Silence is where the snark-to-care shift gets room to register.
4. **Script the narrated-video camera move against the line, not the clip length.** A still under
   narration only works if the push-in/pan tracks the point the VO is making that second, not a
   fixed-duration pan.
5. **Write to a words-per-second budget per shot.** Time each VO line against its still's actual
   on-screen duration before recording; a line that only fits by speed-reading means the shot's too
   short or the line is duo-specific.
6. **Give the generated still a redundancy test tuned to pose/identity drift.** Decide before
   generation which single fact the image must nail (expression, gesture, environment), and write
   the caption to cover what the pose range can't stretch to — not after the image is accepted.
7. **Split narrator captions from in-story dialogue visually, not just in prose style** — different
   container, colour or placement, so the sarcasm/care two-step never reads as the same voice doing
   both jobs.
8. **Budget counterpoint sparingly.** Default to narrator/image agreement; spend mismatch only on
   the beats designed to land the political point, or every line stops being believed.
9. **Let caption density signal register across the serialised arc**, the way Gaiman's script length
   grew with *Sandman*'s voice — plainer captions early, more interdependent beats once reader trust
   is established.
10. **Add the redundancy test as a literal checklist item** in `make-comic` and `music-video-short`'s
    review passes (see Diagnostic questions).

## Diagnostic questions

1. Delete this caption: does the reader lose an idea, or only lose confirmation of what's already
   visible?
2. Does any caption describe a visible action the character is already performing on-panel?
3. Is there at least one beat with zero caption, where the image alone carries a turn?
4. Are narrator caption and character balloon visually distinguishable before either is read?
5. Does every VO line fit its shot's actual on-screen duration at a natural speaking pace?
6. Is there a deliberate narration/image mismatch, and does the rest of the piece establish enough
   sync for it to read as a choice?
7. For a generated still: was the one fact it needs to nail decided before generation, and does the
   caption cover what its pose/identity limits can't?
8. Does a caption name a decision-maker or beneficiary the image alone cannot show?
9. Is the sarcastic register ever suspended for a plain line, and is that reserved for the care beat
   rather than scattered at random?
10. In a splash-scale panel, is the image doing the establishing-shot-plus-tone work, or is a
    caption quietly doing it while the splash is decorative?
11. Across a sequence of the same generated character, is there visible drift the caption is papering
    over rather than the sequence being regenerated to fix?
12. Would a viewer with the sound muted still get the plot's external facts from images alone? Would
    a reader with images hidden get the same facts from captions alone? (Both "yes" signals
    duo-specific redundancy somewhere.)
