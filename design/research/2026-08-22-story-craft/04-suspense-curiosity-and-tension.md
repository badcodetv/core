# Suspense, Curiosity and Tension Mechanics

## What this covers

The machinery that keeps a reader or viewer from leaving: suspense (dread over a known threat) vs
surprise (shock at an unknown one); dramatic irony as suspense's engine when the *audience* knows
more than the character; curiosity as a measurable drive (information-gap theory) rather than a
vague "hook"; the memory mechanics of unresolved tasks (Zeigarnik) and the craft technique built on
top (open loops); named structural formulas for promise-keeping (Sanderson's promise/progress/
payoff, the midpoint turn); the economy principle that a planted detail must fire (Chekhov's gun)
and its cousin, foreshadowing; the ticking clock as a literalised deadline; the mystery box as a
withholding strategy and its best-documented public failure (*Lost*); the philosophical puzzle of
why a known outcome still grips on a re-read (the paradox of suspense); and tension felt
sentence-by-sentence (micro-tension) rather than tension as the structural fact of conflict. Left
to siblings: how a piece earns its first seconds (05, openings), how it lands the final beat (06,
endings), and the underlying engagement research — retention curves, what platforms measure (07).

## Top findings

1. **Suspense beats surprise on duration.** Hitchcock's arithmetic: an unannounced bomb gives
   "fifteen seconds of surprise"; the same bomb shown to the audience in advance, clock ticking,
   gives "fifteen minutes of suspense" — because the audience now participates. His rule: inform
   the audience, except when the twist itself is the point.
2. **Curiosity is a measurable deprivation state, not a mood.** Loewenstein's 1994 information-gap
   theory: curiosity fires on a *noticed* gap between known and wanted knowledge, felt as
   deprivation until closed (secondary reporting — see caveats).
3. **Dramatic irony is suspense's most reliable delivery vehicle.** The audience holds information
   a character lacks, across installation, exploitation and resolution.
4. **An unfinished task sticks in memory better than a finished one — with real caveats.**
   Zeigarnik's 1927 finding is the root of "open loops," but a 2025 meta-analysis found the memory
   claim itself "lacks universal validity," while the *urge to resume* (Ovsiankina effect) held up
   better.
5. **A story is a promise, kept via visible progress, discharged as surprising *and* inevitable.**
   Sanderson's promise/progress/payoff: promise set in the opening pages, progress "signposted" or
   readers quietly quit, payoff "a surprising but fulfilling answer to your promise."
6. **A planted detail that never fires is a broken promise.** Chekhov's gun — "if you have hung a
   pistol on the wall… it should be fired" — with Chekhov's own *Cherry Orchard* breaking the rule
   on purpose, the exception that proves it's a convention, not a law.
7. **Withholding without payoff reads as contempt, not art.** Abrams' mystery box treats an unopened
   box as "forever empowered with potential and wonder"; *Lost* is reported to have trained
   audiences via "Pavlovian conditioning… for eventual (albeit fleeting) reward" until the verdict
   became the box "may as well literally be void."
8. **Suspense survives a known outcome, and nobody agrees on why.** Carroll's paradox: suspense
   needs uncertainty; knowing the ending removes it; people still feel suspense on a re-read.
   Carroll says viewers *entertain* uncertainty; Gerrig says cognition can't retrieve a known
   outcome fast enough to block the feeling; Smuts says powerlessness, not doubt, is what matters.
9. **A midpoint reversal comes in exactly two flavours.** False victory (a win that curdles) or
   false defeat (a catastrophe that cracks open) — Save the Cat: "if you can crack the Midpoint,
   you can crack the story," worked against *Alien* and *Gravity*.
10. **Tension is felt; conflict is structural — you can have one without the other.** Maass's
    micro-tension is generated by conflicting feeling *inside* one point-of-view mind, not by
    external plot conflict, and works "not in the story but in the next few seconds."
11. **Sanderson's escalation engine is "Yes, but / No, and," not a template.** Every attempt
    succeeds-with-a-complication or fails-and-worsens, independent of which formal structure houses
    it.
12. **A ticking clock only works if the audience can see the numbers, and wears out fast if
    overused.** *24*'s own creator: the device "never occurs in real life… but on our show it
    happens every week" — its power and its self-parody are the same fact from two angles.

## The techniques in detail

### Hitchcock's bomb: suspense vs surprise

**What it is.** Hitchcock, to Truffaut: an unannounced explosion gives "fifteen seconds of
surprise"; the same scene, played after the audience has watched the bomb get planted and sees a
clock counting down, gives "fifteen minutes of suspense" because the audience wants to warn
characters who can't hear them. **Why it works.** Surprise is one spike; suspense is sustained
because the audience does active work — worrying, anticipating — for the scene's whole length, not
its last second. **Failure mode.** Treating every reveal as a twist withholds exactly the
information that would have made the preceding scene grip; Hitchcock's own caveat is that pure
surprise is only right "when the surprise is a twist… the highlight" — otherwise it trades fifteen
minutes of tension for fifteen seconds of shock.

### Dramatic irony

**What it is.** The audience knows something a character doesn't, so ordinary lines read as
"counter-productive… to what their situation actually requires," across installation (gap opens),
exploitation (story lives in the gap), resolution (gap closes). **Why it works.** It generalises
Hitchcock's bomb: any known-to-us, unknown-to-them asymmetry reframes every line the character
speaks. **Failure mode.** A gap opened and never closed collapses into audience superiority with no
payoff.

### Loewenstein's information-gap theory

**What it is.** Curiosity is triggered by a *noticed* gap between current and desired knowledge,
felt as deprivation until resolved. **Why it works.** It converts "interesting" into a trigger
condition (must be noticed) plus a resolution condition (closing it relieves the feeling) — the
theoretical floor under both the mystery box and the open loop. **Caveat.** The primary 1994
*Psychological Bulletin* paper could not be read directly this session (hosted PDF is an unreadable
scan; a mirror 403'd), so the mechanism is drawn from converging secondary summaries —
**(unverified)** at the level of exact wording. **Failure mode.** An unnoticed gap generates no
curiosity; one that feels unclosably large generates frustration instead.

### Zeigarnik and open loops

**What it is.** Zeigarnik's 1927 experiments found interrupted tasks recalled better than completed
ones (per Lewin's café observation about waiters), the root of the "open loop" craft device. **The
caveat that matters.** A cited 1968 replication found no significant recall difference, and a 2025
meta-analysis concluded the memory-specific claim "lacks universal validity," while the related
*urge to resume* (Ovsiankina) held up better. **Practical implication.** "Open loops hook the
brain" overstates a contested memory finding; "audiences want closure" is the better-evidenced,
weaker claim underneath it. **Failure mode.** Stacking loops without discharging any reads as
withholding, not momentum (see mystery box).

### Chekhov's gun, foreshadowing, planting-and-payoff

**What it is.** "If in the first act you have hung a pistol on the wall, then in the following one
it should be fired." Foreshadowing is the broader device — planted "suggestions or warnings about
events to come" — distinct from the red herring, designed to mislead rather than pay off; a cited
worked example is Joseph's dream of bowing sheaves, paid off when his brothers literally bow. **Why
it works.** A contract of economy: every noticed detail implicitly promises relevance. **The
counter-current.** Hemingway mocked the rule (readers hunt symbolism regardless of intent); Andrea
Phillips argues over-application makes fiction "colorless"; Chekhov's own play breaks the rule on
purpose. **Failure mode.** Too strict and every object becomes a legible clue, killing texture; too
loose and unfired plants read as sloppiness.

### Ticking clocks

**What it is.** A literalised, visible deadline that converts open-ended dread into a countdown.
**Why it works.** It gives Hitchcock's "the audience must know" condition a concrete form. **Worked
example.** *24*, structured as real-time episodes; its creator conceded the device "never occurs in
real life… but on our show it happens every week" (a fictional overuse serious enough that it later
got cited in real interrogation-policy debate). **Failure mode.** A clock the audience can't
actually see fails to deliver the specific suspense Hitchcock describes; a clock in every scene
cheapens into a tic.

### The mystery box and its failure mode

**What it is.** Abrams' childhood magic-shop box, never opened, "forever empowered with potential
and wonder," offered as a model for withheld story information. **Worked failure case.** Applied
across *Lost*, the technique reportedly trained audiences via "Pavlovian conditioning: temporary
confusion for eventual (albeit fleeting) reward" until the finale left core questions unaddressed
and the retrospective verdict was the box "may as well literally be void." The distinction against
a true MacGuffin (Hitchcock's term for a device secondary to theme) is that a MacGuffin can be empty
*because the theme never rode on it*; *Lost* sold the mystery as the payload itself. **Failure
mode, plainly.** A mystery box is a promise with hidden contents; empty, it isn't mystery, it's
having been strung along.

### The paradox of suspense

**What it is** (Stanford Encyclopedia's formal statement): (1) suspense requires uncertainty; (2)
knowing the outcome removes uncertainty; (3) people demonstrably feel suspense on a re-read of a
known outcome. All three seem true; they can't all be true together. **Competing, unresolved
solutions.** Carroll denies (1) in its strong form — suspense needs only *entertained* uncertainty.
Gerrig denies (2) functionally — "evolution has not equipped humans with the ability to look up
known outcomes to repeated events," so retrieval is too slow to block the feeling. Smuts denies (1)
altogether — powerlessness, not doubt, is what matters. Yanal denies (3) — re-readers feel
misidentified anticipation, not real suspense. **Why it matters for craft.** Whichever is right,
character stakes and dread — not plot uncertainty alone — are what let an outcome telegraphed from
scene one (as BadCode's framing already does) keep gripping.

### Reversals, the midpoint turn, and escalation

**What it is.** A midpoint flip: **false victory** (a win that turns hollow or costly) or **false
defeat** (a catastrophe that cracks open a new path) — Save the Cat: "if you can crack the
Midpoint, you can crack the story," and midpoints often coincide with raised stakes, a new clock,
and previously separate threads crossing. **Worked examples.** False defeat: *Alien*'s chestburster,
turning a contained threat loose. False victory: *Gravity*'s Matt sacrificing himself, making the
"win" cost everything. **Complementary mechanism.** Sanderson's running "Yes, but / No, and" —
every attempt succeeds-with-complication or fails-and-worsens — ratchets tension independent of
which formal template is in use. **Failure mode.** A midpoint that just repeats existing stakes at
larger scale fails to reverse direction and reads as padding.

### Tension vs conflict, and micro-tension

**What it is.** Conflict is structural (incompatible forces); tension is *felt*, and the two are
not the same quantity. Maass's micro-tension — "the moment-by-moment tension that keeps the reader
in a constant state of suspense over what will happen… not in the story but in the next few
seconds" — is generated by conflicting feeling *inside* one point-of-view mind, not external plot
conflict. **Why it works.** A scene with zero external conflict can still grip if the internal
feeling is at war with itself (dread mixed with hope). **Failure mode.** A scene with enormous
structural conflict (a fight, a chase) can read flat if every line inside it carries one note —
action without felt ambivalence is inert on the page even when it isn't inert on the plot.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **Grade spread is wide.** The Stanford Encyclopedia entry is peer-reviewed-adjacent philosophy;
  Loewenstein's paper is peer-reviewed psychology but was only verifiable via secondary summaries
  here, not primary text; Sanderson, Maass and Abrams are practitioner testimony with no empirical
  backing beyond track record; a common "external / internal / philosophical" three-tier stakes
  framing could not be sourced within this session's budget — treat it as **(unverified)** wherever
  it appears elsewhere in this knowledge base.
- **Zeigarnik is craft folklore riding on a contested finding.** The *memory* claim is unreliable
  across paradigms per the 2025 meta-analysis; the *urge-to-resume* claim (a different, related
  effect) holds up better. "Audiences want closure" is the defensible version; "audiences remember
  unclosed things better" is not settled science.
- **The paradox of suspense has no consensus resolution.** Carroll, Gerrig and Smuts each deny a
  different premise; Yanal denies the phenomenon itself. Presented here as an open field, not a
  solved one.
- **Hitchcock's own rule has an unresolved exception.** "Inform the audience" sits next to "except
  when the surprise is a twist," with no stated test for which situation you're in — exactly the
  judgement call a mystery-box-heavy show can get wrong at scale.
- **Chekhov's gun is contested even as a reading of Chekhov.** Wording varies across sources, and
  his own *Cherry Orchard* is cited as breaking the rule on purpose.
- **The mystery box's failure is disputed as concept-vs-execution.** Abrams is reported to have
  "stood his ground," saying he never heard a better proposed ending; the critique argues the
  concept itself curdles once the box is empty. Whether the fault is the *technique* or the
  *production handoff* (Abrams left after the pilot) isn't settled by anything fetched here.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **The narrator already knows how it ends — lean on dramatic irony, not the mystery box.** GPOM's
   framing hands the audience Hitchcock's bomb for free: they know the shape of the outcome before
   a scene starts. Write scenes to exploit that gap (worrying *for* a character who doesn't know)
   rather than withholding the ending as a twist, which is the wrong register for a narrator whose
   authority rests on already knowing.
2. **State the bomb; don't hide it, per scene or panel.** Name the threat early in captions/VO
   ("this is the year the wages stopped moving") rather than saving it as a reveal — reserve pure
   surprise for the rare moment the twist *is* the point, and flag those as deliberate exceptions.
3. **Never open a mystery-box loop you can't guarantee closing in the release that opens it.**
   The-reader.md's audience already distrusts institutions that promise and don't deliver;
   *Lost*'s exact failure would compound that. Close each episode's local loop even while the master
   arc's loop (the 100 survivors, the time machine's actual use) stays explicitly, deliberately open.
4. **Every planted object or claim fires inside its own release.** A Gazette pen shown on-panel, or
   a line establishing "no key over the money," must do narrative work before the piece ends — this
   is also just receipt-discipline for a collective whose public claims get checked; an unfired
   plant reads as either sloppiness or a broken promise to an audience already primed to spot both.
5. **Write micro-tension into the narrator's voice, not just the plot.** Voice.md's "sarcasm
   carries the diagnosis, care carries the re-aim" *is* micro-tension: a line holding contempt for
   the mistake and tenderness for the people at once. Test individual lines for this, not just whole
   scenes.
6. **Use a midpoint reversal to reset a 20-scene arc or a single 3–5 minute video.** A false-victory
   or false-defeat beat around the centre gives both the full arc and a single short a place to
   escalate rather than repeat "and then it got worse" at the same register — check any storyboard
   for a genuine direction-change at its midpoint, not just a louder version of the same threat.
7. **Apply promise/progress/payoff at both episode and arc level.** Each story states its promise
   early (tone plus the political/economic idea it's smuggling), shows signposted progress, and
   lands a payoff that's surprising *and* inevitable — and the same triad should describe the whole
   arc, so a reviewer can ask what the Prologue promised and whether the Coda paid it or just ended
   it.
8. **A ticking clock sells better sung than spoken.** The D&B tracks are the natural home for a
   literalised countdown (a sample, a bar-count, a stated deadline) — but *24*'s lesson is that the
   device wears out fast if used everywhere, so reserve it for pieces structurally about a deadline
   (the coin's oracle-silence retirement clock, for one) rather than reaching for it by default.
9. **Never let curiosity substitute for the rule against unaimed anxiety.** Loewenstein's gap and
   the-reader.md's rule 6 only pull together if the piece *closes* the gap by naming the beneficiary
   in the same release — an automation-anxiety mystery box that never reveals who profits isn't just
   a craft failure (Lost's trap), it's a direct breach of the standing editorial rule. Treat "who
   benefits" as the gun that must fire.
10. **Test dramatic irony against, not instead of, "hero not pupil."** The audience knowing more
    than Bob must never tip into feeling superior to him — the installation/exploitation/resolution
    arc should resolve with Bob *catching up*, armed with what we knew, not left permanently behind
    it while we watch him flounder.

## Diagnostic questions

1. At the moment we want the audience worried for a character, do they already know more than the
   character does — or does a late reveal do the emotional work instead?
2. Is a threat stated and visible *before* it plays out, rather than sprung as a surprise?
3. Does every planted object, line or claim fire by the end of this release, or is it left dangling
   with no explicit "to be continued" flag?
4. Is there an open loop that survives to the next instalment, clearly signalled as intentional
   rather than an oversight?
5. Can the piece's promise be stated in one sentence from its opening beat, and does the ending pay
   off that exact sentence?
6. Is there a midpoint where the direction visibly reverses — a false victory that turns costly, or
   a false defeat that cracks open a path?
7. Does every narrator line carry at least two feelings in tension (contempt and care; dread and
   dry humour), rather than one flat register?
8. Where the story withholds information as a "mystery," do we already know and control what's in
   the box — or are we gambling on an answer not yet written?
9. Does the curiosity gap connect to something the audience already half-suspects, rather than
   something they have no frame to notice as a gap at all?
10. When automation or AI anxiety is the bomb under the table, is the beneficiary named on-panel in
    the same piece — or does the dread go unaimed?
11. Would this scene still generate tension for someone who already knows the outcome — is the
    tension riding on character stakes, not just plot uncertainty?
12. Is there a moment of pure surprise, and if so, is it doing the one job surprise is good for (a
    twist that is itself the point) rather than substituting for suspense we skipped setting up?
13. Does dramatic irony ever tip into the audience feeling superior to the protagonist, and if so,
    does the character eventually catch up to what we knew?
14. Is there a scene with zero external conflict that still reads as tense because of internal,
    conflicting feeling — or does every "quiet" scene go slack?
15. If this piece belongs to the serialised arc, does the known ending work *for* it as dramatic
    irony, rather than something the piece awkwardly pretends the audience doesn't know?
