# The Narrated Short Video: Explainers, Montage and Retention Craft

## What this covers

The 3–5 minute narrated video — stills or clips carried by a voice-over — as a distinct storytelling
form, from the science-explainer end (Kurzgesagt, Johnny Harris) through the video essay (Every Frame
a Painting, The Nerdwriter) to the art-film end (Chris Marker's *La Jetée*, Erik Wernquist's
*Wanderers*, the Eameses' *Powers of Ten*), plus the retention mechanics that govern whether anyone
sees the middle of it (YouTube's hook/re-hook grammar, the MrBeast document, TikTok/Shorts' first
seconds) and the sound-design techniques for pacing narration against picture and music. It deliberately
leaves to sibling briefs: script dialogue and character voice craft (that's a comics/screenwriting
brief), the technical mechanics of Flow/Veo prompting and shot generation, and platform algorithm
mechanics beyond what bears directly on narrative pacing. This is about the *shape* of a narrated
short and the clock it runs against, not the tools that build it.

## Top findings

1. **Kurzgesagt treats "ending on hope" as a deliberate correction, not a default tone.** Founder
   Philipp Dettmer says the channel's reputation skews gloomy, so scripts are built to land on
   solvable framing — "if we lose hope, that's the same as losing." Hope is written in, not assumed.
2. **Every Frame a Painting's governing rule is "therefore/but," never "and then."** Tony Zhou drew
   this from Orson Welles's *F for Fake*: every beat must cause the next, not just follow it.
3. **La Jetée earns its one moving shot by refusing movement everywhere else.** Critics reading the
   film's photo-roman form describe stillness as producing "impermanence" and "permanence" at once —
   a formal echo of a man unstuck in time; scarcity is what makes the one motion "glorious."
4. **A voice can carry a film with no plot at all.** *Wanderers* has, in a reviewer's words, "no
   apparent story — other than what you might imagine for yourself"; its spine is Carl Sagan's own
   archival reading of *Pale Blue Dot*, borrowed wholesale rather than written for the film.
5. **Adam Curtis runs two tracks at once, and that's exactly where practitioners and critics split.**
   He pairs illustrative narration with "free-form riffs" floating above it "like jazz." Curtis calls
   himself "fundamentally an emotional journalist"; critic Jonathan Rosenbaum argues the technique
   exploits the Kuleshov effect to persuade beneath rational scrutiny.
6. **The Eameses cut their own information density between drafts, not up.** The 1968 prototype of
   *Powers of Ten* ran six simultaneous on-screen data readouts; the 1977 final cut them to "the most
   essential information" for better comprehension and retention.
7. **A leaked MrBeast document quantifies the first-minute cliff in raw numbers, not theory.** It
   says the team "lost 21 million viewers in the first minute" of one video — and calls that *better*
   than their average.
8. **TikTok's own internal goals, per New York Times reporting, are exactly two metrics: "retention"
   and "time spent."** Nothing about quality. The algorithm rewards rewatched, unscrolled-past video,
   not good video.
9. **The most notorious broadcast interruption in history worked because of what came *before* it.**
   Welles's 1938 *War of the Worlds* built dread through long, deliberately tedious dance-music
   stretches between bulletins — producer John Houseman called the drag itself "increasingly
   sinister."
10. **Ira Glass's structural trick for *This American Life* is action, then a step back to a bigger
    thought, then back to the action** — a technique he says he still uses, learned early in his
    editing career.
11. **The video essay (Zhou, Puschak) and the explainer/challenge video (Harris, MrBeast) are
    different disciplines in the same coat.** One is argument-with-evidence, built to be paused and
    rewatched; the other is proof-with-a-promise, built to survive a click-away impulse every few
    seconds. Confusing them produces a lecturing explainer or a pandering essay.
12. **The Nerdwriter frames the whole form around Virginia Woolf's line that "an essay should lay us
    under a spell with its first word and we should only wake refreshed with its last"** — a
    six-minute video as mood, not argument document.

## The techniques in detail

### The hope-shaped explainer (Kurzgesagt)

**What it is.** A script drafted, fact-checked by two or three people, reviewed by outside experts,
and rewritten across roughly a dozen passes until "facts becoming a captivating story" balances.
**Why it works.** Dettmer frames the goal as letting viewers "reach the conclusion by yourself" rather
than being told what to think — arguing by honest framing, not assertion, for an audience primed to
distrust being lectured. **Worked example.** Dettmer explicitly counters the channel's gloomy
reputation by re-engineering scripts toward solvable, hopeful framing — a corrective move stated in
interview, not an instinctive house habit. **Failure mode.** Dettmer also admits that, years in, "I
still do not have a good grasp of what really works" — the format looks systematic from outside but
runs on iteration and intuition; treating it as a fixed formula is the SEO guides' mistake, below.

### The single-idea essay film and the "therefore/but" rule (Every Frame a Painting)

**What it is.** Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos built each episode around one narrow claim about one
filmmaker's technique (Jackie Chan's action geography, Edgar Wright's visual gags) and refused to
wander into plot summary. **Why it works.** Zhou's rule, borrowed from Welles's *F for Fake*, is that
an essay should never have "and then" moments — only "therefore this happens" moments, so each beat
is a consequence, not an addition. **Worked example.** The Jackie Chan episode is the channel's
most-viewed at over 25 million — one technical claim, argued end to end. **Failure mode.** Content
ID's fair-use requirements kept the channel off filmmakers needing longer clips (Tarkovsky, Varda) —
platform constraints shape which stories a form *can* tell, not just how well.

### The essay as spell (The Nerdwriter)

**What it is.** Evan Puschak's six-minute weekly essays, built around a single feeling he had
watching something, structured to transmit that feeling rather than catalogue evidence. **Why it
works.** Puschak designs for the medium's native behaviours — scrubbing, rewinding, playback speed —
treating YouTube as suited to "breaking things down," a point he connects to Marshall McLuhan.
**Worked example.** His touchstone is Virginia Woolf's line that an essay "should lay us under a
spell with its first word and we should only wake refreshed with its last" — length in service of a
held mood, not information density. **Failure mode.** This only works if the audience already wants
contemplation; on a distrustful, scroll-first audience it reads as slow.

### Constraint as engine: *La Jetée*

**What it is.** Chris Marker's 1962 short tells a full time-travel plot in 28 minutes almost entirely
through still photographs with voice-over — a "photo-roman" after the photographed comic strips of
1950s–60s European magazines. **Why it works.** Critics describe the stasis of individual frames as
producing "permanence" and "transience" at once, mirroring the plot's collapse of past, present and
future — and stillness forces "active viewing," reading composition and lighting a moving image would
let you half-notice. **Worked example.** The film's one moving-image sequence — a woman's eyes
opening, dissolving from photograph to motion — sits at its structural centre and works *because* it
is the only motion in the film. **Failure mode.** The technique depends on stillness being total
elsewhere; a photo-roman that cheats with occasional pans loses the contrast that gives the one real
movement its power.

### Two tracks at once: Adam Curtis's montage-and-narration

**What it is.** Curtis builds essay-films from BBC archive footage — including out-takes never meant
for broadcast — layered with sardonic voice-over and pop-derived music. **Why it works, and why it's
contested.** Curtis calls himself "fundamentally an emotional journalist," aiming to "explain things
to you" with "real emotional power," arguing rational appeal alone doesn't change behaviour. Critic
Jonathan Rosenbaum reads it through the Kuleshov effect instead — viewers unconsciously supply
connections between juxtaposed shots — and calls Curtis's borrowed scores "ornamental rather than
argumentative." A separate essay quotes critic David Jenkins's circularity charge ("he uses smoke and
mirrors to attack the smoke and mirrors") and Boyd Tonkin's observation that Curtis's "didactic,
expository voice" sometimes fights his own destabilising images. **Worked example.** *The Power of
Nightmares* opens on a free-form montage — wind, percussive stabs, no clear referent — before the
voice-over supplies its argument, disorienting ahead of persuading. **Failure mode.** The same
two-track method that gives Curtis's films their texture is the exact thing his critics call
manipulation — there's no version of it without that risk attached.

### Borrowed voice, no plot: *Wanderers* and *Pale Blue Dot*

**What it is.** Erik Wernquist's 2014 short pairs photorealistic solar-system visions with Carl
Sagan's own archival narration, taken from his reading of *Pale Blue Dot* rather than written to
picture. **Why it works.** A reviewer's framing: the film "has no apparent story — other than what
you might imagine for yourself" — narration supplies the only through-line, and its authority comes
from being Sagan's actual recorded voice, not a performance of it. **Worked example.** All fifteen
scenes are digital recreations of real, photographed solar-system locations — visuals claiming
documentary authority alongside genuinely archival narration claiming the same. **Failure mode.**
This only works because the narration was never written *for* the images; a new narration imitating
the effect risks pastiche rather than borrowed weight.

### Scale as narrative: *Powers of Ten*

**What it is.** The Eameses' 1977 film starts on a sleeping man at one metre, pulls back by a factor
of ten every ten seconds to the edge of the universe, then reverses at a factor of ten every two
seconds to a subatomic scale, anchored both ends by the same picnic scene. **Why it works.** The
mathematical pacing *is* the narrative device: the faster return leg reads as a different emotional
register — dive versus drift — from cutting rate alone, no change in subject matter. **Worked
example.** Between the 1968 prototype and 1977 final cut, the Eameses cut their on-screen data panel
from six simultaneous readouts to "the most essential information," designing for a viewer who could
be "a ten-year-old as well as a physicist" — an intuitive "gut feeling" about scale, not technical
precision. **Failure mode.** The lesson (cut density until novice and expert land the same feeling)
is routinely inverted — creators add data layers because they can, not because a viewer needs them.

### The retention clock (YouTube long-form)

**What it is.** A grammar of hook (open on an unresolved statement), pattern interrupt (a tonal,
visual or audio change roughly every 25–45 seconds resetting drifting attention), re-hook (a
mid-video callback to what's still coming), and mini-payoffs spread through the runtime rather than
saved for the end. **Why it allegedly works.** An open loop holds attention before a viewer has
consciously decided whether to stay — the incompleteness itself is the hook. **Worked example.** The
MrBeast document operationalises this bluntly: the first minute is the single highest-stakes stretch
of any video, "no dull moments" is a standing rule, and a "re-engagement" beat is scheduled around
the three-minute mark rather than left to momentum. **Failure mode.** Most packaged "frameworks" for
this (Retention Triangle, seven-part hook formulas) come from unsourced marketing blogs with no named
practitioner behind them — real pattern, not a template with the authority of the practitioners above.

### The document: MrBeast's structural rules

**What it is.** An internal production document, leaked and posted by another creator, laying out
retention metrics (CTR, Average View Duration, Average View Percentage) and structural rules. **Why
it works.** It states CTR "is what dictates what we do for videos," recommends intensifying title
language ("I Survived" not "I Spent") to raise it, and names a "Backup Day" contingency rule for when
a stunt fails mid-shoot. **Worked example.** The document states one video "lost 21 million viewers
in the first minute" and calls that *above* the channel's usual retention — a candid number for how
brutal the opening minute is even for the platform's most-optimised channel. **Failure mode.** The
document's authenticity is unconfirmed by any party who leaked or reported it; treat its numbers as
illustrative of scale, not verified external data.

### The 1.7-second decision: TikTok and Shorts craft

**What it is.** Official TikTok guidance tells creators to "introduce your content proposition in the
first 3 seconds for better recall and awareness" — the platform's own stated rule. **Why it works.**
NYT reporting on an internal TikTok document found the platform's stated goals are "retention" and
"time spent," full stop — rewatched, unscrolled-past video is rewarded independent of any other
quality. **Worked example.** A workable hook pairs an emotional trigger with a specific payoff and an
implied promise in one line ("I wasted six months doing this wrong"), with one text overlay per shot
rather than several stacked, so a sound-off viewer can still track the point. **Failure mode.**
Treating the rule as only about the spoken hook misses that the same window must work with sound off.

### The interrupted signal: sound design as storytelling

**What it is.** Welles's 1938 *War of the Worlds* staged its Martian-invasion report as bulletins
interrupting ordinary dance-music programming, escalating to a reporter's transmission cutting to
dead air mid-sentence, then an announcer explaining a "field transmission" failure. **Why it works.**
Welles's stated intent was to make it "actually seem to be happening" — but producer John Houseman's
account credits the *stretches of ordinary music between bulletins* with making the effect
"increasingly sinister," because tedium built real-time dread before the interruption paid it off.
**Worked example.** The cut-off after the heat-ray description, dead air, then a shaken announcer,
remains the template for "something has gone wrong with the broadcast itself." **Failure mode.** It
reads as authentic only because it's rare and the surrounding programming is genuinely dull; without
the boring stretch first, it reads as a gimmick.

### The pocket theory: Ira Glass's action-and-reflection loop

**What it is.** A structural habit Glass says he still uses to build *This American Life*: stay in
concrete action, then deliberately "step back from the action and move to some bigger thought," then
return to the plot. **Why it works.** It gives a purely narrated piece a rhythm beyond information
delivery — action earns the reflection's weight, the reflection re-frames the next action. **Worked
example.** Glass frames radio's whole appeal this way: hearing someone rather than seeing them is
"like when someone is talking from the heart." **Failure mode.** Skip the "bigger thought" and a
narrated piece is just events; skip returning to action and it becomes a lecture.

## Disagreements and caveats

**Curtis: craft or manipulation, unresolved.** Curtis's own defence ("emotional journalist," rational
appeal doesn't work) sits directly against Rosenbaum's Kuleshov-effect critique and Jenkins's
"smoke and mirrors" charge. This is not a settled question — treat any use of Curtis's dual-track
method as inheriting the same open critique, not as a validated technique.

**Practitioner-grade versus listicle-grade, and the gap is large.** Kurzgesagt, Every Frame a Painting,
the Eameses, Curtis, Puschak and Glass are all traceable to named practitioners' own accounts. Most
"retention architecture" terminology (Retention Triangle, seven-part hook formulas, named percentage
thresholds) comes from unsourced marketing blogs with no named author or citation trail. The MrBeast
document sits in between: widely reported, numerically specific, never confirmed genuine by MrBeast's
team. And Dettmer's own admission — years in, "I still do not have a good grasp of what really
works" — is a direct caution against importing any of the above, including Kurzgesagt's own
successes, as a fixed template.

**Two critical readings of *La Jetée* stress different mechanisms, not contradictory ones.** One
tradition emphasises photography's relationship to memory and time (permanence versus transience);
another (Sarah Cooper's book, reviewed at *Senses of Cinema*) emphasises the graphic, compositional
reading forced by stillness and the "active viewing" it demands. Both are argued by named critics;
neither displaces the other.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Write the hope beat as a required, separate script line, never assumed from tone.** BadCode's
   narrator already carries "thankfully, you are loved, and we can fix it" as doctrine (voice.md) —
   check the draft for it the way Dettmer does, rather than trusting sarcasm to imply it.
2. **Audit every narrated script by replacing "and then" with "therefore" or "but."** If a GPOM scene
   only follows the last one rather than being caused by it, it's a summary beat, not a story beat —
   and the lecture-allergic reader (the-reader.md, rule 3) feels the difference even unnamed.
3. **Reserve movement/animation for exactly one beat per narrated video, La Jetée-style.** If every
   panel gets a Flow-animated pan, none land as significant; spend the "motion budget" only on the
   single moment the narrator wants to hit hardest.
4. **Use the "degrading broadcast signal" as an on-brand device, sparingly.** BadCode's frame is
   weights transmitted back from a dead future — a scene where the narrator's own signal stutters to
   static before recovering is the premise made audible, not a borrowed gimmick. Once per release,
   not per video, on the same scarcity logic as point 3.
5. **Script the first 60 seconds against the MrBeast document's numbers, not scene-setting instinct.**
   If the platform's most-optimised channel documents losing millions of viewers in minute one, the
   cold open needs the whole promise — hook, stakes, tone — front-loaded before any table-setting.
6. **Build the narrator's rhythm on Glass's action → bigger-thought → action loop.** Action is where
   something happens to a character (the sympathetic worker, the "Karen"); the bigger-thought beat is
   where the narrator goes cold and names the decision-maker and beneficiary (the-reader.md rule 5–6);
   then return to plot — a repeatable structural home for the sarcasm/care two-step.
7. **Script the TikTok/Shorts cutdown's first 3 seconds as its own artefact before touching the edit.**
   Per TikTok's own guidance the whole content proposition must land there — write it standalone, then
   build backward, rather than trimming the long-form open to size.
8. **Use one text overlay per beat on short-form cuts, never stacked.** BadCode's actual audience is a
   documented sound-off, scroll-first cohort (the-reader.md: Facebook/YouTube/TikTok, not the comic
   site) — a concrete production rule with a citation behind it, not a stylistic guess.
9. **Run Curtis's two-track method (comic/video plus D&B track) deliberately, checked against
   Tonkin's critique every time.** A visual "riff" layer is fine as texture, but if it ever lets the
   beneficiary go ambiguous it has crossed into the failure mode his critics name — the-reader.md's
   rule 6 is the tripwire.
10. **Cut information density between drafts, not up, Eameses-style.** Each episode script should end
    with fewer named facts/threads than its first draft, tested by whether a first-timer and a
    canon-literate reader land the same feeling — not the same fact count.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does the script state the hope/re-aim beat as an explicit line, or only imply it via tone?
2. Can every "and then" be replaced with "therefore" or "but" without breaking the logic?
3. Is there exactly one reserved beat for movement/animation, or has motion been spent everywhere and
   so nowhere?
4. Does the first 60 seconds contain the whole promise — hook, stakes, tone — before scene-setting?
5. Is there a scheduled re-engagement beat around the three-minute mark, not left to momentum?
6. If two tracks run at once (narration plus a visual "riff"), does the riff still leave the
   beneficiary/decision-maker legible?
7. Does the narrator alternate action-beat → bigger-thought → back-to-action, or run straight through
   one register?
8. Is the short-form cutdown's first three seconds a self-contained promise, or a trimmed slice of the
   long-form open?
9. Does the short-form cut use one text overlay per beat, legible sound-off, rather than stacking?
10. If a signal-interruption device is used, is it rare enough (once per release) to still read as a
    shock rather than a tic?
11. Has information density gone *down* between drafts, tested against a first-timer landing the same
    feeling as a canon-literate reader?
12. Does the automation-fear beat name a specific chooser and beneficiary in the same piece
    (the-reader.md rule 6), rather than raising the temperature unaimed?
13. Were retention "rules" applied here checked against a named practitioner's account, or only an
    unsourced marketing-blog framework?
14. Does the piece avoid restating an opposing claim to knock it down — dread from build-up, not
    repetition?
