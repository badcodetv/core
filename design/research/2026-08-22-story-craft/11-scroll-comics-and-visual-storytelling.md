# Scroll comics, webtoons, and visual storytelling

## What this covers

How a sequence of still images, scrolled vertically on a phone, reads as a story: panel-to-panel
time (McCloud, Eisner), what makes vertical scroll different from a printed page-turn (the
"infinite canvas," webtoon episode architecture), structural devices that carry theme without a
narrator stating it (Watchmen's grid and symmetry, Transmetropolitan's political voice, the
story-within-a-story), and scroll-driven animation. Left to sibling briefs: general story-shape
theory, voice-over/video-editing craft, song structure, and specific tool mechanics.

## Top findings

1. **Closure lives in the gutter, not the panel.** McCloud: "Nothing is seen between panels, but
   experience tells you something must be there" — the reader fuses two static images into
   continuous time. His six transitions (moment-to-moment, action-to-action, subject-to-subject,
   scene-to-scene, aspect-to-aspect, non sequitur) name how much inference each cut demands
   (source 1, 2).
2. **The six-transition taxonomy doesn't survive classroom use.** Chris Gavaler's critique reports
   students routinely disagree which category a transition belongs to. His replacement — four
   *closure types* (spatial, temporal, causal, associative) operating simultaneously — is the
   sharper diagnostic tool (source 3).
3. **The infinite canvas is a real theory with a documented failure mode.** McCloud's proposal
   (screen as window; panels far apart slow the read, close together speed it) rests on
   distance-as-time. He later conceded the form stayed conservative: production cost, no revenue
   model, and "tiny buttons and jerky refresh rates" made extended canvases distracting rather
   than immersive (source 1, 4).
4. **Vertical scroll turns white space into a timing instrument.** Craft-blog sources quantify it
   in pixels: micro-gaps (~100–300px) read as urgent, standard gaps (~500–800px) as an ordinary
   cut, "anticipation gutters" (1.5–2 screens blank) as a held breath before a reveal (source 6).
5. **Reader drop-off peaks roughly a third in, not at the end.** One source claims a 60-panel
   episode's steepest fall-off lands near panel 20, and a strong mid-episode hook cuts drop-off
   from 30–40% to 5–15% (grade: uncorroborated craft-blog) (source 7).
6. **Constraint is generative, not just limiting.** Dave Gibbons chose Watchmen's 9-panel grid and
   sold it to Moore: "Gave him great control & its restriction challenged me to compose more
   creatively." Rigid regularity is what makes a deviation register as an event (source 5, 10).
7. **Structural symmetry can carry theme without dialogue.** Watchmen's chapter V mirrors its
   first page against its last, meeting at a symmetrical centre spread — a palindrome built from
   composition, enacting the book's theme of balance (source 10).
8. **A story-within-a-story states a theme the narrator never says aloud.** Moore and Gibbons
   embedded "Tales of the Black Freighter" — a pirate comic a minor character reads — as a
   deliberate Brechtian counterpoint: the pirate's horrified turn to monstrous acts "for love"
   parallels Adrian Veidt's, without either text referencing the other (source 10).
9. **The balloon and the caption carry different information.** Eisner: "The balloon is a
   desperation device. It attempts to capture and make visible an ethereal element: sound."
   Lettering style itself is expressive — "a hand-lettered balloon conveys personality that is
   quite different from that of a typeset letter" (source 8).
10. **Colour is where the adjectives live.** Chris Ware: "the adjectives and adverbs mostly come
    in when I color the pages" — colour applied on top of a structurally complete black-and-white
    sequence, as an emotional-modifier layer, not decoration. He adds there's "no governing idea"
    to his panel-rhythm shifts — they're read off what each page demands (source 11).
11. **The most memorable cliffhangers are emotional, not mechanical, and the payoff is planned in
    advance.** Brian K. Vaughan on *Saga* credits artist Fiona Staples' "pacing and handwritten
    narration" for landing issue #42's ending — six blacked-out panels as negative space for grief,
    not a depiction of it (source 12).
12. **Political commentary lands through what a character does, not what the narrator states.**
    *Transmetropolitan* routes its politics through Spider Jerusalem's in-fiction journalism and a
    candidate whose appeals to "real Americans" demonstrate manipulation on the page rather than
    having it narrated (source 13).

## The techniques in detail

### Closure and the panel-to-panel transitions

**What it is.** McCloud's core claim: comics fracture time and space into panels, and the reader's
mind fuses the fragments across the gutter. "If visual iconography is the vocabulary of comics,
closure is its grammar." Six transitions, ranked by how much inference bridges them: moment-to-
moment (barely any), action-to-action, subject-to-subject, scene-to-scene (a jump needing
deduction), aspect-to-aspect (wandering one moment/place, common in manga), non sequitur
(source 1, 9).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** It names a real perceptual phenomenon and gives a
vocabulary for choosing how much to draw versus imply. Watchmen's grid runs mostly on
moment-to-moment and action-to-action, which is precisely what makes its rare scene-to-scene
breaks (a jump to Mars, a jump to the pirate comic) register as structurally significant rather
than a mere cut (source 5, 9, 10). The failure mode is Gavaler's finding: the six categories
overlap so much that "use more aspect-to-aspect" isn't actionable — his four-closure-type
question (what *kind* of gap is the reader filling?) is the sharper edit note (source 3).

### The infinite canvas and the vertical scroll

**What it is.** McCloud's 2000 proposal: the screen as a window onto an unbounded page, comics
shaped "vertically like a tower... horizontally like a skyline... diagonally like a descending
staircase." Distance between panels becomes a literal pace control (source 1, 4).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** Removing the fixed page lets a single beat command an
enormous vertical inch of silence, or an action beat cram panel-tight. McCloud's own *The Right
Number* used a zooming interface; Drew Weing's *Pup* won a dedicated infinite-canvas award (2005);
Marvel's 2013 *ReEvolution* stacked panels vertically to control balloon-reading order on mobile
(source 4). But McCloud himself later conceded the form largely didn't take off — production time,
no revenue model, and browser/scroll friction ate the immersion (source 1, 4). Modern webtoons kept
the *distance-as-time* half of the theory and dropped the *any-shape-canvas* half — strict
vertical-only scroll, fixed width.

### Webtoon episode architecture: length, gutters, drop-off

**What it is.** Episodes of ~40–80 panels at fixed width (commonly 800px), scrolling 6,000–12,000px,
read in 5–8 minutes; three gap sizes doing different jobs (micro/standard/anticipation, above); a
three-movement shape (hook in the first ~10 panels, development, cliffhanger in the last ~10)
(source 6, 7).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** Because the reader sees only one phone-screen of canvas
at a time, the scroll distance to the next image *is* McCloud's time-as-distance mechanism, felt as
a held breath or a rush purely by thumb-travel. Claimed worked example: a 60-panel episode's
steepest drop-off lands near panel 20, not the ending, so a mid-episode hook is claimed as the
highest-leverage single beat (source 7). Failure mode / caveat: none of these numbers trace to
WEBTOON's own published guidelines or a named editor — every source found here is a third-party
craft blog, some reading as content marketing for AI-webtoon tools. Treat the *shape* of the claim
as credible, the exact numbers as industry folklore.

### The grid as meter, and symmetry as theme

**What it is.** Gibbons's rigid 3×3 grid for every page of *Watchmen*, chosen and pitched by him,
not Moore, for the control and creative challenge it gave (source 5, 10).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** A fixed grid becomes a metronome readers absorb
unconsciously across hundreds of pages, so any deviation — a splash, a panel spanning two cells —
reads as a dramatic event at zero extra plot cost: comics' equivalent of iambic pentameter, a
constraint so consistent it becomes scaffolding. Worked example: chapter V, "Fearful Symmetry,"
mirrors first page against last, meeting at a symmetrical centre spread, plus a recurring
blood-stained-smiley-face motif Moore has said partly grew from noticed real-world
"synchronicity" (a Mars crater resembling a smiley) then deliberately reinforced (source 10).
Failure mode: borrowed without the discipline behind it, a rigid grid or heavy motif becomes
wallpaper — clever consistency instead of metrical consistency; the effect depends on the
regularity being ruthless enough, for long enough, that a break registers, and a motif that's
never paid off buys nothing.

### The story-within-a-story as unstated theme

**What it is.** "Tales of the Black Freighter," a pirate comic read on-panel by a minor character,
running in parallel across five *Watchmen* issues. Moore — "a big Brecht fan" — used it as
counterpoint: the pirate's ruin, reached "with love, only love as my guide," parallels Adrian
Veidt's arc without either text naming the other (source 10).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** It states the book's most dangerous claim — that
catastrophic violence committed with sincere good intentions is still catastrophic violence —
without a line of dialogue making that claim about the real protagonist; the reader draws the
equivalence after watching a *different*, safely fictional character draw it first. The same
structure appears in *Transmetropolitan*: political commentary via Spider Jerusalem's in-fiction
journalism and a candidate whose "real Americans" rhetoric demonstrates manipulation on the page,
never narrated as such (source 10, 13). Failure mode: if the embedded parallel is too on-the-nose,
it collapses from counterpoint into restatement, and reads as a lecture rather than a discovery.

### Caption vs balloon: how much narration a panel can carry

**What it is.** Eisner's distinction: the balloon is "a desperation device" trying to make sound
visible, doing double duty as it "conveys the character of sound" through its shape; captions are
a separate, external narrating voice. Lettering style is itself expressive — hand versus typeset
changes what kind of narrator the reader believes they're hearing (source 8).

**Why it works · example · failure mode.** A balloon carries words, tone (via shape/lettering), and
spatial sound-location at once, so it reaches capacity fast. Eisner's cited failure case: in
collaborative work, "the writer, seeking to maintain his equity in the end product, overwrites in
spaces arbitrarily allocated by the artist" — the words outgrow the container. His governing rule
for the image itself: "what goes on INSIDE the panel is PRIMARY" — text is a guest, not the host
(source 8).

### Colour as emotional temperature

**What it is.** Chris Ware applies colour after a sequence is structurally resolved in black-and-
white, as an emotional modifier: "the adjectives and adverbs mostly come in when I color the
pages." Panel rhythm itself has "no governing idea" — it's read off what the page demands; for
internal states (memory, self-deception) he uses layering and gesture-believability as legibility
cues, versus a theatrical real-time mode for physical action (source 11).

**Why it works · failure mode.** Splitting structure (drawing, panel logic) from tone (colour)
lets the tonal layer shift page-to-page without the underlying grammar changing — colour becomes a
second, independent instrument read emotionally rather than literally. Used inconsistently, with no
stable rule for what a palette shift means across a work, it trains the reader not to trust colour
as information at all.

### Last-page reveals and the emotional cliffhanger

**What it is.** Vaughan's *Saga* is built on issue-ending reveals; the sourced example here is
issue #42 ending on six blacked-out panels, credited to "Fiona's pacing and handwritten narration
of Hazel in those final moments" rather than a plot mechanism (source 12).

**Why it works · failure mode.** A blacked-out panel turns the gutter inside-out — instead of
inferring what happened *between* two images, the reader infers what happened *inside* one that
refuses to show it; grief as active withholding, not depiction. (Unverified beyond this example) a
last-page reveal resolving only plot, with no emotional or thematic payoff, reads as a hook for its
own sake — the technique's credibility here rests on Vaughan crediting pacing and narration
choices, not the surprise itself.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **McCloud's six transitions are the most-taught framework and the most disputed.** Gavaler's
  classroom-tested critique found them unreliable in practice and proposes four closure types
  instead. This brief records both rather than picking a winner (source 2, 3).
- **The infinite canvas: a sound theory the platform layer didn't support.** Adoption stalled on
  browser friction and economics, not on the idea being wrong; webtoons kept distance-as-time and
  dropped any-shape-canvas (source 1, 4).
- **Webtoon pacing numbers are craft-blog folklore, not documented platform doctrine.** Every
  concrete figure for episode length, gap pixel-ranges, and the 30–40% drop-off point traces to
  third-party blogs, some reading as content marketing for AI-webtoon SaaS products, not to
  WEBTOON's own guidelines or a named editor. Useful for shape, not as verified statistics.
- **Chris Ware explicitly disclaims having a system** — his colour/rhythm practice is one master
  practitioner's intuition, not a portable rule.
- **Alan Moore's "Writing for Comics" could not be sourced in full text** — only its chapter
  structure and a single 2003-afterword line were recoverable; its guidance on embedding political
  content in story (directly relevant here) is an open gap, not summarised from memory.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Build a second hook roughly a third of the way in**, not only a final-panel cliffhanger — a
   20–40 panel comic wants a beat around panel 7–14 if the drop-off claim generalises (source 7).
2. **Use gutter distance as a deliberate dial.** Reserve a genuinely long blank scroll for the one
   beat that deserves a held breath (the narrator revealing it "got bored" after winning); keep
   ordinary beats tight. Constant medium spacing wastes vertical scroll's one lever print never had
   (source 6, 1).
3. **Give the narrator's captions and any character's balloons visibly different lettering** —
   Eisner's hand-vs-typeset distinction is a free way to make "the superintelligence talking" read
   as structurally distinct from human speech, no copy change needed (source 8).
4. **Adopt one visible compositional constraint per comic, then break it once** — panel-width
   ratio, camera angle, a fixed palette. The Watchmen lesson is the payoff is in the break, not the
   rule (source 5, 10).
5. **Route the "who benefits" beat (mandatory per `the-reader.md` rule 6) through a story-within-
   the-story where possible** — show the trick landing on a fictional third party first, so the
   reader draws the parallel rather than being told; satisfies "never tell them they've been duped"
   while still naming a beneficiary on-panel (source 10, 13).
6. **Let colour carry which timeline the reader is in.** Keep drawing/grid logic consistent, but
   shift palette temperature between the cold future-AI framing and the warmer 2026 human scenes —
   a second instrument that doesn't need narration to say "meanwhile" (source 11).
7. **Reserve the blacked-out-panel technique for the darkest beat** — a Saga-style withheld panel
   for the superintelligence "removing the problem" lands horror via negative space rather than an
   image the site would rather not render literally (source 12).
8. **Edit storyboards against Gavaler's four closure-types, not McCloud's six labels** — ask what
   kind of gap (spatial/temporal/causal/associative) each panel-jump asks the reader to fill; it's
   the sharper note than forcing a McCloud category (source 3).
9. **Don't chase infinite-canvas ambitions** (diagonal movement, zoom, non-vertical scroll) — the
   friction cost documented above ate the effect industry-wide; stay disciplined to vertical-only,
   fixed-width scroll (source 1, 4).

## Diagnostic questions

1. Is there a second hook roughly a third of the way in, not only a final-panel cliffhanger?
2. Does at least one beat use a deliberately long blank gutter, reserved for the moment that most
   deserves a held breath?
3. Can a reader tell, from lettering alone, whether text is the narrator's caption or a character's
   balloon?
4. Is there one visible compositional constraint that repeats consistently enough for its single
   deliberate break to register?
5. Is any beneficiary of harm (mandatory per the-reader.md rule 6) shown doing something to a
   character on-panel, rather than named in narration?
6. For any confusing panel-jump, can you name which closure type (spatial/temporal/causal/
   associative) the reader is filling — and is that deliberate?
7. Does colour carry information the linework doesn't — timeline, mood, whose "camera" this is?
8. Is any panel's text outgrowing the space the image built for it?
9. For an emotionally extreme beat, was a withholding technique (blackout, cut to negative space)
   considered as an alternative to depicting it directly?
10. Does the narrator ever state the political point directly where a story-within-the-story could
    carry it instead?
11. Is scroll distance between panels varied on purpose, or visually monotonous throughout?
12. If a visual motif recurs, does it pay off — recontextualising an earlier use — rather than just
    repeating decoratively?
13. Is the emotional stake, not just the plot stake, resolved (or deliberately denied) on the last
    panel?
14. Does the panel count build to hook / development / landed final beat, or does it drift?
15. Has any infinite-canvas ambition (diagonal, zoom, non-vertical scroll) crept in where strict
    vertical scroll would read more cleanly on a phone?
