# Openings: Hooks, Cold Opens and the First Minute

## What this covers

How a piece earns the right to keep going, in the first slice of its runtime, across prose,
screen, comics, short-form video and audio: first-page rejection patterns, in medias res and its
overuse, the TV cold open's history, Snyder's Opening/Closing Image and "promise of the premise",
Sanderson's "Promise", Gardner's "fictional dream", the YouTube/short-form retention evidence (and
the marketing noise around it), pattern interrupts and re-hooks, Webtoon's first-three-episodes
doctrine, the comics splash page, and how podcasts (Ira Glass) and narrated explainers
(Kurzgesagt) open. Endings go to sibling brief 06; the narrated-video form's full mechanics go to
brief 12 — this stops at the first 10% of any piece.

## Top findings

1. **The opening's job is a promise, not a hook.** Sanderson frames the first chapter as
   establishing what kind of experience the reader has bought into — tone, genre, stakes, who
   matters — so they can decide whether they want it. "Hook" implies a trick; "promise" implies a
   contract you intend to keep (brandonsanderson.com).
2. **In medias res is usually misapplied by starting too late, not too early.** The common failure
   is action with no one to care about and no stakes — "walking into a cinema halfway through the
   film." *Lord of the Flies* opens on the boys' *reaction* to the crash, not the crash — action
   plus instant characterisation (helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com).
3. **Gardner's "fictional dream" gives a concrete failure test.** Fiction induces a continuous
   mental image; a jarring word choice, a logic slip, a stop-and-look-up word "wakes" the reader —
   most fragile in the opening lines, before the dream has taken hold (literarylab.blogspot.com,
   quoting Gardner directly).
4. **Snyder's Opening Image and Closing Image are a paired bet.** Page 1 sets tone and the
   "before" state; the Closing Image must visibly answer it — *The Matrix*'s blinking cursor
   becomes Neo in flight (studiobinder.com).
5. **"Promise of the premise" is a later beat, not the opener.** In Snyder's structure it's "Fun
   and Games" (~25–50% through) — where the logline's concept gets to be fun. Conflating this with
   the opening image causes writers to cram spectacle into page 1 the story hasn't earned
   (mikebattle.com).
6. **The cold open smuggles exposition past a channel-flipping audience.** *Hill Street Blues*
   popularised the pre-credit teaser — a roll-call briefing planting every subplot thread — as
   in-world ritual, not lecture (Wikipedia, quoting Steven Johnson and Alan Sepinwall).
7. **A video's first ~30 seconds sets the retention ceiling** — but the specific percentages
   quoted across creator blogs ("18% higher retention," a "Stanford 8–15s study") have no
   traceable citation; a more careful guide, fetched directly, dropped the Stanford claim and cited
   only YouTube's own Creator Academy (teleprompter.com — see Disagreements).
8. **Pattern interrupts break prediction; re-hooks redeploy the opening hook mid-piece.** The
   brain predicts what's coming to save energy; an unpredicted change resets that loop — every
   3–5s for fast content, 7–10s for slower formats (creatorscope.io).
9. **Webtoon treats its first three episodes as a demo, not a rough draft.** The platform prompts
   readers to follow at episode three, so creators post all three at launch, polished to the
   *best* current standard ("the demo doesn't need the whole game, but it needs your best" —
   absolutelyskint.com).
10. **Ira Glass's "anecdote and moment of reflection" starts in the opening minutes.** The
    anecdote buys attention before the audience knows why to care; the reflection ("here's what it
    made me think") converts that into investment: "you have to be very tactical about making
    people want to listen for even two minutes... by being funny" (dumbofeather.com;
    natelistrom.com).
11. **Kurzgesagt opens on a question because a question is an open loop.** "What would happen if
    you had a black hole in your pocket?" turns a topic into a mystery the viewer wants resolved —
    cheaper and less preachy than a thesis (animationexplainers.com).

## The techniques in detail

### Gardner's fictional dream and the agent's first page

**What it is.** Gardner's claim, from *The Art of Fiction*, that good fiction produces a continuous
mental image, and the writer's job at sentence level is to protect it. **Why it works.** A reader
dreaming the story isn't evaluating your prose; one who's been woken — by a slip, an
inconsistency, a word they stop to look up — starts reading *you* instead of the story. Classic
breakage cases (literarylab.blogspot.com): tense inconsistency, dictionary-trip vocabulary,
physical details that don't add up. **Failure mode.** Writers chasing a "strong hook" often break
the dream on purpose — a shock image, a showy sentence, direct reader-address — in the very
sentence meant to draw the reader in, before any trust has accumulated to survive it.

### In medias res: correct use and its overuse

**What it is.** Starting "in the middle of things" instead of with orienting exposition. **Why it
works when it works.** It converts absent context into a short curiosity gap the reader tolerates
because they're getting something — action, voice, stakes — in exchange. *Lord of the Flies*
opens on the boys already reacting to being marooned, delivering characterisation and stakes at
once. **Failure mode**, per both Helping Writers Become Authors and Fawkes: assuming action alone
is a hook, so the opening becomes a blur of movement with no one to root for — forcing a clumsy
mid-scene info-dump or flashback later, slower than just starting earlier would have been.

### Sanderson's Promise, and the first chapter's teaching job

**What it is.** The first chapter teaches genre, tone and stakes conventions specific to *this*
book, calibrating expectations before a single event has finished happening. Epic fantasy's stock
opening (a young person looking up at the sun) teaches "this is that kind of story" in one image.
**Failure mode.** A tonally inconsistent opening — jokes in chapter 1, grimdark violence in
chapter 3 with no signal — reads as a broken promise, and a reader who feels lied to by page 3
stops extending trust.

### Snyder's bookend, and the separate "promise of the premise"

**What it is.** Two beats often conflated: the Opening/Closing Image bookend (tone and "before"
state, then its visible transformation), and the "promise of the premise" — what the logline sold
the audience — delivered in "Fun and Games," roughly a quarter to half of the way through, not
page 1. *The Matrix*'s cursor-to-flight bookend is the worked example. **Failure mode.** Writers
under-deliver spectacle on page 1 (confusing it with Opening Image) or over-deliver it too early
from anxiety the audience will leave — both from not knowing these are separate beats.

### The TV cold open

**What it is.** A pre-titles scene starting the story before the audience has committed via the
theme song. *Hill Street Blues* (1981) popularised it: a 7am roll-call briefing planting that
episode's threads across an ensemble. **Why it works.** It delivers plot-relevant exposition
disguised as in-world ritual, so the audience absorbs setup without feeling lectured. **Failure
mode.** A cold open that's just an unrelated cool scene burns patience without doing the
threading work the device exists for.

### YouTube retention: the first 15–30 seconds, and re-hooks

**What it is.** The claim that a video's opening stretch (8–15s, or "the first 30 seconds," cited
inconsistently) disproportionately sets the retention curve — a steep early drop as casual
browsers self-select out, then a shallower decline. **Why it's plausible.** Viewers arrive at zero
invested cost, so the opening must answer "why keep watching" almost immediately. **The evidence
problem.** Every specific percentage found (a "Stanford" study, "18% higher retention") appeared
only on SEO/creator-tool blogs with no citation trail; a more careful guide, fetched directly,
dropped the Stanford claim and cited only YouTube's own Creator Academy in general terms.
**Re-hooks** redeploy the opening hook's trick mid-video — a verbal tease ("here's the part almost
nobody edits for") — rather than trusting the opener to carry the whole runtime.

### The leaked MrBeast document

**What it is.** A ~37-page document, reportedly internal to the MrBeast team, leaked and analysed
by multiple outlets (authenticity unconfirmed). Its framework: "hook people at the start,
transition them to an amazing story they're invested in, have no dull moments, then a satisfying
payoff." It treats the first minute as maximum viewer loss and the 3–6 minute stretch as the next
critical zone, needing fast pacing and scene changes. **Treat critically.** Outlets covering it
agree on the framework, but it's a production playbook encoding one team's beliefs — strong
practitioner evidence, not proof of a general mechanism.

### Pattern interrupts and the prediction mechanism

**What it is.** Any sudden, unanticipated change — a cut, a zoom, an on-screen graphic, a tone
shift — inserted to reset drifting attention. **Why it works.** The brain predicts what's coming
to save energy; once it thinks it knows, engagement drops, and an unpredicted change forces
re-evaluation. **Worked guidance.** 3–5s intervals for fast content, 7–10s for slower/educational,
~4–6 interrupts across a 30-second Reel (creatorscope.io). **Failure mode.** Mistaking randomness
for interruption — purposeless cuts read as chaotic rather than re-engaging.

### Short-form video's first 1–3 seconds

**What it is.** The claim that most short-form viewers decide whether to keep watching within
three seconds, and platforms read an early scroll-past as a negative signal. **The evidence
problem, again.** Figures in circulation ("71% decide within 3 seconds," "above 65% three-second
retention") trace, on inspection, to a single blog's self-reported analysis of "creator accounts
ranging from 5,000 to 500,000 followers," not a published study (opus.pro, on fetch) — treat as
pattern-matching, not measured fact.

### Webtoon and serial fiction: the first three episodes

**What it is.** Webtoon prompts a reader to follow at episode three, so creators are advised to
launch with all three ready, polished past "early work" standard — a demo of the finished quality
bar, not a rough draft the story grows into. **Failure mode.** Treating episode 1 as a placeholder
("it gets better later") loses the reader before the improvement arrives.

### The comics splash page

**What it is.** Eisner's term (per search-level synopsis of *Comics and Sequential Art*) for an
opening page treated as one large image rather than a panel grid — the whole page as establishing
frame. **Caveat.** No primary passage confirming Eisner's exact wording could be fetched; flagged
as unverified beyond the search synopsis, though the underlying principle — composition, not just
content, does opening work — is consistent with Sanderson's and Snyder's arguments for other media.

### Podcasts: Ira Glass's anecdote and moment of reflection

**What it is.** Glass's structural unit: the anecdote (causally linked events) and the moment of
reflection (an explicit "here's what it made me think"). **Why it works at the opening.** The
anecdote's forward motion buys the first minutes of attention before the audience knows why to
care; the reflection converts borrowed attention into investment. **Failure mode.** Reflection
before events have happened reads as lecture; events with no reflection read as pointless.

### Kurzgesagt: opening with the question

**What it is.** Framing the whole subject as a specific question rather than a thesis statement.
**Why it works.** A question is an open loop the brain wants closed; a stated thesis has already
given away the ending. **Failure mode**, by inference: a question too abstract or too easily
guessed doesn't generate the same pull.

## Comparison: what a strong opening does at 1%, 5%, 10%, by medium

| Medium | First ~1% | First ~5% | First ~10% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prose novel/chapter | First line keeps the fictional dream intact; voice signalled | Protagonist, want and tone/genre are namable (Promise) | Inciting incident landed or clearly imminent |
| Screenplay/film | Opening Image on screen (page 1) | World and protagonist's "before" state fully legible | Catalyst has hit or is imminent (~page 10–12 of 110) |
| TV episode | Cold open begins mid-scene, no titles yet | Cold open resolves; episode's threads planted | A-plot problem stated post-credits |
| Narrated video (3–5 min) | Hook line/question lands (~8–15s, contested figures) | Viewer has decided to stay; value proposition answered | First pattern interrupt/re-hook fires |
| Short-form video (15–60s) | First frame + verbal hook together (0–3s) | First visual proof/payoff tease | First pattern interrupt (every 3–10s) |
| Scroll/webcomic page | Splash panel sets tone and scale | First caption/dialogue confirms voice and stakes | First page-turn reveal or reaction beat |
| Serialised webtoon/arc | Episode 1 cover art and opening panel | Episode 1's hook scene | Episode 3's cliffhanger (the follow-gate) |
| Podcast segment | First words of the anecdote | Anecdote's stakes are clear | First hint of the reflection to come |
| D&B track | Intro hits (first bar) | Vocal hook or lyric line arrives | First drop lands |

## Disagreements and caveats

- **In medias res: two camps, same word.** Craft blogs (HWBA, Fawkes) say the technique is sound
  and execution is the problem; some agent-facing sources treat the technique itself as overused —
  both describe the same failure (context withheld too long) from different ends.
- **The biggest weak spot here is quantitative video-retention claims.** Every specific percentage
  or named study about YouTube's "first 15 seconds" or TikTok's "first 3 seconds" traced to
  SEO/creator-tool content with no verifiable citation — including a "Stanford study" that
  vanished when a more careful version of the same claim was fetched directly. Treat every number
  in this brief's video sections as listicle-grade: a real, plausible pattern, unreliable size.
- **The MrBeast document is practitioner evidence with unconfirmed provenance.** Outlets agree on
  content; none has verified it's genuine internal material — worth taking as "what a
  retention-optimised operation believes," not measured fact.
- **Eisner's splash-page framing couldn't be source-verified here** — credible and consistent with
  other media's craft consensus, but only a search-engine synopsis, not a fetched text.
- **Le Guin's specific position on openings is a gap.** *Steering the Craft* almost certainly
  covers beginnings, but no fetched source surfaced her exact argument — don't cite Le Guin on
  openings from this brief without going to the book directly.
- **"Promise of the premise" is widely mis-taught as synonymous with "the opening."** Snyder's own
  structure places it a quarter to half through; several secondary sources conflate the two, which
  would mislead anyone using this brief to plan pacing.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Cold-open every narrated video on the human, not the narrator.** Per the-reader.md's
   hero-not-pupil rule, open mid-scene on the wronged character already in the situation — Hill
   Street Blues's trick of threading exposition through in-scene action keeps the narrator from
   sounding like it's lecturing before it's earned trust.
2. **Treat the narrator's first line as the Promise, and keep it.** A mid-video swing from dry
   cosmic deadpan into straight sincerity, unsignalled, reads as a broken promise, not a register
   shift.
3. **Separate "opening image" from "promise of the premise."** GPOM's opening image (the AI alone
   on an empty planet) sets tone; the political mechanism made vivid belongs a quarter to a third
   in, as its own beat — not crammed into scene 1.
4. **Open on a question, not a thesis, when explaining a mechanism.** Ask the question the story
   answers; don't state the moral — a closer fit for a lecture-allergic reader.
5. **Build the re-hook into the drop.** Plan the first drop to land at the video's first re-hook
   point (~20–40s for a 3–5 minute piece) rather than treating music and re-hook timing as
   separate problems.
6. **Give the scroll comic's first panel splash-page treatment.** Tone, scale and "before" state
   should read from composition alone before any caption adds narration.
7. **Apply "first three episodes are the demo" to EP1 of any serialised release.** If a story's
   opening comic + track pair is the reader's only evidence of the arc's quality, polish it past
   the standard later instalments will hit.
8. **Never let a raised automation-fear cold open go unaimed.** Rule 6 of the-reader.md and this
   brief's cold-open findings combine directly: if a video opens on a human casualty of
   automation, the same piece must put a beneficiary on-panel before it ends.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does the first panel/frame/line establish protagonist, tone and stakes without a narration
   dump?
2. Is the opening specific and grounded (a named mechanism, a real-feeling institution) rather
   than abstract scene-setting?
3. If opening in medias res, does the reader know who to root for in the same beat that gives them
   action?
4. Does the piece answer "why keep watching/reading" within its own first ~10–15%, without the
   answer being a stated thesis?
5. Does the wronged human appear in scene in the opening, rather than the narrator explaining the
   wrong directly?
6. Does the narrator's first line diagnose the system rather than accuse the viewer of being
   fooled?
7. Is there a planned re-hook or pattern interrupt before attention would plausibly drift
   (~20–40s into a narrated video), coinciding with a track beat?
8. Does the closing image/beat visibly rhyme with and transform the opening image?
9. For a serialised release, is the first instalment held to the same polish bar as later ones?
10. Does the opener commit to a specific tone that the rest of the piece actually honours, without
    an unsignalled whiplash?
11. If the cold open raises automation/AI anxiety on a human casualty, is a beneficiary named
    on-panel somewhere in the same piece?
12. Would a stranger, shown only the first 10% of this piece, say correctly what kind of story it
    is and who it's about?
