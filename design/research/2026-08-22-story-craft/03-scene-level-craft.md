# Scene-Level Craft: How a Scene Earns Its Place

## What this covers

The unit below the act and above the sentence: what a scene needs so it feels worth the reader's
time, and what a line needs for the same reason. Covers scene/sequel theory (Swain, Butcher), the
"does it turn" tests (Story Grid, McKee), causality-not-sequence structuring (Parker & Stone,
Sanderson), prose- and dialogue-level craft (Palahniuk, Le Guin, Mamet, Sorkin, subtext vs
on-the-nose), and exposition devices (Snyder's "pope in the pool," Mamet's expository traps,
Arrival's colonel scene). Leaves act/sequence-level structure to sibling brief 01 and
suspense/tension mechanics to sibling brief 04 — touched here only where they operate inside a
single scene's turn.

## Top findings

1. **A scene is Goal → Conflict → Disaster, not Goal → Conflict → Resolution.** Dwight Swain
   (*Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965) ends scenes on an unanticipated setback, because a
   solved problem stops the reader turning the page and an unsolved one doesn't.
2. **Every scene must turn a value from one charge to its opposite.** Robert McKee's test: is the
   value at stake (love/hate, freedom/slavery) at a different charge at the end than the start? If
   not, it's exposition wearing a scene's clothes.
3. **The gap between expectation and result is where a scene's energy lives.** McKee: a character
   acts expecting one outcome, the world answers differently, and the reader's attention snaps into
   that gap — engineer the gap, don't deliver information.
4. **Join beats with "but" and "therefore," never "and then."** Trey Parker and Matt Stone's NYU
   rule: "and then" makes a list, not a story, because nothing was caused by anything else.
5. **A scene's outcome is one of four answers — mostly avoid the boring one.** Jim Butcher's
   Yes / Yes-but / No / No-and-furthermore makes Swain's "disaster" explicit; Brandon Sanderson's
   yes-but/no-and try-fail cycle is the same mechanism, reached independently from the
   discovery-writer's side.
6. **A sequel exists so the reader feels something before being asked to act again.** Swain and
   Butcher both sequence the aftermath as Reaction (raw emotion) → Dilemma (logical weighing) →
   Decision (new goal) — in that fixed order, because reason-before-feeling reads as sociopathic.
7. **Kill the thought verbs; unpack the thought into what a camera could see.** Chuck Palahniuk's
   "Nuts and Bolts" rule: ban "thinks/knows/realizes/wants/loves" and replace the abstraction with
   the physical behaviour a reader can infer it from.
8. **Nothing in a well-made scene is wasted.** George Saunders' "Ruthless Efficiency Principle"
   (*A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*): a story is "a system for the transfer of energy," and a detail
   that looks clumsy on a first pass is usually doing structural work.
9. **Rhythm is not decoration, it's the meaning.** Ursula K. Le Guin (*Steering the Craft*):
   "Style is a very simple matter; it is all rhythm. Once you get that, you can't use the wrong
   words."
10. **Ask three questions of every scene: who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, why
    now.** David Mamet's memo to the writers of *The Unit* — a scene that bores the writer reading
    it will bore the audience watching it.
11. **The two worst expository sins: two characters discussing a third, and "as you know."** Also
    Mamet — both make a scene about information instead of want-versus-obstacle.
12. **Disguise exposition as argument, or hide it inside something irresistible to watch.** Blake
    Snyder's "pope in the pool" and Arrival's colonel scene are the same move from opposite
    directions: never let information stand alone with nothing else at stake.

## The techniques in detail

### Swain's scene: Goal, Conflict, Disaster

Swain's scene runs on a **goal** (specific, concrete, established early), **conflict** (an
opposing force that actively blocks it and escalates), and **disaster** — an unanticipated setback,
not a resolution — read as "yes, but…" (goal met, new problem attached) or "no, and…" (goal missed,
things worsen). Swain allows exceptions but treats disaster as default. It works because the goal
gives the reader something to want, conflict reveals how much the character wants it, and disaster
refuses to let the question close — which is what keeps pages turning. (September C. Fawkes'
summary, cross-checked against Wikipedia's "Scene and sequel" entry.) Any rescue-attempt scene
illustrates it: free X (goal), guards intervene (conflict), X is freed but the building's now on
fire (disaster). **Failure mode:** stopping at "goal → conflict → resolution" produces a
technically complete but flat scene — the tension simply evaporates instead of converting into new
story material.

### Swain/Butcher's sequel, and Butcher's four-way scene answer

A sequel is the connective tissue between scenes: **Reaction** (the emotional hit) → **Dilemma**
(weighing options) → **Decision** (the choice that becomes the next goal) — fixed order, because it
mirrors how people actually process a setback. Jim Butcher's own LiveJournal essays — which he says
describe how he's written every novel — rename the same shape Emotion → Reason/Anticipation →
Choice, and add the scene-side taxonomy: **Yes** (flat success), **Yes, but** (success plus
complication), **No** (flat failure, use sparingly), or **No, and furthermore** (failure plus a
worse problem — Butcher's favourite for sustaining tension). Genre tunes the sequel's balance:
Fawkes' summary of Swain notes romance leans into Reaction, mystery into Dilemma, action compresses
Decision with the other two barely dramatised. **Worked example:** after a bomb-disposal failure
that cuts the power, a sequel shows fear (reaction), the weighing of evacuate-vs-find-the-fuse-box
(dilemma), and the choice of the fuse box, which becomes the next scene's goal. **Failure mode:**
cutting straight from disaster to the next scene's action, no sequel at all, reads as a character
who doesn't feel consequences — Butcher: sequels are "what really make or break books."

### Story Grid's Five Commandments and the "must turn" test

Story Grid holds that every unit of story — line, scene, sequence, act, book — contains the same
five beats: an **inciting incident** that destabilises balance; **progressive complications** that
make restoring it harder each time; a **crisis**, framed as a binary Best Bad Choice or
Irreconcilable Goods; a **climax**, the active answer to that crisis; and a **resolution**, the
direct consequence of the climax choice. The diagnostic: does the scene contain "a defined value
shift" — it doesn't end until the value has shifted. Fractal repetition means a writer stuck at any
scale can run the same five-question checklist, and the crisis-as-binary-choice piece forces
specificity: "what does the protagonist actually have to choose between, right here" beats a vague
"what's the conflict." **Failure mode:** a scene with an incident and complications but no real
crisis — the protagonist drifts to an outcome instead of choosing one — is incident without stakes;
the crisis must be a genuine dilemma with "meaningful stakes," not a choice with an obvious answer.

### McKee's gap, turning points, and value/charge polarity

McKee defines a scene as "a story in miniature... that turns the value-charged condition of a
character's life... from positive to negative or negative to positive." The engine is **the gap
between expectation and result**: a character acts expecting one outcome, the world answers
differently, and the gap between the two is where "the spark of life ignites." Turning points must
generate surprise, curiosity, insight and new direction together, and fail when writers
"overprepare the obvious and underprepare the unusual" — setups near-invisible, payoffs a discovery
rather than a lecture. McKee also holds that only two base emotions exist, pleasure and pain, and
that **mood is not a substitute for a real value shift** — a scene that stays tonally tense
throughout with no charge reversal has mood but no turn. **Failure mode** (McKee's Law of
Diminishing Returns): repeating the same emotional beat without variation loses impact each time —
intensifying dread rather than turning the value produces numbness, not escalation.

### "But / therefore," never "and then" (Parker & Stone)

In their 2011 NYU guest lecture (MTVU's *Stand In*, Ken Liotti's class), Trey Parker: outlining
beats where "the words 'and then' belong between those beats... you've got something pretty
boring." Every join should be "**therefore**" or "**but**" — this happens, *therefore* this
happens; this happens, *but* this happens. Matt Stone: "this happens and then this happens... —
that's not a movie, that's not a story," because "but"/"therefore" supplies causation and "and
then" only supplies sequence. It's a mechanical, real-time version of McKee's gap and Story Grid's
progressive complication that a writers' room can apply to an outline without the theory. Parker
and Stone describe outlining South Park on a whiteboard, testing each joint for a sneaking "and
then." **Failure mode:** sequencing scenes by chronology rather than causation produces an
itinerary, not a plot — fixed by reading the outline aloud and forcing "but"/"therefore" at every
seam.

### Sanderson's try/fail cycles and "yes, but / no, and"

Sanderson's BYU lectures teach the same mechanism from the discovery-writer's side: at each
attempt, ask "does it work?" — yes gets a "but" (a complication survives the success); no gets an
"and" (the failure worsens things). Sanderson: "it creates this sort of sense of motion where
something is always going wrong for the character... to keep your stakes up in a discovery written
story." That Swain (1960s pulp-plotting manual), Butcher (2000s craft blog) and Sanderson (2020s
discovery-writing lectures) converge on the identical mechanism from unconnected disciplines is
suggestive evidence this is load-bearing, not stylistic preference. **Failure mode:** running the
cycle without escalation — the same problem recurring in the same form — is the treadmill effect
Saunders and McKee both warn against under different names: repetition without a stakes increase
reads as stalling.

### Palahniuk's thought verbs and "unpacking"

Palahniuk's "Nuts and Bolts: 'Thought' Verbs" (LitReactor, 2013) bans, for six months: *thinks,
knows, understands, realizes, believes, wants, remembers, imagines, desires*, plus *loves, hates,
is, has*. "Thinking is abstract. Knowing and believing are intangible. Your story will always be
stronger if you just show the physical actions and details... and allow your reader to do the
thinking and knowing." The remedy is **unpacking**: instead of "Kenny wondered if Monica didn't
like him going out at night," show Monica's cold coffee and faked sleep, and let the reader infer
it. It converts narratorial assertion into evidence the reader actively interprets — a stronger
cognitive event than being told the conclusion — and blocks "thesis-statement" scenes that announce
their own meaning before demonstrating it. **Failure mode:** overcorrecting into sensory inventory
that doesn't actually imply the emotion — the detail chosen still has to be diagnostic, not merely
present.

### Saunders' escalation and the Ruthless Efficiency Principle

*A Swim in a Pond in the Rain* (2021), built from Saunders' Syracuse Russian-short-story course,
frames a story as "a continual system of escalation" and "a system for the transfer of energy." His
Ruthless Efficiency Principle: in a well-made story nothing is wasted — a detail that reads clumsy
on a first pass typically turns out, on rereading, to be doing structural work. It reframes craft
feedback from "is this good" to "is this detail earning its place in the escalation" — testable,
not a taste question. Saunders builds the case across seven Chekhov/Tolstoy/Gogol/Turgenev stories
printed in full, each followed by a technical dissection of a "clunky"-looking choice that turns out
load-bearing. **Failure mode:** treating the principle as licence to cut anything not immediately
purposeful — it's retrospective (great writers make everything pull weight), not a rule an early
draft must already satisfy.

### Le Guin's rhythm and point of view

*Steering the Craft* teaches rhythm as inseparable from meaning: "Style is a very simple matter; it
is all rhythm. Once you get that, you can't use the wrong words." Le Guin diagnoses failure states
in prose sound as "Dull, choppy, droning, jerky, feeble" against the target "Lively, well-paced,
flowing, strong, beautiful," and sets an exercise of writing a read-aloud passage using
onomatopoeia, alliteration and invented sound-words to train "the mind's ear." On point of view she
treats who is watching, and how close narration sits to them, as a craft decision with real
consequences — skilled handling "shifts imperceptibly from the author's voice to the point of view
of a character." Sentence rhythm does emotional work whether intended or not: short, jerky
sentences in a calm scene read as anxiety leaking from the prose itself. A commonly paired
demonstration is Gary Provost's varied-sentence-length passage. **Failure mode:** uniform rhythm
across a scene regardless of whether the moment is a hush or a fight flattens a signal the prose
could otherwise carry for free.

### Dialogue craft: subtext, on-the-nose, and Mamet's memo

Subtext is the meaning a line carries beneath its literal content, inferred from context and tone;
"on the nose" dialogue is its opposite — characters saying exactly what they mean, draining
tension. Mamet's memo to *The Unit*'s writers operationalises this: **who wants what; what happens
if they don't get it; why now.** Two named traps: "any time two characters are talking about a
third, the scene is a crock," and any line containing "as you know." Sharpest test: "if you pretend
the characters can't speak, and write a silent movie, you will be writing great drama" — meaning
forced into action, not speech. Standard: a scene not both **advancing the plot** and **standing
alone** is "either superfluous, or incorrectly written." Sorkin's version, aimed at the audience:
"the worst crime you can commit is telling the audience something they already know, in any
fashion, even for a moment" (*New York Times*, 2020). Subtext forces active decoding rather than
being told; the silent-movie test removes the easiest cheat — a character announcing their own
motivation. **Failure mode:** subtext so total nobody ever says what they mean produces
inscrutability, not tension.

### Exposition devices: pope in the pool, and Arrival's colonel

Snyder's "pope in the pool" (*Save the Cat!*) delivers information while something visually
engaging happens simultaneously — the Pope's advisers brief him plot-critical facts while he swims
laps, so the audience watches an incongruous image and absorbs the facts as a side effect. Related
instances: *Jurassic Park*'s "Mr. DNA" short; *Wonder Woman*'s animated-painting bedtime story;
*Doctor Strange*'s hallucinatory training visions. *Arrival* runs the complementary version: rather
than distracting from exposition, it makes the linguistic theory (Sapir-Whorf, diagrammed by
Louise on a whiteboard) the *substance of an argument with real stakes* — Colonel Weber is
characterised deliberately as "a man trying to do what is best for everyone in a volatile
situation," not a stock obstructive officer, and needs the information to decide what to do next,
so the explanation *is* the conflict. Both obey Mamet's silent-movie logic: information with
nothing else at stake reads as lecture; information as a precondition for a decision, or alongside
an engaging image, reads as story. **Failure mode:** a decorative visual with no causal tie to the
information — the distraction has to connect to it, not just coincide with it.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **Grades vary.** Swain, Palahniuk, Le Guin, Mamet, Saunders, McKee and Sorkin are practitioner
  primary sources (own books/essays/memos/verified quotes). Story Grid and "pope in the pool" are
  commercial-methodology grade — useful, widely adopted, not peer-reviewed; treat their formulas as
  heuristics. Blog summaries of Swain/Butcher used here are reliable paraphrase, not the authors'
  words in every sentence.
- **Save the Cat! draws real pushback** — critics (Mythcreants) argue Snyder's beat formulas are
  often over-applied as rigid templates rather than the flexible heuristics intended.
- **Swain and Sanderson converge from opposite disciplines** (1960s plotting manual vs 2020s
  discovery-writing lectures) — suggestive of a load-bearing pattern, not a controlled finding.
- **McKee's "only two base emotions" is a design framework, not an empirical claim** from affective
  science.
- **Parker/Stone's "but/therefore" and Story Grid's progressive complication describe the same
  phenomenon at different resolutions** — the fast seat-of-the-pants version and the formalised
  one, not competing theories.
- **King's *On Writing* is named in the brief but no fetched page yielded verbatim craft-specific
  quotes within this session's budget** — nothing from King is asserted above as a result.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Run every scene through Mamet's three questions before scripting it.** A scene that fails
   (nobody's want on screen) is where the narrator will end up *telling* the political point
   instead of demonstrating it — the sermon voice.md forbids.
2. **Use "pope in the pool" logic for the political/economic idea itself, not just backstory.**
   The idea should arrive while something visually arresting is happening (a panel transition, a
   worker doing something physical) — never as the narrator pausing to explain.
3. **Apply Mamet's "talking about a third" ban to the master-AI narrator format directly.**
   Retelling-from-the-future risks every scene becoming "here's what happened to someone else" —
   counter it by giving the on-panel character an active want inside the scene, not just a fate
   the narrator reports.
4. **"No, and furthermore" is BadCode's default scene ending, not "Yes."** Given the thesis
   (things go wrong, humanity gets a warning), most discovery-timeline scenes should end worse for
   the human side — save flat "Yes" for a rare false-relief beat right before a reversal.
5. **Use Le Guin's rhythm discipline on narration lines specifically.** The sarcastic-authority
   voice depends on short, dry sentences punctuated by one long cosmic one — read every line aloud
   and check the contrast is deliberate, not accidental.
6. **Palahniuk's "unpack the thought verb" rule maps onto comic panel captions.** A caption saying
   a character "realizes" something is the moment to show the physical tell in the panel instead —
   the same rule in prose and in scroll-comic form.
7. **State each scene's crisis as a Best-Bad-Choice or Irreconcilable-Goods sentence before
   building it.** If it can't be stated that tightly, the scene hasn't found its turn, and the
   political point risks arriving as narration rather than dilemma.
8. **Sorkin's rule is the test for whether a scene is aimed at the reader or the writers' room.**
   A scene that only restates a point the reader already senses, without a new mechanism or
   culprit, fails and should be cut or re-aimed.
9. **Run the "but/therefore" pass on every 20-scene outline before production.** Read the beat list
   aloud; any seam where only "and then" fits is a candidate for merging, cutting, or adding a real
   complication.
10. **Arrival's colonel-scene move is the model for any scene carrying the ownership/inequality
    argument itself.** Give the argument to a character with a real, non-strawman stake in the
    wrong answer (never punch down, per voice.md), and let the disagreement do the explaining.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does this scene's value sit at a different charge at the end than at the start?
2. Can you name the character's concrete goal for this scene in one sentence?
3. Does the scene end on disaster/complication ("yes, but" / "no, and") rather than clean
   resolution?
4. Strip the dialogue — does the scene still show who wants what and what's at stake (the
   silent-movie test)?
5. Does any line have two characters discussing a third, or contain "as you know"?
6. Does the beat before this one connect with "but" or "therefore" — or does "and then" fit better?
7. Is there a sequel (reaction → dilemma → decision) after a major setback, or does the story cut
   straight to the next scene's action?
8. Does the crisis reduce to one sentence, stated as a binary choice with real stakes both ways?
9. Are there "thought verbs" doing work that could be unpacked into a physical, inferable detail?
10. Is exposition delivered alongside something else happening (pope in the pool), or does the
    scene stop to explain?
11. Does the reader already know the political point before the scene starts — and if so, what new
    mechanism or character does it actually add?
12. Read the narration aloud — does the rhythm match the moment, or is it uniform throughout?
13. Is the antagonist's position a strawman, or a real, non-punching-down stake?
14. Does this scene's ending generate the next scene's goal, or could the next scene start without
    it?
15. If you removed this scene, would the story lose a turn — or just a fact?
