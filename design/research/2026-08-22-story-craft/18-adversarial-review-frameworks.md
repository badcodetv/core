# How Professionals Review a Story: Coverage, Braintrusts, and Checklists

## What this covers

This brief is about *diagnosis*, not composition: how professionals find out whether a finished or
near-finished draft works, before anyone else sees it. It covers script coverage (the reader's
report and verdict system), Pixar's Braintrust as a peer-review structure, checklists from three
story consultants (Matt Bird, Shawn Coyne/Story Grid, K.M. Weiland), the MICE quotient as a
structural diagnostic, how screenwriters give and take notes without wrecking the relationship
(Scriptnotes), Ira Glass's test for whether a pitch is a story at all, Michael Arndt's ending test,
and table reads/beta readers. It leaves to sibling briefs how to *build* structure (01), theme and
character engine (02), scene-level construction (03), and openings/endings craft on their own terms
(05, 06) — this is the checklist layer on top, the adversarial pass composition alone misses.

## Top findings

1. **Pixar's Braintrust works because it has zero authority.** Catmull's defining rule: "the
   Braintrust has no authority. The director does not have to follow any of the specific
   suggestions." Removing the power to mandate a fix is what makes candour possible.
2. **The best notes diagnose; they don't prescribe.** Mazin/August's rule from *Scriptnotes* 86:
   the ideal note says "this isn't working for me... but I'm not going to tell you how to solve it."
   A note that ships its own fix pre-empts the writer's better solution.
3. **The three responses that kill a notes meeting: Defend, Deny, Debate.** Mazin's failure modes.
   The fix is reframing from defending *the script* to jointly discussing *the movie*.
4. **A story pitch needs something at stake before it's worth pursuing.** Ira Glass's test, via
   producer Julie Snyder: "I'm driving across the country and taping people" fails — "there's no
   question driving it forward... no conflict." A pitch that stacks two live conflicts teaches the
   difference.
5. **Killing a third of what you start is healthy, not a failure.** Glass: "if I'm not killing at
   least a third of the interviews... we're not taking enough chances." Abandonment rate is
   evidence of real swings, not bad judgement.
6. **An ending must resolve external, internal and philosophical stakes in the same beat.** Michael
   Arndt's "insanely great" endings combine surprise, logic and thematic insight simultaneously —
   not a plot win followed by a separate emotional coda.
7. **The MICE quotient nests like Russian dolls; threads close in reverse order of opening.** Mary
   Robinette Kowal's diagnostic (from Orson Scott Card) sorts threads into Milieu/Inquiry/
   Character/Event; an unclosed thread is a diagnosable defect, not a vibe.
8. **The Story Grid spreadsheet exists to make invisible repetition visible.** Shawn Coyne calls it
   "a car inspection report" — its value is showing patterns a writer missed, like "six scenes
   establishing something that you could have done in one scene."
9. **Script coverage's verdict is about production viability, not artistic merit.** Readers
   overwhelmingly Pass — "only three or four Recommends" a year — and Pass isn't a claim the
   writing is bad, only that this company wouldn't make it.
10. **A table read exposes what silent reading hides, via one blunt test.** "If you removed the
    character names, could you tell who is speaking?" Indistinct voices and stalled scenes surface
    audibly in ways they don't on the page.
11. **One revision pass, one focus.** Jericho Writers: decide "which element... will this editing
    round focus on?" before starting, rather than fixing everything in one undifferentiated read.
12. **Matt Bird's checklist keeps theme "untidy" on purpose.** His theme test asks whether "the
    dilemma [is] ultimately unsolvable" — punishing neatly resolved morals on principle.

## The techniques in detail

### Script coverage: the reader's report
**What it is.** The standard film/TV gatekeeping document: cover page, logline, one-to-two-page
synopsis, a half-to-full-page comments section on strengths/weaknesses/marketability, and a final
verdict — usually **Pass / Consider / Recommend**, sometimes with a numeric grid across concept,
character, structure, dialogue (Script Reader Pro; StudioBinder; No Film School). **Why it works.**
Same shape of judgement every time, so verdicts across readers are comparable, and it separates "is
this good" from "is this for us" — a Pass reflects whether *this company* would produce it, not a
claim the writing is bad. **Worked example.** The Black List aggregates a 1–10 scale across readers
into a searchable ranking, so a script accumulates social proof over time rather than living or
dying on one verdict. **Failure mode.** Read as an artistic verdict, coverage demoralises rather
than informs — most good scripts Pass too.

### The Pixar Braintrust
**What it is.** A standing group of experienced storytellers who give candid feedback direct to the
director of a film-in-progress, with no power to compel a change. **Why it works.** Catmull's
mechanism: authority and candour are opposites in a room. The moment a note carries the threat of a
mandated rewrite, people posture and defend; strip the mandate and the true thing can be said
without the director losing ownership. Producer Jonas Rivera "headlines" the session — distilling
scattered notes into what's actually landing — so it doesn't become forty separate prescriptions.
**Worked example.** On *Toy Story 3*, Andrew Stanton's critique of the toys' unclear motivations
pushed writer Michael Arndt off a planned heroic speech and into Big Baby's sympathies instead; on
*Inside Out*, Brad Bird pushed back on Pete Docter's early simplicity while the group reinforced the
maturation theme. **Failure mode.** An implicit hierarchy — the senior voice's suggestion becoming a
de facto instruction — collapses back into notes-with-authority; the structure, not the goodwill, is
the mechanism.

### Giving and taking notes (Scriptnotes)
**What it is.** Craig Mazin and John August's rules, from the episode "Taking Notes," for surviving
a notes meeting intact. **Why it works.** Their "3 Ds" — Defend, Deny, Debate — name the moves that
turn a session into a fight; neutral questions instead ("Can you explain that? Can you go deeper?")
let a weak note justify or collapse itself without confrontation. Reframing from "the script" (which
the writer owns) to "the movie" (everyone's making together) removes the personal stake that
triggers defensiveness. **Worked example.** Mazin's ideal note separates diagnosis from
prescription: "this isn't working for me... but I'm not going to tell you how to solve it" — trusting
the writer, not the note-giver, to find the fix. **Failure mode.** A note-giver who prescribes
pre-empts a better solution than the one they happened to think of; a note-taker who
Defends/Denies/Debates burns the trust the *next* note needs.

### Structural diagnostics: Story Grid's Six Core Questions and spreadsheet
**What it is.** Shawn Coyne's macro audit: (1) genre; (2) the conventions/obligatory moments the
genre demands; (3) narrative device and point of view; (4) protagonist's and antagonist's objects of
desire — do they conflict; (5) the controlling idea (a single-sentence value-change argument); (6)
what happens in the Beginning Hook, Middle Build, Middle Climax, Ending Payoff. Underneath it, the
**spreadsheet** tracks per-scene data — word count, value shift, polarity shift, turning point,
time, location, cast — what Coyne calls "a car inspection report." **Why it works.** The six
questions catch category errors before line notes waste anyone's time; the spreadsheet turns "the
middle feels slow" into a visible pattern, like six scenes doing one scene's job. **Worked example.**
Story Grid's case-study episodes apply the six questions scene-by-scene to a published novel to show
where its structure follows its own genre's rules and where it doesn't. **Failure mode.** Used as
compliance rather than lens, it can pressure a writer into manufacturing a value shift in every
scene whether needed or not — Coyne frames it as a tool for "when you want," not a mandatory fill.

### Checklists: Matt Bird, K.M. Weiland, Jericho Writers
**What they are.** Three overlapping, differently-shaped question sets. Bird's *Ultimate Story
Checklist* runs concept → character (Believe/Care/Invest) → a four-quarter structure test (challenge
in Q1, easy way Q2, hard way Q3, climax Q4) → scenework (Set-Up/Conflict/Outcome) → dialogue
(Empathetic/Specific/Heightened/Strategic) → theme (Difficult/Grounded/Subtle/**Untidy**). Weiland's
maps percentage-based beats (plot points at 25/50/75%, pinch points at 37/62%) onto character arc
(Lie vs Truth, Want vs Need, the Ghost), theme, setting, POV. Jericho Writers' set is smaller and
sequencing-focused: pick the round's single focus, ask if each scene is necessary, check opening and
ending, check characters have flaws *and* motivation, read aloud for rhythm. **Why they work.** Each
turns "something's off" into an answerable question; Bird's is diagnostic, Weiland's closer to a
beat-sheet template. **Worked example.** Bird's theme question — "is the dilemma ultimately
unsolvable?" — punishes tidy resolutions; a theme statable as a solved equation fails regardless of
everything else. **Failure mode.** Percentage-based checklists risk hitting the numbers without the
function landing — a plot point "at 25%" that doesn't escalate stakes passes the letter, fails the
intent.

### The MICE quotient
**What it is.** Orson Scott Card's four story types — Milieu, Idea (renamed Inquiry by Kowal),
Character, Event — each with an opening/closing condition and species of conflict: Milieu opens on
entering a place, closes on leaving; Inquiry opens on a question, closes on its answer; Character
opens on "who am I," closes on self-recognition; Event opens on a disruption, closes on a new status
quo. Kowal's addition: **nesting** — threads open and close like nested boxes, in reverse order,
last opened first closed. **Why it works.** It turns "is this the right length?" and "did I abandon
a thread?" into checkable structure, not feel. **Worked example.** Kowal's *Wizard of Oz*
walk-through: Character opens (Dorothy's dissatisfaction), then Event (tornado), then Milieu (Oz),
then Inquiry (the ruby slippers) — closing in the mirrored order. **Failure mode.** Applied
mechanically it becomes box-ticking taxonomy; the value is catching an unclosed thread, not
labelling every story one "type."

### Killing stories and the "what's at stake" test
**What it is.** Ira Glass's filter for *This American Life* pitches. A pitch fails with no question
driving it and no conflict — his example, "I'm driving cross-country and I'll tape the people I
meet," fails because "there's nothing at stake." A working pitch stacks conflicts (his "Trek"
example: will distant relatives turn out to have opposed apartheid, and will two lifelong friends of
different races start fighting about race for the first time). Producer Alex Blumberg adds two more
failure modes: material that's merely *pretty* (an interviewee who sounds like public radio, no
actual content), and a subject chosen because it's "the type of story you might hear on the radio,"
not real interest — his fix, always thinking "this is like this, this means that." **Why it works.**
It separates interesting material from a story, with a concrete reason to abandon the former fast.
**Worked example.** Glass's own rate: "if I'm not killing at least a third of the interviews... we're
not taking enough chances." **Failure mode.** Applied too early it kills promising raw material —
Glass himself gathered tape "without knowing anything about what kind of tape I'd get" and found the
story afterward; the test is for pitches before you've gathered anything.

### Endings: Michael Arndt's three-stakes test
**What it is.** From his lecture "Endings: The Good, the Bad, and the Insanely Great" — a great
ending pays off external stakes (what's won or lost materially), internal stakes (the emotional
cost/change) and philosophical stakes (what the story argues about how to live) **in the same
beat**, not as separate boxes. **Why it works.** It targets the common failure where the plot
resolves, then separately the character has a feeling, then separately someone states the theme —
three payoffs stapled together instead of one doing all three jobs. **Worked example.** Arndt
describes Pixar writing the ending before the first act, since the ending is the contract the
opening must set up. **Failure mode.** An ending that hits internal/philosophical stakes but skips
the external one (or vice versa) reads as hollow spectacle or unearned sentiment.

### Table reads and beta readers
**What they are.** Hearing a script performed aloud before locking it; a structured questionnaire
for early manuscript readers. **Why they work.** Both exploit the fact a writer's brain "gloss[es]
over any problems on the actual page" — silent reading auto-corrects awkward lines. Table Read
Studio's tests: could you tell who's speaking with names removed; does each scene end with someone
learning, choosing, revealing, losing ground or gaining power; does the next scene answer,
complicate, or contrast the one before. Beta-reader templates ask about the opening's grip,
confusing passages, "laggy" pacing, character believability, whether the ending satisfied. **Worked
example.** Both recommend reading for one thing at a time — first the hook, then indistinguishable
voices, then the ending — mirroring Jericho Writers' one-focus-per-pass rule. **Failure mode.**
Actors who "sell" weak material through performance mask the problems a table read exists to expose.

## Disagreements and caveats

**Prescriptive percentages vs diagnostic questions.** Weiland's beat-sheet (plot points fixed at
25/50/75%, pinch points at 37/62%) is a stronger structural claim than Bird's or Story Grid's
question-based checklists, which ask whether a *function* is served without insisting on a page
number. Treat the percentages as one practitioner's pattern-matching across commercial genre
fiction, not a law; neither is peer-reviewed.

**Vendor content vs practitioner voice.** The script-coverage sources (Script Reader Pro,
StudioBinder, No Film School) are companies selling coverage services or education — useful for the
industry-standard *shape* of a report, but category counts and verdict labels vary between them;
there is no single canonical template, only a converging convention.

**MICE quotient is a teaching heuristic, not a tested taxonomy.** It comes from Card's own craft
book and Kowal's extension of it in her teaching — genuinely useful for diagnosis, but a
practitioner's organising scheme, not an empirically validated category system.

**Ira Glass's numbers are medium-specific.** "Killing a third" is about first-person radio
interviews at *This American Life* and doesn't obviously transfer to, say, a scripted comic with no
raw tape to abandon. The underlying test — is there a real conflict, is something at stake —
transfers; the rate is anecdotal to his medium.

**The Braintrust's "no authority" claim is itself contested elsewhere** in the wider discourse
around whether the structure survived Pixar's later leadership changes intact. This brief relies on
Catmull's own account via a strong secondary source and does not independently verify current
practice.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Write coverage on your own releases before anyone else does.** For each ~20-scene GPOM story:
   a one-page coverage sheet — logline, synopsis, per-scene comments, a verdict — before it ships.
   The verdict question: does this land the encoded political point without sermon.
2. **Adopt "notes not fixes" as the Kai/Jack review discipline.** Phrase a note as "this beat isn't
   landing for me" and stop — let the other person find the fix. Cheap, and the exact mechanism the
   Braintrust and Scriptnotes both rely on.
3. **Run Arndt's three-stakes test on every ending, especially the narrator's sign-off.** Does the
   final beat pay off the plot, the character's cost, and the "don't make this mistake" point in the
   *same* line, not a plot beat then a separate voiced moral?
4. **Use the MICE quotient to choose format, not just structure.** A single-scene short is an Event
   piece, judged by whether the disruption resolves; a scroll comic touring a location is a Milieu
   piece and shouldn't end until the reader narratively "leaves." Naming the type first stops a
   short acquiring a digression it can't afford.
5. **Apply Ira Glass's stake test at the idea-inbox stage, not after the storyboard is drawn.**
   Before `new-idea` graduates to `new-story`, ask what conflict is driving this and what's at
   stake. Park ideas that are just "wouldn't it be interesting if..."
6. **Table-read the narrator's VO script before locking a narrated video** — aloud or via TTS —
   scene by scene: does each beat end with someone learning, choosing, revealing, or losing/gaining
   ground, or is it just scene-setting?
7. **Adopt Jericho Writers' one-focus-per-pass rule for comic and lyric revision** — a political-
   clarity pass, a character pass, a punchline pass, run separately rather than all at once.
8. **Fold `the-reader.md`'s rules into the coverage checklist itself**: hero or pupil; a beneficiary
   named on-panel wherever automation fear is raised; any beat that could misread as "you've been
   duped." Your Story Grid-style obligatory-moments check, specific to this reader.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Can the controlling idea be stated in one sentence without that sentence being spoken aloud in
   the piece?
2. Does the originating pitch have a real conflict and something at stake, or is it "wouldn't it be
   interesting if..."?
3. Read it aloud: is there a line that exists only to explain plot to the audience?
4. Does the ending pay off external, internal and philosophical stakes in the same beat, or as
   three separate payoffs?
5. Which MICE thread opened first — does it close last? Is any thread left open?
6. Is there a run of scenes that doesn't shift value or polarity — could six become one?
7. Is a given note a diagnosis ("this isn't landing") or does it ship a prescribed fix?
8. Whose ego is protected if this note gets softened before it's given?
9. If you had to kill one in three ideas at pitch stage on principle, would this one survive?
10. With names removed, could you tell who's speaking from voice alone?
11. Does every scene end with someone learning, choosing, revealing, losing ground, or gaining
    power — or does one just sit there?
12. Is the theme "untidy" — does the dilemma remain genuinely unsolvable, or has it resolved into a
    tidy, statable moral?
13. Does the reader-analogue appear as wronged hero, never as audience-being-taught — and is a
    beneficiary named on-panel wherever automation fear is raised?
14. As one-line coverage — Pass, Consider, Recommend — what's the verdict, in one sentence why?
15. Was this revision pass aimed at one layer (plot, character, dialogue, theme), or all at once?
