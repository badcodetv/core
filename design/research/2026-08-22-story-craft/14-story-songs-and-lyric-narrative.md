# Story-songs: narrative in lyrics

## What this covers

How a song tells a story in three to four minutes: point-of-view mechanics, the letter/epistolary
device, displacement (hiding a private wound inside a public image), moral neutrality in
first-person villain narration, spoken-word/news-bulletin interludes as licence to say the
unsayable plainly, naming names as protest craft, and structural ambition in concept albums and
drum & bass suites. Drawn from craft books (Pattison, Tweedy), artist-on-record accounts of
specific songs (Springsteen on *Nebraska*, Cocker on "Common People", Dammers on "Ghost Town",
Skinner on *A Grand Don't Come for Free*, Kano on his own catalogue), and structural analysis of
"Stan", "Fitter Happier" and "Fight the Power". Left to sibling briefs: video/visual narrative,
comic-page pacing, general screenwriting structure, and the psychology of persuasion — this is
about what happens inside the lyric sheet and the arrangement.

## Top findings

1. **Pat Pattison's four-point-of-view framework** makes POV a deliberate choice. Third person is
   "basically God" — past, present, future, every mind, no "I" ("Eleanor Rigby"). Second person
   stays outside the song addressing a "you" the narrator can tell things it doesn't yet know
   ("Like a Rolling Stone"). First person shares the singer's own experience but "should be
   interesting to the world." Direct address (I/you) is pop's default and Pattison names its trap:
   "I personally have difficulties listening to a live set of 'I/you' songs... it becomes static."
   Source: Sound on Sound's interview with Pattison on *Writing Better Lyrics*.
2. **State the drama before you write the verse.** Mike Skinner took Robert McKee's screenwriting
   course before *A Grand Don't Come for Free* and distilled it: "Every song needs a drama at the
   centre of it, and once you have the drama, the song writes itself." A concept album is this
   rule run across a tracklist. Source: Wikipedia's account of the album; Loud & Quiet's interview
   with Skinner.
3. **Comment, don't describe.** Skinner's second rule, in his own words: "I'm only ever commenting
   on what is going on, rather than describing what's going on" — the songs work like film noir,
   through suggestion, so a listener can't predict plot from lyrics alone. Withholding exposition
   is a three-minute story's main defence against sermon. Source: Loud & Quiet.
4. **A second voice inside the track can carry dramatic irony the narrator can't state.** "Stan"
   builds three verses as escalating fan letters over a chorus sampling Dido's "Thank You" — a
   warm hook that reads as ironic counterpoint, standing in for the unheard girlfriend, as the
   letters darken. The final verse flips to Eminem's own voice, realising too late. Nobody
   moralises; structure delivers the tragedy. Source: primesound.org's structural analysis.
5. **Displacement: write the private wound as the public image.** Jerry Dammers, after a year
   writing "Ghost Town": "'Ghost Town' was about the breakup of the Specials... I just didn't want
   to write about my state of mind so I tried to relate it to the country as a whole." The private
   grief supplied emotional charge; observed detail (shuttered shops, women "selling their
   household goods") supplied the political image. Source: Songfacts.
6. **A political song can lie about its own ending and be more honest for it.** Jarvis Cocker
   inverted the real story of "Common People" — in life he never made a move; in the song the
   woman wants to sleep with him, "which was a lie" — because the argument (posh people can *leave*
   poverty at will) needed that beat to land. Source: Songfacts, citing Cocker/Uncut.
7. **First-person villain narration works by moral neutrality, not sympathy.** Springsteen, on
   voicing killer Charles Starkweather on "Nebraska": "I wanted the listener to hear my characters
   think, to feel their thoughts, their choices" — flat delivery, verdict left to the listener,
   written while reading Flannery O'Connor and watching *Badlands*. Source: American Songwriter and
   Mojo.
8. **Concrete gesture beats stated emotion.** Springsteen's own method on the same record: "The
   writing was in the details; the twisting of a ring, the twirling of a baton, was where these
   songs found their character." Source: Mojo.
9. **A flat, non-singing voice can say what melody can't.** "Fitter Happier" is built entirely from
   a Macintosh text-to-speech voice reciting a "checklist of slogans for the '90s" Thom Yorke
   called "the most upsetting thing I've ever written" — he could not deliver it sung. Source:
   Songfacts.
10. **Naming names is deliberate protest craft.** "Fight the Power" names Elvis Presley, John Wayne
    and Bobby McFerrin, opening on sixteen seconds of an uncredited civil-rights speech before any
    music plays. Chuck D on McFerrin: "If you're not worried and you're happy, you're like, why
    protest?" — specificity turns mood into an argument a listener can check. Source: Wikipedia
    and Songfacts.
11. **A concept album can carry a twist that recasts everything already heard**, and can refuse to
    pick one ending: *A Grand Don't Come for Free* resolves Simone's infidelity, then offers both a
    bitter ending and a redemptive one (the money found behind the television). Source: Wikipedia,
    citing *Q* magazine's "spoken-word opera" description.
12. **Let the story exist before the technique announces itself.** Kano: "I was choosing to spit on
    music that would allow me to tell a story, and not cloud me, and not get in the way of what
    was being said" — beat subordinate to narrative, written mostly outside the studio. Source:
    Clash Magazine.

## The techniques in detail

**Point-of-view choice.** What it is: Pattison's third/second/first-person/direct-address
taxonomy, each with its own licence. Why it works: each POV changes what the narrator is allowed
to know and who is implicated — second person lets a narrator warn a character of what it can't
yet see; direct address is intimate but static across a set. Worked example: "Like a Rolling
Stone" stays second-person throughout, telling "you" what she's become before she can see it
(Pattison's own example). Failure mode: defaulting to I/you because it's the pop norm, then every
track in a release feels like the same relationship restated.

**Drama-at-the-centre.** What it is: naming the single external conflict before writing a line,
borrowed by Skinner from McKee. Why it works: a stated external drama (missing money, an
infidelity) gives every verse something to advance rather than restate. Worked example: *A Grand
Don't Come for Free*'s whole arc is legible from the drama alone. Failure mode: a "concept" that's
actually just mood or setting — no yes/no question, so atmosphere accumulates with nothing to
resolve.

**Comment, not describe.** What it is: reacting to events rather than laying them out, per
Skinner. Why it works: a short song has no room for exposition; withholding mechanics forces the
listener to reconstruct the story, which disguises the political point as observation. Worked
example: Skinner's own noir comparison — mood implies the crime, no stated sequence. Failure mode:
commentary with no underlying event — nothing to reconstruct, so "suggestion" reads as vagueness.

**The second voice as dramatic irony.** What it is: a sample, hook or duet partner that knows or
represents something the narrating voice doesn't. Why it works: two truths run at once without
either voice stating the gap; the listener supplies the irony. Worked example: "Stan"'s Dido
chorus, unchanging while the letters around it darken. Failure mode: a "duet" where both voices
just agree — no gap, no irony, doubled melody only.

**Displacement.** What it is: encoding an unstatable private feeling inside an ostensibly political
subject, as Dammers did (a band's break-up, dressed as urban decline). Why it works: private
material supplies real charge; public image supplies reach. Worked example: "Ghost Town" hit
number one during real 1981 unrest partly because the desolation wasn't performed. Failure mode:
displacement so total the political image never lands an argument — mood with no content, readable
as vague doom rather than diagnosis.

**Moral neutrality in villain narration.** What it is: singing as a wrongdoer without narrating
judgement onto them — flat delivery and specific detail standing in for verdict. Why it works: the
listener is denied being told what to feel, so they assemble it themselves, which lands harder and
reads as less preachy. Worked example: Springsteen wanted listeners to "feel their thoughts, their
choices," not his judgement. Failure mode: neutrality tips into glamorisation when no structural
consequence (arrest, a second voice's grief) ever anchors the listener's verdict.

**Concrete gesture over stated feeling.** What it is: one physical, picturable action replacing an
abstract emotional claim. Why it works: gesture is specific and does double duty as
characterisation and pacing. Worked example: Springsteen's "twisting of a ring, the twirling of a
baton." Failure mode: gesture as decoration that reveals nothing about state — just scenery.

**The spoken-word / non-singing interlude.** What it is: breaking from melody into flat, spoken,
sampled or synthesised delivery for content the sung register can't carry without sounding
preachy. Why it works: an audience's guard against "message" is calibrated to melody and rhyme;
stripping those resets the guard so blunt content reads as information. Worked example: "Fitter
Happier"'s TTS checklist; "Fight the Power" opening on an uncredited speech sample before any
music, framing the argument as documentary. Failure mode: the interlude becomes novelty (a funny
robot voice, a random clip) disconnected from the song's own argument — texture, not content.

**Naming names.** What it is: attacking or crediting specific, checkable people or institutions
rather than a system or mood. Why it works: a named target can be verified or disputed, which
makes the claim an argument rather than an atmosphere. Worked example: "Fight the Power"'s targeting
of "Don't Worry Be Happy" as the actual thing protest is arguing against. Failure mode: a target so
niche it needs a footnote — the specificity that should sharpen the point instead requires
explanation.

**Long-form structural ambition (the DnB/jungle suite).** What it is: stretching a track beyond
song length into movements — Goldie's title track "Timeless" runs roughly 21 minutes across
sub-sections ("Inner City Life / Pressure / Jah"). Why it works (tentatively): length lets an
emotional arc build the way a film score builds one. Worked example: *Timeless*, per Wikipedia's
account and the Guardian's "50 key events in dance music" framing. Failure mode (this brief's own
inference, unverified): not directly transferable to a 3–4 minute track — the transferable lesson
is *movement thinking within a short form* (register shift between verse and drop), not literal
duration.

**The dual ending.** What it is: offering two resolutions to the same story, as *A Grand Don't
Come for Free* does. Why it works: holds diagnosis and the possibility of repair in the same
piece without picking a side that reads as naive or nihilistic. Failure mode: a dual ending that
reads as indecision if nothing earlier sets up why both outcomes are plausible.

## Disagreements and caveats

**Authenticity versus fabrication for craft.** Skinner has said he "didn't want to lie" about his
songwriting's realism, while Cocker openly fabricated "Common People"'s ending to sharpen the
point, and Dave's "Lesley" blurs autobiography and testimony to the point critics note he "breaks
the fourth wall." Different practitioners draw this line differently; this brief doesn't resolve
it — BadCode's AI-narrator conceit (a machine claiming lived history it cannot literally have) sits
deliberately on the fabrication side.

**Moral neutrality is contested, not settled.** Springsteen frames neutral first-person villain
narration as a strength; no countervailing practitioner source turned up in this research, but the
general worry that "neutral" delivery can read as endorsement without a structural anchor for
judgement is this brief's own synthesis, flagged as such, not a sourced disagreement.

**Source grading.** Practitioner-grade (direct artist statement, fetched): Pattison (Sound on
Sound), Tweedy (Penguin Random House excerpt), Springsteen (Mojo, American Songwriter), Cocker and
Dammers (Songfacts, sourced from Uncut/Q), Skinner (Loud & Quiet), Kano (Clash), Chuck D
(Songfacts). Tertiary/journalism-grade: the Wikipedia entries on "Fight the Power", *A Grand Don't
Come for Free*, "Lesley" and *Timeless* — useful for structure and reception, not primary
testimony. Listicle grade: primesound.org's "Stan" breakdown, used only for structural description
consistent with search-consensus accounts, not for any quote attributed to Eminem (none was
found). No source was found for the jungle/DnB storytelling artists named in the brief beyond
Goldie (Roni Size, Chase & Status, Bou, SpectraSoul) — any claim about their technique is
intentionally omitted rather than asserted from memory.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Give the AI narrator Pattison's second-person mode as default**, not direct address:
   "received wisdom from a future that already went wrong" is structurally a narrator who knows
   what "you" cannot yet know. Ration direct address for the rare moment it breaks its own remove.
2. **Write the drama in one sentence before writing bars**, and check it against the-reader.md's
   rule 5: does it name a decision-maker and a beneficiary, not a mood ("automation is scary")? If
   it can't, don't write the verse yet.
3. **Split narrator and character duties along "comment, not describe."** Let a character's verse
   live in the scene with no self-narrated meaning; keep all commentary and future-tense certainty
   for the narrator's bars — matches the-reader.md's rule 7 (perspective-getting) while keeping the
   Hitchhiker's Guide register intact.
4. **Borrow "Stan"'s letter/twist-verse shape for a perspective-switch track**: two or three verses
   in a wronged character's own voice (a voice note, a message thread), then a final verse from
   the narrator's already-knowing vantage. Literalises the "sent back as a warning" premise inside
   one song, not just the album frame.
5. **Use displacement to solve voice.md's sarcasm/care two-step**: hide the narrator's own grief
   (bored, regretful) inside the verse's political image, so the surface stays sarcastic while the
   private ache supplies care underneath, without ever stating it.
6. **Reserve one flat, non-musical moment per EP for the plainest statement of the political
   point** — Fitter Happier's move. Natural home for the-reader.md's requirement that every
   automation beat name the actual beneficiary; keep it rare so the interruption still works.
7. **Name institutions and mechanisms, never individuals or groups the-reader.md rules out.** "The
   firm that owns eleven letting agencies in this postcode" beats "the landlord" — the same
   discipline as rule 5, with the mechanism for *why* it works.
8. **Swap stated feeling for one concrete gesture in lyrics and comic captions** — the discipline
   the-reader.md's rule 7 already points toward; Springsteen's "twisting of a ring" is the craft
   language for it.
9. **Build a serialised episode like *A Grand Don't Come for Free*: one external object driving the
   plot, with a late twist recasting an earlier chorus line** — seed a detail early that only pays
   off as a reveal in a later release.
10. **Use the dual ending as a structural signature**: every story already carries "don't make this
    mistake" (bitter, unheeded) and "you are loved, we can fix it" (redemptive) — a ready-made
    technique for holding both, the exact tension voice.md names as load-bearing.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Can the central drama be stated in one sentence before any lines were written, naming a
   decision-maker and a beneficiary?
2. Whose "you" is being addressed, and does the POV stay in one register or shift deliberately?
3. Does the chorus state the thesis outright, or accumulate meaning as a refrain — deliberately?
4. Is a beneficiary or decision-maker named on-panel, rather than an abstract "system"?
5. If a wrongdoer speaks in first person, is judgement withheld from the narration, with
   consequence or a second voice left to anchor the verdict?
6. Is there at least one concrete, picturable gesture standing in for a stated emotion?
7. If two voices or a sample interact, does the second know or represent something the first
   doesn't, rather than doubling the same sentiment?
8. Does the piece withhold at least one piece of plot information the listener must infer?
9. If there's a spoken/flat moment, does it earn its plainness, or is it decoration?
10. Does the final verse turn — new information, a reversal, a reveal — rather than restate the
    chorus?
11. Are the sarcasm beat and the care beat kept in separate lines, not blended into one tone?
12. Would the political charge survive if a named institution were swapped for a vaguer synonym?
13. Does the story stand up as a story before any political reading is layered on?
14. Is there a beat where the audience gets both "how this goes wrong" and "how it doesn't have
    to"?
15. Does the wronged character speak in their own words at some point, rather than only being
    spoken about?
