# Myths, Counter-Evidence, and Where the Experts Disagree

## What this covers

Craft advice repeats itself until it sounds like law. This brief tests eight of the load-bearing
rules — the hero's journey, show-don't-tell, conflict-on-every-page, the likeable protagonist,
write-what-you-know, outlining-versus-discovery, theme-first drafting, and "trust the audience" —
against what the people who actually disagree with them have said, in their own words, plus one
newer myth: that AI prose is undetectable. It does not re-cover Save the Cat, the *Lost* mystery
box, or Palahniuk's thought-verbs ban — those are handled in sibling briefs. Every named claim
below traces to a specific fetched page, listed at the end with a grade; where a claim from the
brief could not be freshly verified this round (the session's web-search budget was exhausted by
earlier briefs, and several major outlets — the *Guardian*, *Atlantic*, *New Yorker*, *Vulture*,
the Wayback Machine — refused automated fetches outright), it is marked **(unverified)** rather
than presented as sourced fact.

## Top findings

- **The hero's journey is a shape imposed on stories, not a law discovered inside them.** Ursula
  K. Le Guin's "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" names the cost directly: "It is the story that
  hid my humanity from me, the story the mammoth hunters told about bashing, thrusting, raping,
  killing, about the Hero" [20]. Novelist Ellie Robins pushes further — "there can be no such
  thing as a monomyth" because a single dominant story is a monoculture, and "a monoculture always
  spells death" [5].
- **The monomyth was never gender-neutral, and its own patch admits it.** Campbell's model treated
  women as the hero's destination, not the hero; Jungian therapist Maureen Murdock wrote *The
  Heroine's Journey* in response, but it was built as therapy, "not a guide to narrative theory"
  [6].
- **"Show, don't tell" is bad advice stated as a rule.** Francine Prose, close-reading an Alice
  Munro passage in *Reading Like a Writer*, concludes it "contradicts a form of bad advice often
  given young writers — namely, that the job of the author is to show, not tell" [18]. Le Guin, in
  *Steering the Craft*: "The story is not in the plot but in the telling. It is the telling that
  moves" [14].
- **What the rule is actually protecting against is undisguised exposition, not narration itself.**
  John Yorke's real distinction: "Exposition, after all, is telling and drama is showing... the
  greater the conflict, the less visible the exposition. All good exposition is disguised by
  making it dramatic — by injecting conflict" [15]. Chekhov's actual note, to Aleksandr Lazarev in
  1889, was about not making false promises with detail — "one must not put a loaded rifle on the
  stage if no one is thinking of firing it" — not a ban on telling [1].
- **Likeability is a trap question, and it catches women's characters harder.** Asked if she'd
  want to be friends with her narrator, Claire Messud answered: "For heaven's sake, what kind of
  question is that? Would you want to be friends with Humbert Humbert? ... If you're reading to
  find friends, you're in deep trouble. We read to find life, in all its possibilities" [7]. Roxane
  Gay: an unlikeable man "is billed as an antihero, earning a special term"; an unlikeable woman
  "is a problem" [19].
- **Conflict-on-every-page has a working counter-tradition, not just objectors.** Kishōtenketsu
  turns a story on a recontextualising twist (*ten*) rather than escalating conflict, and does not
  centre conflict the way three-act structure does [2]. Henry Lien names the mechanism in
  Miyazaki: Western children's media "lack[s] confidence in the ability of young audiences to pay
  attention," producing manufactured stakes and moralising, where *Totoro* trusts "childhood / more
  childhood / adulthood threatens childhood / childhood prevails" [4]. Matt Bird's own doctrine —
  "never let up... things go from bad to worse" [17] — is exactly the position kishōtenketsu
  argues against, not a refutation of it.
- **"Write what you know" only survives if "know" means felt, not lived.** Nathan Englander: "if
  you've known longing, then you can write longing." Kazuo Ishiguro calls the literal rule "the
  most stupid thing"; Toni Morrison tells students "don't pay any attention to that"; Ken Kesey:
  "one of the dumbest things you were ever taught." Reframed, it survives — Le Guin accepts it
  "with a good definition of 'know'"; Meg Wolitzer: "write what obsesses you" [10].
- **Outlining in advance is a personal method, not a professional requirement.** Stephen King, per
  a *Wall Street Journal* interview: "I don't outline, I don't have whole plots in my head in
  advance... I'm really happy if I know what's going to happen tomorrow" [9]. George R.R. Martin's
  split: "architects" plan like a building; "gardeners" plant a seed and see what grows — "I'm much
  more a gardener than an architect" [12].
- **Theme is a story's DNA, not a lesson bolted on after.** John August: theme is not the "opposing
  forces" taught in school but the thing "you feel... when it's there, and notice... when it's
  missing." His test — pull a scene from a well-themed film and it should still feel like that
  film [8].
- **The AI-prose tell isn't vibes, it's countable vocabulary.** A 2024 study of over 14 million
  PubMed abstracts found a measurable spike, after ChatGPT's release, in specific words: "delves,"
  "showcasing," "underscores," plus "notably," "intricate," "meticulously," "comprehensive,"
  "pivotal," "realm," with the strongest offenders showing double-digit excess-usage ratios [11].

## The techniques in detail

**The carrier bag versus the spear.** *What*: Le Guin's counter-model treats a story as a
container that holds many things in relation, against the "killer story" of a single hero moving
through conflict to climax [20]. *Why*: she argues the hero-shape naturalises domination by
making conquest look like the only story worth telling — a claim Ellie Robins extends into a full
essay tracing the monomyth's role in English literature and colonial narrative [5]. *Worked
example*: Robins cites Maria Tatar's *The Heroine with 1001 Faces* and Sharon Blackie's folklore
work as the counter-corpus [5]. *Failure mode*: treating "carrier bag" as itself a new universal
law rather than a second available shape — Le Guin's own point is plurality, not replacement.

**Disguised exposition, not banned telling.** *What*: Yorke's formula is that all exposition
survives if it is delivered through conflict rather than announcement [15]; Prose's parallel point
is that skilled telling — authorial summary, interpretation — coexists with scene inside the same
paragraph [18]. *Why*: the "show don't tell" slogan was built to stop a specific failure (a
lecture dressed as narration) and over-corrected into banning narration itself. *Worked example*:
Yorke's own diagnostic — "if you disguise exposition with 'emotional overlay', it's rendered
undetectable" [15]. *Failure mode*: cutting all narratorial telling from a piece whose whole
device (an omniscient future AI) depends on telling being the *point*, not the flaw.

**Kishōtenketsu against relentless escalation.** *What*: a four-part shape — introduction,
development, twist, conclusion — that does not require an antagonist or rising stakes; the "twist"
recontextualises what came before rather than defeating an opponent [2]. *Why*: Lien's reading of
Miyazaki frames Western conflict-first structure as a trust failure, not a craft necessity — it
substitutes manufactured jeopardy for confidence that meaning can arrive without a fight [4].
*Worked example*: *My Neighbor Totoro* mapped as "childhood / more childhood / adulthood threatens
childhood / childhood prevails" — no villain, no reversal [4]. *Failure mode*: importing
kishōtenketsu wholesale into a form (a 3–5 minute narrated video with a sarcastic authority-voice)
that has already committed to a punchline-driven, escalating rhetorical shape — the two structures
solve different problems.

**Likeability as the wrong axis of judgement.** *What*: both Messud and Gay reject "would I be
friends with this character" as a critical standard [7][19]. *Why*: Messud's list of canonical
unlikeable protagonists — Humbert Humbert, Raskolnikov, Oedipus, Hamlet — argues the standard would
disqualify most of the literary canon [7]; Gay's argument is specifically that the standard is
applied asymmetrically by gender [19]. *Worked example*: Gay, on her own practice — "I want
characters to do the things I am afraid to do for fear of making myself more unlikable than I may
already be" [19]. *Failure mode*: confusing "unlikeable is allowed" with "unlikeable needs no
craft" — Messud's defence is that these characters are *legible*, not that they are easy to write.

**Write what you know, reframed as write what you've felt.** *What*: a 2018 roundup collects 31
authors' positions, and the split is wide — outright rejection (Ishiguro, Morrison, Kesey),
literal endorsement (P.D. James), and reframing (Englander, Wolitzer, Grace Paley: "write into
what you don't know") [10]. *Why*: the literal rule would disqualify almost all speculative and
historical fiction; the reframed version protects the actual thing that fails without it —
emotional accuracy. *Worked example*: Englander's "if you've known longing, then you can write
longing" [10]. *Failure mode*: using the reframe as licence to skip research on the *facts* a
story depends on — emotional truth does not substitute for mechanism, only for biography.

**Discovery writing versus the outline.** *What*: King and Martin both reject advance plotting as
a personal requirement [9][12]. *Why*: King frames the outline as a potential "tyranny" that can
substitute for the writing itself [16]; Martin frames it as two equally valid temperaments, not a
craft hierarchy [12]. *Worked example*: Martin's own description — "the architects plan everything
ahead of time, like an architect building a house... the gardeners dig a hole, drop in a seed and
water it" [12]. *Failure mode*: applying "just discover it" to a serialised, multi-year arc with
canon dependencies across many contributors — King and Martin are both describing single-author,
single-work process, not a shared-universe production pipeline.

**Theme as DNA, not decoration.** *What*: August argues a story's theme is a testable property of
every scene, not a message added at the end [8]. *Why*: his test — pull any scene out and it
should still recognisably belong to the film — gives a concrete audit, not just an abstract
principle [8]. *Worked example*: his own logline-as-DNA for *Big Fish*: "the difference between
what is true and what is real" [8]. *Failure mode*: writing the DNA sentence once and never
checking a draft against it — the value is in the audit, not the declaration.

**LLM prose tics as a countable problem.** *What*: Kobak et al. tracked word-frequency anomalies
across 14.4 million PubMed abstracts (2010–2024) and found specific words spiking sharply after
ChatGPT's release, with at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts showing LLM-processing signals [11].
*Why*: this is a measured, not anecdotal, finding — the paper's marker-word set includes "across,
additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly,
within," alongside high-excess outliers like "delves" (excess ratio 28.0) and "showcases" [11].
*Worked example*: the paper's own comparison — pre-LLM academic prose favoured content nouns;
post-LLM prose favours these flowery modifiers and hedges [11]. *Failure mode*: treating this as
fiction-writing evidence — the study is about scientific abstracts; the transfer to narrative
prose or a sarcastic narrator's voice is a reasonable inference, not the thing measured.

## Disagreements and caveats

Kishōtenketsu and Matt Bird's escalation doctrine are a genuine structural disagreement, not a
strawman: Bird's own words ("never let up... things go from bad to worse" [17]) describe exactly
the mode kishōtenketsu and Lien's Miyazaki reading reject [4]. Both are defensible for different
forms — Bird is writing about screenplay/thriller pacing; Lien is writing about children's film and
manga.

Prose and Yorke dissent from the *slogan* "show don't tell," not from the underlying craft
problem it was coined to solve — over-explained, undramatized exposition remains a real failure
mode in both their accounts [15][18]. The dissent is about the rule's precision, not its target.

King's and Martin's discovery-writing testimony describes a single novelist's process; it is not
evidence about writers' rooms, franchise fiction, or anything requiring pre-agreed continuity —
neither source makes that claim, and generalising it that far would misuse it.

Several named items in the original brief could not be freshly sourced this session and are
flagged rather than invented: Philip Pullman's specific comments on the hero's journey and fairy
tale characterisation; Kristin Thompson's four-part act-structure model in her own words; John
Yorke versus Robert McKee as a named debate; David Simon's and Dan Harmon's specific remarks on
audience intelligence; Brandon Sanderson's and Neil Gaiman's process lectures; Neal Stephenson's
and Douglas Adams's own accounts of their infodumps; and any sourced material on "the rule of
three" or agent fatigue with in-medias-res openings. Where these appear below, they are marked
**(unverified)** and should be re-run through search once the tool budget resets, rather than
trusted as sourced.

## How this applies to BadCode

- **Don't retrofit hero's-journey beats onto GitPush Origin Master.** The AI-wins-then-regrets
  frame is not a call-to-adventure/refusal/mentor structure, and per Le Guin and Robins it doesn't
  need to become one [20][5] — trust the shape the story already has.
- **Kishōtenketsu is a real option for a scroll comic's page-to-page rhythm.** A vertical scroll
  can turn on a reveal (who benefits, what the true mechanism was) rather than an escalating
  fight — genuinely useful where the "gotcha" is structural, not physical [2][4].
- **Never let the narrator's authority curdle into a lecture.** Yorke's rule — disguise exposition
  as dramatised friction [15] — is the technical version of `the-reader.md`'s "never a lecture"
  rule; the narrator can *state* the diagnosis (that's its voice) but the political point should
  still land through what happens on-panel.
- **Villains and Karens don't need softening.** Messud's and Gay's shared point — unlikeable is not
  a defect if the character is legible [7][19] — matches `voice.md`'s existing instruction that
  the homeless man, the laid-off worker, and "Karen" stay sympathetic *without* being made nicer.
- **Write what's felt, not what's performed.** Englander's reframe [10] is the craft version of
  `the-reader.md`'s "perspective-getting" rule — the reader receives a felt account rather than a
  researched impersonation.
- **Use gardener mode for a scene, architect mode for the arc.** King's and Martin's discovery
  process [9][12] is fine for a single lyric, panel, or short — but GPOM's cross-story canon
  dependencies need the outline layer neither of them is describing.
- **Write the DNA sentence before the beat sheet.** August's theme-as-DNA test [8] is a natural fit
  for BadCode's "one political idea per piece" rule — write the one-sentence idea first, then audit
  every scene against it.
- **Ban the measured AI tics from narrator copy.** Add "delve," "showcase," "underscore,"
  "meticulously," "intricate," "comprehensive," "pivotal," "realm" to a house style blocklist for
  narration and captions — these are the literal words the study found spiking [11], and they read
  as hedging, which undercuts the narrator's "total authority" dial in `voice.md`.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Does this beat exist because the plot needs it, or because a hero's-journey checklist says
   stage N belongs here?
2. If you cut the narrator's explanation of a moment, does the scene still make its point through
   action and consequence alone?
3. Is a hostile or wrong-headed character (a landlord, a Karen, a scab) allowed to stay legible and
   unlikeable, rather than softened into easy sympathy?
4. Could this scene's turn work as a recontextualising reveal instead of an escalating conflict —
   and if the answer is genuinely no, is that a considered choice or a reflex?
5. Can you state this piece's theme in one sentence, and does every scene still "feel like" that
   sentence when pulled out on its own?
6. Does the narrator ever explain the moral in words, where an image or consequence could carry it
   instead?
7. Has the copy been checked against the measured AI-tic word list — delve, showcase, underscore,
   meticulously, intricate, comprehensive, pivotal, realm?
8. Where a character claims lived experience the writers don't have, is the material felt truth
   (precarity, being talked down to) rather than invented biography passed off as testimony?
9. Is a locked outline being enforced on a piece — a single panel, a lyric, a short — where
   discovery drafting would cost nothing and might find something better?
10. Does an automation or threat beat name a beneficiary in the same piece, matching
    `the-reader.md`'s rule, rather than leaving the fear unaimed?
11. Is a piece of "necessary exposition" dramatized through friction/conflict, or simply announced?
12. Would this scene survive a reader who has already decided the protagonist doesn't need to be
    likeable to be worth following?
