# Persuasion Without Sermon: How Stories Change Minds Without Lecturing

## What this covers

The mechanics of carrying a political or economic argument inside a story so a resistant,
distrustful audience absorbs it rather than defends against it: the academic models of why
narrative persuades differently from argument (entertainment-education, the extended
elaboration likelihood model, the overcoming-resistance model), reactance and counterarguing
and how fiction suppresses both, inoculation theory's "weakened dose," the sleeper effect,
satire's two registers (Horatian/Juvenalian), and the Trojan-horse principle of embedding a
claim in consequence rather than dialogue. It uses the "preachy" backlash against *Don't Look
Up* against the acclaim for *The Big Short*, *Network* and *Parasite* as the live case study of
where the line sits, and works out the diagnosis-vs-accusation distinction BadCode's own
reader research already gestures at. It leaves deep case-study readings of individual
political/economic satires to sibling brief 16 — this is the underlying evidence and general
technique, not the worked examples in full.

## Top findings

1. **Character-target displacement lowers reactance and counterarguing.** Moyer-Gusé and
   Nabi found a dramatic teen-pregnancy narrative reduced reactance by fostering parasocial
   interaction and *decreasing perceived persuasive intent* — and identification with the
   character reduced counterarguing directly. The message survives because it happens to
   someone else, not the viewer.
2. **But transportation is not automatically protective.** The same study found narrative
   transportation *increased* counterarguing — the opposite of what entertainment-education
   theory predicts. Immersion and identification are not the same lever, and conflating them
   is a live fault line in the field, not settled fact.
3. **Inoculation works by running the trick on someone else first.** Roozenbeek and van der
   Linden's *Bad News* game has players adopt the manipulator's role against a simulated
   audience, not their own beliefs. Across a 15,000-plus, five-country study this raised
   resistance to real misinformation, *d* = 0.33–0.41, with negligible demographic moderation.
4. **The sleeper effect can make a discredited-seeming source recoverable over time, but it's
   fragile.** Hovland's Yale studies found a message paired with a low-credibility source
   produced *more* attitude change nine weeks later than after five days — the discounting
   cue fades from memory faster than the content. It has since proven "notoriously difficult
   to obtain or replicate," and only appears under narrow conditions.
5. **Swift's "trap": never state the argument, let the narrator's logic curdle until the
   reader supplies the horror.** Scholar Charles K. Smith's reading of *A Modest Proposal*
   describes Swift building sympathy for the Irish and revulsion at the narrator
   simultaneously — the proposer's calm economic voice degrades people "into animals, then
   meat, then tonnage worth a price per pound," and the reader's own disgust *is* the
   argument. The word "exploitation" never appears.
6. **The boots theory is Trojan-horse economics: the general law is never stated, only one
   character's one purchase.** Pratchett's Sam Vimes reasons a $50 pair of boots lasting a
   decade beats ten years of $10 boots replaced annually — "poverty is expensive" is never
   spoken, only derived, from one transaction with real numbers. That paragraph has since been
   cited in academic and policy discussion and produced a real UK cost-of-living measure, the
   Vimes Boots Index.
7. **McKay's *Big Short* trick: reframe density as detective work, not lecture, and let
   humour carry the exposition.** McKay used celebrity cutaways (Bourdain's "three-day-old
   halibut stew" for CDOs, Selena Gomez at a blackjack table) because, in his words, CDOs were
   "so toxic you could spend an hour talking" and lose the audience; he frames the film's
   pleasure as "looking behind the curtain for the first time," not being taught.
8. **Same director, same premise — the difference was tonal discipline, not subject matter.**
   *Don't Look Up* is criticised for abandoning comedy for straight tragedy in its final act
   and stacking too much commentary onto a crowded plot; *The Big Short* kept one register
   (dark comedy) throughout and let the ending's consequences — nobody goes to prison, the same
   instruments get repackaged — carry the point instead of a speech.
9. **"Preachy" is a contested critical judgement, not an objective measure.** Pauline Kael
   called *Network*'s dialogue "preachy" and Chayefsky's tone "self-righteous" the same year
   Vincent Canby called it "brilliantly, cruelly funny" and Roger Ebert later called it
   prescient enough to "outlive a lot of tidier movies." The charge tracks whether the speech
   is dramatised as a specific character's breakdown (Beale, going mad on air) as much as it
   tracks message density.
10. **The comic medium itself may not run on the mechanism the wider literature assumes.** A
    thesis testing an anti-fracking comic against Slater and Rouner's E-ELM found the comic
    *did* persuade, but neither transportation nor identification predicted it — the standard
    prose/TV pathway did not hold. Single thesis, not a settled finding, but directly relevant
    to a comic-first collective.
11. **Propaganda states, theme questions.** Author Stina Leicht: propaganda "demands that you
    agree" and forecloses other readings; message fiction invites the reader to reach their
    own conclusion and can survive a reader who disagrees with it. She cites *A Clockwork
    Orange* as never endorsing either side of its own tension.
12. **Orwell's resolution of the art/propaganda conflict is self-awareness, not neutrality.**
    In "Why I Write" he states every serious line he wrote after 1936 was written "directly or
    indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism" — yet "the more one is
    conscious of one's political bias, the more chance one has of acting politically without
    sacrificing one's aesthetic and intellectual integrity." Declared bias, held consciously,
    is what keeps propaganda from curdling the prose.

## The techniques in detail

### Entertainment-education: the two rival models

**What it is.** Slater and Rouner's extended elaboration likelihood model (E-ELM) treats
absorption and character identification as what suppresses counterarguing by making
persuasive content process like *story*, not *claim*. Moyer-Gusé and Nabi's entertainment
overcoming resistance model (EORM) specifies which resistance gets overcome: reactance (via
reduced perceived intent and parasocial interaction), counterarguing (via identification),
and perceived invulnerability (identification can *increase* felt vulnerability, which is the
goal in health messaging). **Why it works.** A character's misfortune is processed as
something that happened to someone else, a far less defended posture than an argument aimed
at the viewer. **Worked example.** Moyer-Gusé and Nabi's teen-pregnancy drama reduced
reactance and counterarguing versus a non-narrative version of the same facts. **Failure
mode.** The same study is the best evidence the model is incomplete: transportation
*increased* counterarguing in their data — conflating "immersed" with "identified" is a
mistake the field has not resolved.

### Inoculation: show the trick being run on someone else

**What it is.** Prebunking, formalised in Roozenbeek and van der Linden's *Bad News* game:
give people a weakened, exaggerated dose of a manipulation technique, run by them on a
fictional audience, before they meet the real thing. **Why it works.** It builds resistance
to the *technique* rather than refuting one claim, and because the player wields the trick
against strangers in-game, no one has to admit being fooled by it. **Worked example.** The
cross-country study (Sweden, Germany, Greece, Poland; 15,000+ participants) found post-play
headlines rated less reliable, *d* = 0.33–0.41, negligible demographic moderation. **Failure
mode.** It collapses into accusation the moment the "someone else" being fooled looks like the
audience's own reflection.

### The sleeper effect: a discredited source can still land the message

**What it is.** A delayed increase in persuasive effect when a message carries a discounting
cue (a suspect source) that fades from memory faster than the content does. **Why it works.**
Hovland's WWII Army studies found soldiers shifted opinion *more* nine weeks after a
propaganda film than after five days. **Worked example.** Cook and colleagues later showed the
effect only appears when the message is genuinely persuasive, the discount genuinely strong,
and enough time passes — narrow conditions. **Failure mode.** Multiple teams call it
"notoriously difficult to obtain or replicate"; treat it as a real but unreliable tailwind, not
a mechanism to design around.

### Horatian and Juvenalian satire, and Swift's trap

**What it is.** Horatian satire is gentle, implicates the satirist alongside the target, and
aims at a laugh conceding shared folly; Juvenalian satire is bitter and accusatory, aiming for
anger. *A Modest Proposal* is the canonical Juvenalian text: a sincere-sounding proposer
recommends eating Irish infants, in the calm register of a political-economy pamphlet. **Why
it works.** Per scholar Charles K. Smith, Swift's "trap" builds sympathy for the Irish and
revulsion at the narrator in the same motion — the proposer's own language degrades people
"into animals, then meat, then tonnage worth a price per pound," and the reader completes the
sentence himself. **Worked example.** The essay never states its thesis; the thesis is the gap
between tone and content. **Failure mode.** Juvenalian satire misfires when the target is
ambiguous or the sincerity breaks too early — spot the irony in sentence one and there is no
trap left, only a lecture in costume.

### The Trojan-horse principle: the boots theory and *The Big Short*

**What it is.** Encode the general claim entirely inside one specific, numbered instance and
never say the general claim aloud. **Why it works.** A stated thesis is dismissible in one
move; a concrete transaction must be individually refuted, and a reader who derives the
conclusion owns it more than one handed it. **Worked example.** Vimes never says "poverty is
expensive" — he compares a $10 boot replaced yearly to a $50 boot lasting a decade, and that
paragraph produced the Vimes Boots Index. McKay's equivalent is the celebrity cutaway:
Bourdain's fish-stew CDO explanation stayed inside a joke while, per reporting, satisfying
real finance professionals as structurally accurate. **Failure mode.** The moment the cutaway
*narrates the moral* instead of *performing the mechanism*, the horse is empty — a speech with
production values.

### Diagnosis versus accusation

**What it is.** A diagnosis names what is wrong and who benefits; an accusation tells the
audience they were fooled, weak, or complicit. **Why it works.** This is the seam under
several findings above: displacing the message onto a character reduces perceived intent;
inoculation insists the trick be demonstrated on someone else; Leicht's statement/question
split works the same way. The instant a story's target becomes the viewer's own past
judgement, resistance reactivates. **Worked example.** *Parasite*'s reception (its critics,
per Wikipedia — A. O. Scott, Jessica Kiang) praises how it communicates class anger through
architecture and plot mechanics rather than any character stating the film's thesis — furious,
but structurally furious, never addressed at the audience. **Failure mode.** "You've been
duped" is accusation wearing a diagnosis costume — it names a victim's own past belief as the
problem, which turns persuasion into a fight.

### Message fiction versus propaganda, and Orwell's plain language

**What it is.** Leicht: "It's the distinction between a question and a statement" — propaganda
forecloses other readings, message fiction survives disagreement (her example: *A Clockwork
Orange* resolves its central tension toward neither side). Orwell's "Why I Write" names four
motives (egoism, aesthetic pleasure, historical record, political purpose) reconciled only by
staying conscious of one's own bias rather than pretending to have none; "Politics and the
English Language" argues vague, euphemistic prose is a political failing, not a stylistic one
— it's how atrocity gets made sayable ("pacification" for bombing villages). **Why it works.**
Corrupted thought produces debased language, and debased language then corrupts thought
further — precision of prose does persuasive work, it doesn't just decorate it. **Worked
example.** Orwell's six rules (no stock metaphor, no long word where a short one will do, cut
what can be cut, prefer active voice, avoid jargon, break any rule sooner than sound
barbarous) are tools for detecting one's own dishonesty, not just improving style. **Failure
mode.** A piece can hit every stylistic mark of plain, active, concrete language and still be
propaganda if the plainness states a conclusion rather than making the mechanism legible; and
the line between "explores a tension" and "refuses to have a position" needs Orwell's declared,
conscious bias to keep it from collapsing into mush.

## Disagreements and caveats

- **E-ELM and EORM disagree on what transportation does.** Slater and Rouner treat immersion
  as protective against counterarguing; Moyer-Gusé and Nabi's own test found transportation
  *increased* it. Unresolved tension inside peer-reviewed research — don't treat "get them
  immersed" as a safe blanket strategy.
- **The sleeper effect is real but fragile,** repeatedly called hard to replicate before
  researchers narrowed the conditions under which it appears. Grade peer-reviewed but
  contested, not a reliable lever.
- **"Preachy" is a contested critical judgement, not a craft metric.** *Network* was called
  preachy and brilliant/prescient by different critics in the same season — the charge tracks
  whether critics already resist a film's politics as much as it tracks message density, which
  BadCode should expect regardless of craft.
- **The comics finding is one thesis, not a body of evidence.** A single, unreplicated study
  found persuasion without the mechanism the wider prose/TV literature assumes — a genuinely
  relevant result for a comic-first collective, but a hypothesis worth testing, not settled.
- **Effect sizes across this literature are modest.** *d* = 0.33–0.41 for inoculation games is
  at the better end; much entertainment-education research reports smaller shifts, consistent
  with BadCode's own honest-limits framing elsewhere (durable effects around *d* ≈ 0.04–0.08).
  The bet is cumulative presence, not conversion events.
- **Horatian versus Juvenalian is a spectrum, and sources disagree on which "works" better.**
  *A Modest Proposal* as Juvenalian is well attested; broader claims about which register
  converts more reliably were not found in a source strong enough to cite here.

## How this applies to BadCode

1. **Route every idea through one character's one costed transaction before it becomes a
   narrator line.** Before the AI narrator gets a sentence about automation, find the
   boots-theory version: one laid-off character's next three real purchases, real numbers.
2. **Reserve the narrator's direct claims for cosmic scene-setting, never the climax.** Let
   the point land as consequence — the way *The Big Short*'s point is that nobody goes to
   prison, not a line saying so — and hold narrator certainty for an epilogue-style beat after
   the feeling has landed, where McKay places his closing text cards.
3. **Dramatise the trick on a background character, never the reader's avatar.** Already
   the-reader.md's rule 2; ground it in the inoculation mechanism explicitly — a scam or bad-
   faith pitch working on someone the audience isn't rooting for, so the viewer learns to spot
   it without being told they fell for it.
4. **Keep one comic register end to end — don't do a *Don't Look Up* in the last third.** The
   clearest, most falsifiable failure mode found here is McKay's own: stay darkly funny to the
   credits. Never go sincere-without-irony in the closing beats even when the beat is heavy —
   carry the sarcasm dial (voice.md's dial 1) through and let care (dial 4) ride underneath,
   not replace it.
5. **Don't assume "transported" equals "working" for the scroll comic specifically.** The one
   comics-specific study suggests persuasion may not run on transportation/identification the
   way TV and prose do. Build every panel to carry weight alone — a strong image, a number, a
   legible mechanism — rather than betting the whole load on cumulative immersion.
6. **Use the narrator's own suspect status on purpose, and don't hide it.** A superintelligence
   admitting it caused humanity's near-death is an obvious discounting cue — lean into that
   disclosure (using-ai.md already does), because the sleeper effect suggests content can
   outlast an audience's initial distrust once the specific reason for it fades from memory.
7. **In lyrics, one costed image beats one stated theme per verse.** A specific number, name,
   or transaction, not a chorus stating "the game is rigged" — save the stated line for the
   narrator's asides, not the verses carrying the argument.
8. **Assign satirical register by character, not by story.** Give the reader's avatar
   (Bob-type characters) Horatian treatment — gentle, self-implicating — inside a piece that
   is Juvenalian toward the named institutional villain. This operationalises the-reader.md's
   "never punch down" with a named technique.
9. **Run the question-vs-statement test on every scene before it locks.** Could someone who
   disagrees with BadCode's politics still feel this character's specific loss, independent of
   agreeing with the thesis? If a scene only works for a viewer who already agrees, rewrite it.
10. **Write the mechanism sentence, then delete it and check the story still proves it.** Draft
    the plain-English political point privately (short words, active voice, no euphemism) as a
    clarity check only — then confirm the finished piece never says that sentence aloud.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Is the political point ever stated as a general claim, or does it exist only as consequence?
2. Is there one character's one specific, numbered transaction doing the work of the abstract
   point (the boots-theory test)?
3. Is the trick demonstrated on a third party before — or instead of — being pinned on the
   reader's own past belief?
4. Does the piece ever imply the reader/Bob has been "duped" or wrong to believe what they do?
5. Is a specific decision-maker and beneficiary named, rather than "the system"/"the machines"
   gestured at as inevitable?
6. If the narrator states something plainly, does it land after the emotional consequence,
   rather than instead of one?
7. Does the piece keep one tonal register to the end, or drop humour for sincerity late on?
8. Would someone who disagrees with BadCode's politics still feel this character's specific
   loss, independent of the argument?
9. Could an unsympathetic reader summarise this piece's core line as "you've been fooled"?
10. Is there at least one panel/shot/line strong enough to carry the point on a skim?
11. Does the satirical target get Juvenalian treatment while the reader's avatar gets Horatian?
12. Could the most "on the nose" line be deleted and the point survive in image/consequence?
13. Does the ending show a concrete image of who benefits, not only who suffers?
14. Is there a specific, checkable number, name or mechanism doing the work, not just a mood?
15. If this is inoculation-shaped, is the target of the trick clearly not the reader's own
    reflection?
