# 03 - Colour design - palette as argument

## 1. The short answer

- Plan colour for the *whole piece* before you make a single frame - decide what each beat should feel like in colour, then build every shot to serve that plan, rather than grading shot-by-shot after the fact.
- A palette that removes colours is doing more work than one that adds them - the colours left standing carry the meaning because nothing competes with them.
- Give one colour to one idea, character, or era, and only let it appear when that thing is present - the audience learns the code without being told, and its return becomes a signal.
- Warm-versus-cool ("temperature") contrast inside a single frame - a warm light source against a cold ambient, or vice versa - reads instantly as "someone lit this on purpose," which is most of what a viewer means by "production value."
- Teal-and-orange is not a colour theory law, it is a 2000s trend that started as a genuine complementary-contrast solution and became a stylistic default through commercial habit, VFX-compositing convenience (orange skin reads against blue-lit backgrounds), and time pressure on colourists - and by now it signals "generic" rather than "cinematic" to anyone who has clocked the pattern.
- Complementary-colour contrast (opposite hues, e.g. teal/orange, red/green) is a real optical fact - opposite hues do produce maximum simultaneous contrast - but that fact does not license the specific claim that "orange pushes forward, blue recedes"; that add-on is folklore riding on a real base fact.
- Colour-emotion links are not pure invention: a large, cross-cultural body of controlled-lab evidence supports broad associations (dark = negative/sad, bright/warm = positive/high-arousal, red = both anger and passion depending on context) - but almost all of that evidence is single-colour-on-a-blank-background, so it tells you about a swatch, not about a graded, lit, composed frame.
- Desaturation and monochrome are choices with a cost (they remove the audience's biggest source of instant mood information), so they need a reason - "we ran out of colour ideas" is not a colour script, it is an absence of one.
- The strongest colour-as-story-marker examples (a colour tagged to a character or theme) work because the tag is rare and consistent, not because of what the colour "means" in the abstract - the code is arbitrary and learned, like a flag, not decoded from the colour wheel.

## 2. The findings

**Pixar's "colour script" plans a film's whole emotional arc in colour before a frame is animated.** Grade: **P** (practitioner practice, widely documented, single clear origin). Ralph Eggleston, art director on *Toy Story* (1995), introduced the technique to Pixar - pastel thumbnails on black paper mapping the sequence of the entire film by colour, temperature, value and saturation rather than by story event. It has since been adopted at Disney Animation, DreamWorks, Sony Pictures Animation and Blue Sky, and Pixar has published full colour scripts for films through *The Art of Pixar* books. Sources: Cartoon Brew obituary of Eggleston, MoMA's *Toy Story* colour-script exhibit note, Amid Amidi (*The Art of Pixar*) interview via Animated Views.

**Deakins plans colour and tonal relationships across a whole film, but resists a fixed formula for how scenes should alternate.** Grade: **p** (one practitioner, his own forum, in his own words as summarized by the site). On his own site's forum, Roger Deakins confirms he is conscious of "the relationship between each scene and the overall flow of the visuals as the story develops," and that his method varies by production: *Blade Runner 2049* had lighting concepts drawn out well before shooting, while *Empire of Light* was worked scene-by-scene because it relied on real daylight. He also references building a "mood board" of visual references per scene on *The Assassination of Jesse James*. Source: rogerdeakins.com/forums, "Colors/Tone/Contrasts alternation along a movie" thread.

**Deakins used colour to track time-of-day as a deliberate structural device on *1917*.** Grade: **p**, and *(unverified in sweep - drawn from a search-engine summary of secondary reporting, not confirmed against Deakins' own words in this pass)*. The claimed structure - cool blue dawn, neutral morning, warm afternoon, golden dusk, cold night firelight - is a well-known reading of the film's single-take structure, but this pass did not verify the specific wording as Deakins' own; treat it as a plausible but unconfirmed practitioner claim until traced to a primary interview.

**A restricted palette can carry as much dramatic weight as a saturated one.** Grade: **p** (case-study consensus around a single well-known film, not a named-practitioner quote in this sweep). *Se7en* (dir. Fincher, DP Darius Khondji) is the standard example cited across film-craft writing for a desaturated, near-monochrome palette used to sustain dread rather than to decorate scenes. This sweep did not reach a primary Khondji or Fincher interview stating the rationale in their own words - treat the mechanism (restriction concentrates meaning) as sound craft logic, but the *Se7en* attribution specifically as secondary until traced to a primary source.

**Complementary colours (opposite on the colour wheel) produce the strongest simultaneous contrast the eye can register.** Grade: **A** (optical/perceptual fact, this part is textbook colour science, not folklore - simultaneous contrast between opposite hues is a long-established, measurable perceptual effect, not exclusive to film). The film-specific overlay - that this is *why* teal/orange works, and that orange dominates because human skin sits in orange hues so it reads cleanly against a blue-graded background - is **P**, practitioner-level technical reasoning repeated consistently across colourist-facing writing, not a controlled study of audience response.

**Teal-and-orange became the default Hollywood grade through a mix of real technique and commercial convenience, and by ~15 years of saturation it now reads as a cliché to attentive viewers.** Grade: **p/mixed** - this sweep's sources are secondary journalism (Priceonomics) rather than a named colourist's own statement about intent, so treat the causal story (time pressure on colourists, "makes it look like a movie") as a plausible trade explanation, not a confirmed fact. The trend's rise following the increased use of digital intermediates and grading through the 2000s is well documented; the *specific* claim that it originated with Michael Bay's *Bad Boys II* and colourist Stefan Sonnenfeld surfaced in earlier search results but was not confirmed against a primary source in this pass - flag as **unverified in sweep**.

**Colour-emotion associations have real, replicated, cross-cultural empirical support - but only for single colours on blank backgrounds, not for graded film frames.** Grade: **A** for the base finding, explicitly **not A** for applying it to cinematography. Jonauskaite & Mohr, University of Lausanne, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (2025 per the summarizing source - note the DOI resolves to 2024 in the URL, so treat the exact publication year as unconfirmed pending primary-text access), reviewed 132 peer-reviewed studies from 1895–2022 across 42,000+ participants in 64 countries. Findings with the strongest, most consistent support: yellow/white → joy, calm, positivity (highest consistency, ~90%+ in yellow's case); dark colours (black, grey, brown) → sadness, fear, negativity; warm colours (orange, red) → high-arousal positive emotion. Weaker/contested: blue is genuinely ambivalent - linked to sadness in over half of cases and to calm in under half, not cleanly "calming" as pop colour-psychology claims; purple is similarly mixed. Critically, the review's own scope explicitly **excludes** children, clinical populations, and real-world/contextual stimuli - it is evidence about isolated colour swatches judged in lab conditions, and the review itself flags that context, lighting, and competing colours in real scenes can overpower a single hue's effect. This is the single most important caveat for this brief: a graded, lit, composed film frame is not a swatch, and no study in this review tested one.

**Red carries two opposed strong associations depending on context (anger vs. passion/romance), not one fixed meaning.** Grade: **A** per the same review - 73% of studies linked red to anger, while a separate cluster linked it to romance/passion. This directly undercuts single-word folk claims like "red means passion" - the same review that is the best evidence for colour-emotion links is also the best evidence that any single-word gloss on a colour is an oversimplification of what the data shows.

## 3. What survives our constraints

- **The colour script itself survives completely, and is arguably more useful to us than to a live-action production.** We generate stills and short clips one at a time with no continuous shoot to keep consistent - a written colour plan (what colour/temperature each beat of a comic page, film, or short is built around) is the discipline that keeps a set of independently-generated images from drifting into incoherence. This is cheap to do (a document, not a shoot) and should be mandatory for any narrated film or multi-panel comic.
- **Restricted/limited palettes survive and are a net *advantage* under our constraints.** A tighter palette gives a generative model fewer places to drift off-brief between panels or clips, and gives us fewer variables to fight when judging whether a generation is "on model." This aligns directly with the existing house register (near-black, one thin light).
- **Colour as a story marker (tagging a colour to a character/idea/era) survives and is cheap.** It requires no crew, no reshoot - just consistency of instruction across separate generations, which is a discipline problem, not a production problem.
- **Warm/cool contrast within a single frame survives on a still or a generated clip** - it is a lighting/scene-description decision, not a blocking or performance decision, so it can be specified for a single generated image exactly as it would be lit on a set.
- **Desaturation/monochrome as a deliberate choice survives entirely** - it's a global instruction to the whole piece, arguably easier for us than colour-consistency across many separate generations, since removing colour removes one axis we'd otherwise have to hold constant.
- **What does not survive:** the on-set craft of achieving warm/cool contrast practically (gelling a practical light one temperature while daylight or another source reads another) is a lighting-department skill that doesn't transfer - for us the "how" collapses to a written scene description, and the "why" (it reads as intentional, produced) is the only part that matters. Likewise, colour continuity built up over a shooting day (matching grade across dozens of angles of the same scene) doesn't apply - we're not matching coverage, we're specifying one shot at a time against a written plan.
- **The Pixar-style *evolution* of a colour script during story development** (iterating the colour plan as the story changes, thumbnail by thumbnail, over months) is real practice but heavier process than we need - our version should be a short written colour brief per story/film, revisited when the beats change, not a maintained art-department deliverable.

## 4. Contested

- **Is a fixed colour-alternation formula (e.g. always cool-then-warm, or acts mapped to hues) good craft, or a trap?** No practitioner in this sweep argued *for* a rigid formula. Deakins explicitly resists one, saying the approach depends on the film and is often decided scene-by-scene against story and location rather than a preset scheme (rogerdeakins.com forum). This sweep found no named counter-voice arguing for a fixed alternation rule as superior - so record it as Deakins' position standing largely unopposed in the sources reached, not as a two-sided debate.
- **Is teal-and-orange a legitimate technique unfairly maligned, or a genuine creative failure?** The sources gathered here present both framings without resolving them to named individuals taking opposite sides: one line of argument (color-theory-grounded) says the technique is sound complementary contrast, properly used for separating skin tones from backgrounds, and the backlash is really a complaint about *overuse*, not about the technique; another line (critical/journalistic) treats the ubiquity itself as evidence of creative default, driven by time-pressured grading rather than intent. This sweep did not find a named colourist and a named critic in direct disagreement - it found the "tool not a rule" framing appearing as an unattributed synthesis in search summaries, which is a sign this specific debate needs primary-source verification before it's treated as settled.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"Red means passion / blue means calm / [colour] means [single emotion]" as a universal, fixed code.** The best available evidence (Jonauskaite & Mohr's review) shows red splits roughly 73% anger vs. a separate strong romance/passion cluster - both real, both context-dependent, not one fixed meaning. Blue is explicitly ambivalent in the data, not reliably calming. Treat any single-word colour-to-emotion mapping as myth-grade unless the specific study and context are named.
- **"Complementary colours mean orange objects visually advance and blue objects recede" as a film-grading law.** The underlying optical fact (opposite hues create maximum simultaneous contrast) is real; the specific "advance/recede" framing as applied to grading decisions is practitioner shorthand repeated in content-mill writing, not a cited perceptual study in this sweep. Use the contrast fact; drop the advance/recede dressing unless a primary optics source is found.
- **Colour-psychology claims sourced to "colour psychology" websites/PDFs generally (e.g. the Dewey Color System material that surfaced in search results).** Commercial colour-psychology material routinely overstates lab findings as universal, actionable rules and blends in uncited folklore. None of that tier was used as a source in section 2 above and none should be cited in any BadCode copy.
- **A single, agreed origin story for teal-and-orange (e.g. "invented by colourist X on film Y").** This sweep found the claim in secondary search-result summaries but could not verify it against a primary interview or credited account - do not repeat a specific inventor/origin-film claim without independently confirming it.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| term | plain-English gloss | what it buys you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colour script | a whole-film colour plan, mapped before you shoot/generate anything | keeps every separately-made image or clip pulling toward one emotional arc instead of drifting |
| Restricted/limited palette | deliberately using fewer colours than you could | makes the colours you do use carry more weight, and gives a generative model fewer ways to go off-brief |
| Complementary contrast | opposite hues on the colour wheel placed together (e.g. teal/orange, red/green) | the strongest optical "pop" available - use sparingly, it reads as an effect the moment it's noticed |
| Colour temperature contrast | mixing a warm light source against a cool ambient (or vice versa) in one frame | the single fastest way to make a shot read as deliberately lit rather than flat/found footage |
| Colour marker / colour tag | assigning one colour to one character, idea, or era and holding it consistent | a silent, learnable code the audience picks up without being told - its return becomes a signal |
| Desaturation | pulling colour intensity down globally, as a choice not a default | removes the audience's fastest mood cue on purpose, usually to signal dread, memory, or bleakness - needs a reason |
| Simultaneous contrast | the optical effect where a colour looks more intense next to its opposite hue | the real science underneath "complementary colours pop" - genuine, measurable, not folklore |
| Valence / arousal | the two axes psychology actually measures colour-emotion on (good-bad, calm-excited) | the honest, evidence-backed vocabulary to use instead of "red means X" |

- The exact origin claim for teal-and-orange (Sonnenfeld / *Bad Boys II*) was not verified against a primary source and should be treated as unconfirmed - a targeted search on American Cinematographer or a Sonnenfeld interview would settle it.
- The Jonauskaite & Mohr review's precise publication year (2024 vs. 2025) was not confirmed against the primary paywalled text - worth a direct check if the citation needs to be exact.
- Nothing in this sweep tested colour-emotion association on a *composed, graded film frame* rather than a single swatch - this is a real gap in the literature itself, not just this sweep, and is worth stating plainly whenever the psychology evidence is invoked for a cinematography claim.
- Whether named practitioners actually disagree on teal-and-orange (rather than the "debate" being a synthesis of unattributed opinions) was not resolved - worth one more targeted search on American Cinematographer or a colourist trade interview if this specific contested-claim needs to hold up under scrutiny.
