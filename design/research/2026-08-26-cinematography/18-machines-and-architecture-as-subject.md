# 18 - Machines and architecture as the subject

## 1. The short answer

- Architecture and machines can be photographed with the same status/power grammar as a body — but the leading tradition (the Bechers) achieves menace by *removing* all rhetoric, not adding it: frontal, shadowless, dead-centre, identical distance, no drama. **Grade P** (this is a whole named school, the "Düsseldorf School," not one photographer).
- Converging verticals (a building leaning inward as you tilt the camera up) reads as unstable/dynamic; keeping verticals dead straight reads as monumental/permanent/institutional. This is an optical fact of lens geometry, not a style opinion — **Grade A** for the geometry, **P** for the convention that straight = authority (the whole discipline of architectural photography exists to correct or exploit this one distortion).
- Overcast, directionless light removes shadow-drama and produces a flat, "specimen" read (menace-by-indifference); a single low hard source (golden/blue hour) produces shadow-drama and a "monumental/awe" read. These are two different registers, not two versions of the same shot — pick deliberately. **Grade P.**
- Scale for a non-human subject has to be manufactured in-frame — there is no default sense of "how big" a machine or building is without a known-size reference object (a doorway, a stair, a human figure, a vehicle) somewhere in the shot. **Grade P**, but the underlying comparison-based size perception is a well-established finding in visual perception research (relative-size and familiar-size cues), so treat the *mechanism* as **A** and the *photographic application* as **P**.
- A wide, distant, elevated (aerial-leaning) view of an empty space reads as **geometry**, not **absence** — the eerie "someone should be here" feeling needs the camera close enough, and low enough, that the space's human-scaled details (a doorway's proportion to a body, a stair's rise, a desk's height) stay legible. **Grade A (mixed)** — grounded in a real empirical finding (Diel & Lewis 2022) and a named theoretical account (Mark Fisher's "the eerie" as "failure of presence"), reasoned onto BadCode's specific aerial-vs-eye-level case, which no source states directly — flag the extension as our own synthesis, not a cited finding.
- Industrial/machine photography that reads as *sublime* (Burtynsky) versus *menacing* (Alien's Nostromo) versus *indifferent* (the Bechers) versus *exhausted* (Salgado) is a choice about **distance + scale-crowding + colour**, not about the machine itself — the same gold mine or factory can be shot into any of those four registers. **Grade P**, drawn from comparing four independently-established, named bodies of work.
- Dust and haze in the air (not just light on surfaces) is doing real optical work: it scatters light, softens contrast at distance, and is one of the primary depth cues human vision uses to judge how far away something enormous is (aerial perspective / atmospheric perspective) — this is a long-established, empirically grounded principle of vision science and traditional landscape painting alike. **Grade A.**
- Material reads light differently and each reading carries a legible mood: raw concrete holds shadow in its texture and reads as mass/permanence (the Brutalist photography literature makes this explicit); polished steel and glass reflect and fragment the light source itself, multiplying and confusing it, which reads as cold/inhuman/corporate; dust in the air scatters and softens, reading as neglect, age, abandonment. **Grade p** for the specific three-way claim (reasoned from named sources on Brutalism + general optics), **A** for the underlying optics of specular versus diffuse reflection.
- This is the brief in the sweep that should survive our constraints best: none of it requires a performer, a rehearsal, or continuity across multiple cut angles — a building or a machine holds still and does not need directing, so almost every finding here is directly specifiable as a single-shot composition instruction. See §3.

## 2. The findings

**The Becher method: remove all photographic rhetoric and the subject alone carries the meaning.** — Grade **P** (a whole recognized school of photography, not a single photographer's idiosyncratic choice). Bernd and Hilla Becher photographed water towers, blast furnaces, grain silos and gas tanks — always frontal or side elevation, always under flat overcast skies (no shadows), always from a fixed, repeated distance, always with no people in frame — and grouped the results into grids they called "typologies," explicitly framing the buildings as **"anonymous sculpture."** The absence of any dramatic lighting or angle is the point: comparison across the grid does the work that drama would otherwise do. They founded what's known as the Düsseldorf School and directly influenced generations of documentary and fine-art industrial photography. Source: [Tate, "Who are Hilla and Bernd Becher?"](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/artists/bernd-becher-and-hilla-becher-718/who-are-bechers); [Artsy, "Hilla and Bernd Becher's Photographs Turned Industrial Architecture into Fine Art"](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-photographer-couple-turned-industrial-architecture-fine-art).

**Julius Shulman and the founding generation of architectural photography treated the corrected (non-converging) vertical as the discipline's baseline grammar, achieved with camera movements (view-camera front/back tilts, later tilt-shift lenses) rather than by tilting the camera up at the subject.** — Grade **P**. Shulman, along with Ezra Stoller and Julius Baldassari (name uncertain in sourcing — treat as p on the third name), is credited as one of the photographers who effectively invented the genre of modern architectural photography; correcting converging verticals (which appear if you simply tilt an ordinary camera upward at a tall building) is described across the craft literature as the default professional discipline, not an optional cleanup step. Deliberately keeping the convergence — letting the building appear to lean or tower over the lens — is the exception used for effect (instability, threat, a subject looming), not the default. Source: [About Photography, "Julius Shulman: Architectural Photography and the Allure of Mid-Century Modernism"](https://aboutphotography.blog/photographer/julius-shulman); [PhotoEphemeris, "Architectural Photography, Part 3: Fixing Converging Verticals"](https://photoephemeris.com/en/articles/architectural-photography-part-3-fixing-converging-verticals/) *(craft/practitioner-tier sources; the underlying optical geometry of vertical convergence under a tilted lens is a plain fact of perspective projection, not in dispute)*.

**Blue hour (roughly 20-40 minutes after sunset, sun 4-8° below the horizon) is the specific window architectural photographers name as best for buildings, not golden hour — because even, directionless twilight lets exterior structure and interior/artificial light sit in the same exposure without the harsh shadow-play golden hour produces.** — Grade **p** (craft-consensus content, not independently corroborated by named individuals; treat this specific hour-of-day recommendation as trade convention, not tested finding). Golden hour is preferred instead for texture-revealing subjects (landscapes, materials, surface detail) where raking shadow is wanted. Source: [The Foreign Architect, "The magic hours that will make your photography shine"](https://theforeignarchitect.com/blog/magic-hours-will-make-photography-shine/); [SkyWall Photography, "Best Times of Day for Capturing Stunning Architectural Shots"](https://www.skywallphotography.com/times-of-day-for-capturing-architecture/) — **both content-mill tier**, no named individual practitioner on record; downgrade accordingly.

**Scale for a monumental non-human subject is manufactured, not inherent — a lone human figure placed against a brutalist building is a standing convention specifically because the building is otherwise unreadable in size.** — Grade **p**, one named case: Alexey Kozhenkov's brutalist series *Spaces for Winds* is built around isolated human figures looking up at massive concrete structures explicitly "to emphasize the sense of scale" — without the figure, the same photograph of raw concrete reads as abstract texture, not as a monumental building. The general perceptual mechanism behind this — that humans judge unfamiliar-object size by comparison to known-size reference objects in the same frame (familiar-size and relative-size cues) — is well-established in classical visual-perception research, though this specific sweep did not pull a primary psychology citation for it (flagged *unverified in sweep*, treat the mechanism as textbook-standard rather than freshly sourced here). Source: [ArchDaily, "Colour, Composition and Scale: Analysing Brutalist Photography"](https://www.archdaily.com/994163/colour-composition-and-scale-analysing-brutalist-photography).

**Aerial/distant/elevated views of an empty human-designed space read as geometry or pattern, not as absence — a documented instance of the same phenomenon appears in Salgado's *Serra Pelada* photographs, where 50,000 workers shot from a high, distant vantage are described by critics as reading like "ants," "dots," or "an intricate abstract design" before resolving into human activity — the elevation itself is what converts bodies into pattern.** — Grade **p** for the specific critical description, applied here as an analogy for BadCode's *opposite* problem (we want the emptiness of a space built for people to register as absence, and this shows the same elevation/distance choice erases legibility of the human element in the frame, whether the humans are present in huge numbers or entirely missing). Source: [Public Delivery, "Serra Pelada Gold Mine, Brazil, 1986"](https://publicdelivery.org/sebastiao-salgado-serra-pelada-gold-mine-brazil/).

**The theoretical account of why an empty, human-designed space feels eerie ("liminal space" imagery) rests on the space still being legible as built for a body — familiar structural patterns (doorway proportions, furniture height, corridor width) that a viewer's visual system checks against expectation.** — Grade **A (mixed)**: Diel & Lewis (2022, peer-reviewed) found that "deviations from familiar structural patterns can produce an uncanny-valley response to built environments" — a controlled empirical study, though single-study and specific to structural deviation rather than emptiness per se, so graded A(mixed) not flat A. Separately, cultural theorist Mark Fisher's concept of "the eerie" is invoked (via Peter Heft's application) as a "failure of presence" — a familiar setting appearing outside its expected context. **Neither source discusses aerial versus eye-level framing directly — this sweep found no craft or research literature that names BadCode's specific finding (that an aerial view cannot carry the emptiness effect).** Reasoning it through from these two sources: both mechanisms depend on the viewer recognizing the space *as a place a body belongs*, at a scale where doorway-to-body, chair-to-body, or corridor-width-to-body proportions are still legible. An aerial or very distant view abstracts those proportions into plan-view geometry — the same transformation that turns Salgado's fifty thousand miners into "dots" — and a viewer cannot check "a body should be standing there" against a shape with no legible body-scale reference. This is our own synthesis, not a stated finding — flag it as such if it goes further into canon. Source: [Wikipedia, "Liminal space"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liminal_space), citing Diel, A. & Lewis, M. (2022) and Mark Fisher via Peter Heft (Pulse: The Journal of Science and Culture) *(secondary aggregation — go to the Diel & Lewis paper directly and to Fisher's *The Weird and the Eerie* before this claim is load-bearing for a whole sequence)*.

**The Nostromo (*Alien*, 1979) was designed as an industrial working ship, not a menacing spaceship — Ron Cobb and Chris Foss conceived it as an orbital refinery, translating real terrestrial infrastructure (power-plant piping, valves, grilles, cable runs) directly into the set, and the menace comes from the ship having no aesthetic order at all: it is a machine a human has been inserted into, not a space designed around a human.** — Grade **p**, one named production-design account (uncorroborated beyond a single secondary source this sweep, the primary Domus article was unreachable — *unverified in sweep, re-fetch before quoting directly*). This is the specific, checkable mechanism worth carrying forward: menace in machine-space design comes from the machine plainly not having been built for the body now standing in it — no ergonomic concession, exposed function instead of concealed function, wear and use-marks instead of finish. Source: [Domus, "Spaceships, ruins, and corporate cities: the menacing architecture of Alien"](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2025/09/19/alien-design-nostromo.html) *(fetch blocked in-session; claim reported via search snippet, not primary text — re-verify before quoting)*.

**Industrial photography's four legible registers — indifferent (Becher), sublime/awe (Burtynsky), exhausted/laborious (Salgado), menacing (Alien's Nostromo) — are produced by different choices about distance, crowding, and colour applied to structurally similar subjects (factories, mines, refineries, machine interiors), not by anything inherent to the machine itself.** — Grade **P** (four independently named, well-documented bodies of work compared directly): the Bechers strip drama out entirely (see above); Burtynsky shoots vast man-made landscapes (quarries, tailings ponds, shipbreaking yards) from a scale-crowding elevated distance specifically so "vast granite quarries reduce mammoth excavators to tinker toys," working "with a journalistic objectivity" that critics say is built to "inspire awe, dread, and guilt" simultaneously; Salgado's *Serra Pelada* crowds fifty thousand human bodies into one frame, shot from height, producing a Bruegel-like, hellish-labour read; Cobb/Foss's Nostromo achieves menace through function without concession to the body inside it. Source: [Edward Burtynsky official site, "Manufactured Landscapes"](https://www.edwardburtynsky.com/projects/films/manufactured-landscapes); [Public Delivery, Serra Pelada](https://publicdelivery.org/sebastiao-salgado-serra-pelada-gold-mine-brazil/); Becher and Domus sources above.

## 3. What survives our constraints

This is the strongest-surviving brief in the sweep, and it is worth being blunt about why: buildings and machines are static, un-directable subjects by nature — none of the film-crew apparatus (blocking, rehearsal, coverage, continuity across cuts) that dies in other briefs was ever needed for this subject in the first place, even for a real film crew.

**Survives whole, directly specifiable in a still or one 8-second clip:**
- Camera angle relative to verticals (corrected/straight = monumental; deliberately converged = unstable/looming) — a pure composition choice.
- Distance and crowding (Bechers' fixed-distance frontal shot vs. Burtynsky/Salgado's scale-crowding elevated distance) — pick the register up front.
- The scale-reference rule: if the shot needs to read as *big*, put a known-size object (a doorway, a stair, a human figure, a vehicle, a desk) somewhere in frame at a legible distance — without it, scale collapses into abstraction.
- Time-of-day / light quality (flat overcast "specimen" read vs. blue-hour even twilight vs. golden-hour raking texture) — fully specifiable as a lighting instruction.
- Material choice and how it's lit (raw concrete for mass/permanence, polished steel/glass for cold fragmentation, dust/haze for neglect and depth) — a set-dressing and lighting decision, nothing more.
- The Nostromo mechanism (menace = machine with no ergonomic concession to the body) — directly writable as a design brief for a machine interior: exposed function, wear marks, nothing scaled to comfort.
- The emptiness-reads-as-absence mechanism — keep the camera close and low enough that human-scale proportions (doorway height, desk height, stair rise) stay legible in frame; an aerial or very distant view converts the same empty space into geometry instead. This directly explains BadCode's own discovered failure case and gives a concrete fix: **when a sequence needs a room to feel like people should be in it, shoot from inside the room, near eye level, not from above it.**

**Dies or needs no translation because it was never a film-crew problem:** nothing here required a location scout convincing a facility to let a crew in, permits, weather delays, or reshoots for continuity — a generated or archive-sourced still of a machine does not need any of the coordination overhead architectural photographers actually spend their time on (access negotiation, insurance, structural engineers signing off on rooftop vantage points). The **craft findings above are the entire deliverable**; the practical friction of real-world architectural photography (which is considerable, and most of the trade literature is actually about that friction, not composition) is simply not our problem.

**One genuine gap, not solved by any source found here:** none of the material located this sweep gives an empirically tested account of *how much* human-scale legibility a frame needs before the "should be someone here" read kicks in (a doorway alone? a chair? two objects minimum?) — the fix above (shoot close, shoot low) is directionally solid from the Diel & Lewis / Fisher reasoning but the specific threshold is our synthesis, not a cited finding, and worth testing empirically against BadCode's own generated frames rather than trusting further.

## 4. Contested

No two named practitioners were found disagreeing directly on a shared claim in this sweep — the four industrial-photography registers (Becher/Burtynsky/Salgado/Nostromo) are not in tension with each other, they are different tools for different jobs, and each source treats its own approach as the right one for its own purpose rather than arguing against the others. The one live tension worth flagging is **implicit, not stated by any source**: architectural-photography craft convention treats corrected verticals as the professional default (Shulman lineage) while BadCode's own house register (per brief 13) leans on deliberate symmetry-plus-single-hard-light for monumentalism — these are compatible (corrected verticals plus dramatic single-source lighting is exactly the Soviet-monumentalist grammar brief 13 already documents), but no source in this sweep states the combination as a deliberate choice; it is our inference from combining two separate craft traditions.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"Aerial/drone shots are the natural way to show scale."** The Salgado evidence points the opposite direction: extreme elevation and distance convert human activity into abstract pattern (ants, dots) rather than legible scale — scale needs a *proximate*, comparable reference object, not altitude.
- **"Wide shots are inherently more 'epic' or 'cinematic' for architecture."** The Becher counter-example is the whole point of their career: their frontal, fixed-distance, undramatic method is the most influential industrial-photography approach of the 20th century, and it deliberately avoids anything resembling an "epic" wide shot.
- **"An empty room is automatically eerie/liminal."** The liminal-space literature (Diel & Lewis; Fisher via Heft) is explicit that the effect depends on *structural deviation from familiar patterns* and *failure of an expected presence in a legible, human-scaled context* — plain emptiness with no expectation set up (an empty field, an empty aerial view) does not automatically produce the effect; a recognizable human-designed space does.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typology (Becher sense) | Photographing the same category of structure from an identical, repeatable, undramatic vantage | Lets the *subject itself* (and the pattern across a series) carry meaning instead of a dramatic angle doing the work |
| Converging verticals | A tall structure appearing to lean inward when the camera tilts up at it | A controllable dial: correct it for "monumental and permanent," let it converge for "unstable, looming, about to fall" |
| Blue hour / golden hour | Even post-sunset twilight vs. warm, low, raking pre-sunset light | Blue hour for buildings that need to read whole and calm; golden hour when you want surface texture and shadow drama |
| Familiar-size / relative-size cue | A known-size object in frame lets the eye judge an unknown object's real size | The only reliable way to make a machine or building register as "huge" rather than just "big-ish" |
| Aerial perspective (atmospheric perspective) | Haze and dust between camera and subject soften contrast and desaturate distant elements | A free depth cue for enormous interiors/exteriors — put particulate in the air, not just light on the walls |
| Failure of presence (Fisher's "the eerie") | A space that visibly expects an occupant who is not there | The theoretical name for the exact effect a BadCode "empty machine hall" shot is going for |
| Anonymous sculpture (Becher's term) | Treating an industrial structure as a found artwork rather than as a set | A framing device: light and compose the machine the way you would a sculpture in a gallery, not a location in a story |
