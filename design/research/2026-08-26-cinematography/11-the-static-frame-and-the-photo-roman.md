# 11 - The static frame - making stillness gripping

## 1. The short answer

- A still only holds attention if it contains an unresolved question — a gesture mid-way,
  a gaze aimed somewhere, a body about to fall or about to speak. Resting poses die fast.
- Voice-over and image should not agree with each other on timing. If the narration
  describes exactly what's on screen exactly when it appears, the still has nothing left
  to do. Let the words arrive early, late, or sideways of the image — the reader does the
  join, and the join is where the feeling lives (this is literally how comics work, see §6).
- One real moment of motion after a long stretch of stillness is worth ten scattered
  motion beats — it converts because it's rare, not because it's flashy. La Jetée spends
  ~26 minutes as photographs and cashes its one moving shot exactly once.
- The "decisive moment" is not about catching action — Cartier-Bresson meant the moment
  when the *composition itself* resolves into meaning, geometry and event locking together
  at once. A still that has strong composition but no story beat is furniture regardless
  of what's happening in it.
- A caption or VO line only needs to name what the eye can't get from the frame alone
  (motive, backstory, irony) — never what the frame already shows. Redundant narration is
  the single fastest way to kill a still image's tension.
- Pan-and-zoom on a still (the "Ken Burns effect") does not manufacture drama that isn't
  in the composition already — it only reveals drama that was staged into the frame in
  advance. Used to disguise a boring photo, it reads as exactly that.
- A still that shows an unstable arrangement — off-balance body, object about to tip,
  two figures not looking at each other — reads as "before" or "during," which is what
  makes a frozen frame feel like it's still moving.
- The gutter (the *gap* between two images) is where a comic reader constructs cause,
  effect, and elapsed time — not the panels themselves. What you cut *out* between two
  stills does more storytelling work than what you put in either one.
- Duration has no fixed number — the shot dies the instant it's finished delivering
  information or feeling, and that moment is set by content, not a stopwatch. A talking
  head with new information every second earns 6-8s; a wide establishing beat with nothing
  left to read past 2s should cut at 2s.
- A held image over narration works like a comic panel with a caption box, not like film
  coverage — the reader/viewer scans it actively while listening, so composition needs a
  clear entry point and a place for the eye to land when the sentence ends.

## 2. The findings

**La Jetée builds its entire film from ~422 still photographs, filmed and re-photographed
with a rostrum camera, at roughly one image per few seconds.** Grade: p (single primary
case study, extensively analysed but not an empirical study). Chris Marker called it a
"photo-roman" (photo-novel). Source: [Criterion Collection](https://www.criterion.com/films/329-la-jetee), [Senses of Cinema](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2015/feature-articles/la-jetee/).

**The film's single moment of real motion — a woman's eyes blinking awake — works because
of scarcity, not spectacle.** Grade: p. Film scholar Vivian Sobchack (quoted/analysed in
Senses of Cinema's 2015 piece by Steven Woodward) argues the shift is ontological: "everything
radically changes, and we and the image are reoriented in relation to each other... the
space between the camera's gaze and the woman becomes suddenly habitable." The single
motion beat converts because everything before it trained the eye to expect stillness.
Source: [Senses of Cinema, "Surprised by La Jetée"](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2015/feature-articles/la-jetee/) *(secondary — reports Sobchack's argument, primary Sobchack text not reached in this sweep, unverified in sweep)*.

**Marker's voice-over deliberately does not sync with what the still shows — it runs
ahead of or beside the image rather than describing it, which is what keeps the sequence
tense rather than illustrative.** Grade: p. Ned Schantz (quoted in the same Senses of
Cinema piece) argues the images "always verge on surprise encounters" because narration
never fully prepares the viewer for them. Source: same as above *(unverified in sweep —
Schantz's original text not reached)*.

**Cartier-Bresson's "decisive moment" is widely misread as "catch the action at its peak"
when his own definition is about composition and event resolving together, not motion
caught mid-air.** Grade: p (single primary quote, but the quote is Cartier-Bresson's own
words, which is as good as this kind of claim gets). His stated definition: "the simultaneous
recognition, in a fraction of a second, of the significance of an event as well as of a
precise organization of forms which give that event its proper expression." Source:
[PetaPixel, "The Decisive Moment: What Henri Cartier-Bresson Actually Meant"](https://petapixel.com/the-decisive-moment/).
Note: the English title itself is a mistranslation of his book's French title (*Images à la
sauvette*, roughly "images on the run") — the popular meaning was baked in by an editor, not
by Cartier-Bresson. *(Translation-history claim unverified in sweep — reported by PetaPixel,
primary French title not independently checked here.)*

**Gregory Crewdson and Jeff Wall build single photographs with full film-crew staging —
lighting rigs, actors, sets, sometimes weeks of prep for one frame — explicitly to compress
narrative that would normally take an entire film into one image.** Grade: P (practitioner
consensus/critical consensus; both artists are widely discussed the same way across sources,
though this sweep did not reach a primary interview for either). Their images are read as
having "no beginning or end" — the story is implied entirely by staged tension inside the
frame (an unmade bed, a figure facing away, an object out of place). Source:
[Aesthetica, "Jeff Wall: Master of the Photographic Tableau"](https://aestheticamagazine.com/jeff-wall-master-ofthe-photographic-tableau/); [ProEdu on Crewdson](https://proedu.com/blogs/photographer-spotlight/gregory-crewdson-cinematic-staged-photography-and-surrealism-capturing-suburban-dreamscapes) *(both secondary/magazine sources, not primary artist statements — grade accordingly)*.

**Scott McCloud's six panel-transition types (moment-to-moment, action-to-action,
subject-to-subject, scene-to-scene, aspect-to-aspect, non sequitur) and his concept of
"closure" — the reader mentally completing what happens in the gutter between two panels —
is the most rigorous available account of how static images build motion and time without
moving.** Grade: P (widely taught, cited, and used as a working framework across comics
scholarship and practice, though McCloud's own taxonomy has been empirically challenged —
see §4). "Closure is the phenomenon of observing the parts but perceiving the whole" —
McCloud, *Understanding Comics* (1993). Source: [Wikipedia summary of Understanding Comics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding_Comics); [Visual Language Lab, Neil Cohn's critique](https://www.visuallanguagelab.com/P/NC_Time&Transitions.pdf).

**The roman-photo / fotoromanzo (photo-novel using real photographs instead of drawings,
with speech bubbles and captions) is a mid-20th-century mass-market form, born in Italy in
1947 (Stefano Reda) and exported to France as roman-photo by 1949, that is the direct
ancestor of what BadCode does with stills-plus-captions.** Grade: p/P (historical fact,
multiple encyclopaedic sources agree, but genre-history claims are thinly sourced overall).
Its own lineage runs back through the ciné-roman (stills narrated by extracts of a film
script). Source: [Britannica, "Photo-roman"](https://www.britannica.com/technology/photo-roman); [cine-books.com history](https://cine-books.com/news/blog/from-a-roman-photo-to-a-photo-story).

**There is no fixed "safe" shot duration — practitioners describe cutting the instant a
shot stops earning its place, with published ranges only as loose defaults per format
(talking-head ~4-8s, fast commentary 1-3s, short-form social 1-2s).** Grade: p (practitioner
rule-of-thumb repeated across editing-advice sources, none of it experimentally tested in
what this sweep reached). The strongest formulation found: "cut a shot the moment it stops
earning its place, and not a frame before." Source: [Blackbird, "Are the rules of editing
still valid?"](https://www.blackbird.video/archive/editing/rules-editing-still-valid/) *(this is an editing-tool company blog — treat as practitioner-adjacent, not authoritative)*.

**The Ken Burns effect is respected as a documentary convention but is criticised, in the
same breath, as a "lazy artist" shortcut when the underlying photograph does not itself
contain a compositional reason to move the frame.** Grade: p (this sweep reached only
tertiary sources — TV Tropes-style pages — repeating a widely-held practitioner view; no
named editor or critic's original essay was reached). Source: [TV Tropes, "The Ken Burns
Effect"](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheKenBurnsEffect) *(low-grade —
crowd-sourced wiki, not a critical source; treat as a pointer to the consensus, not the
consensus itself)*. **This claim needs a better primary source before it is asserted with
confidence — flagged, not fully verified.**

## 3. What survives our constraints

Nearly all of this survives, because BadCode's dominant form (a still under narration, an
8-second AI clip with one simple move, or archive footage we didn't shoot) *is* the form
these sources are describing — a single generated or found frame has to do the work a whole
scene would, exactly like a La Jetée photograph, a Crewdson tableau, or a comic panel.

- **Survives whole**: the decisive-moment discipline (compose for the moment where meaning
  and geometry lock, not the moment with the most visible action); McCloud's closure and
  panel-transition taxonomy (this is literally the mechanic BadCode uses between comic
  panels, and is a direct model for what to leave out of a caption or narration line so the
  reader/viewer does the joining work); the Crewdson/Wall discipline of staging a single
  frame to imply a story with no beginning or end (this is exactly what an AI-generated
  still can be art-directed to do — no crew needed, since the "crew" is prompt-craft, which
  is out of scope here but the staging *logic* — what to put in frame to create implied
  before/after — is pure human craft and fully portable); the caption/VO-timing discipline
  from La Jetée (never let narration describe what the image already shows).
- **Survives with translation**: "shoot for the decisive moment" assumed a photographer
  waiting for a real unrepeatable event; for us it becomes "art-direct/prompt/select the
  candidate still where the composition resolves a story beat," and the judgement (does
  this frame answer or raise a question?) transfers even though the mechanics of getting
  there don't.
- **Dies or needs heavy translation**: nothing here actually required a crew, actors, or a
  shooting day (that's the point of this brief — it's the still/photo-craft lineage, not
  blocking-and-coverage lineage) — so unusually for this sweep, very little is lost. The
  one place our constraints bite is Ken Burns critique: our "camera move" on a still is
  usually a single AI-directed pan/zoom decided once, not an editor's live judgement call
  frame-by-frame — so the discipline of "only move if the photo justifies it" applies even
  more strictly to us, because we don't get a second pass at cutting the move short.

## 4. Contested

- **McCloud's six-category panel-transition taxonomy vs. empirical comics-cognition
  research.** McCloud (practitioner-theorist) proposed the six transition types as a
  near-complete descriptive system. Neil Cohn, a cognitive scientist studying visual
  narrative (Visual Language Lab), has published work arguing the categories don't hold up
  under empirical testing of how readers actually process panel sequences, and that comics
  comprehension is better modelled with a grammar of narrative *roles* (Establisher, Initial,
  Peak, Release) than with McCloud's transition types. Source:
  [Visual Language Lab, "Time & Transitions"](https://www.visuallanguagelab.com/P/NC_Time&Transitions.pdf).
  For BadCode's purposes both are useful — McCloud's vocabulary is more intuitive for a
  writer/artist deciding what to put in a panel; Cohn's narrative-role model is more useful
  for deciding *pacing* (which panel is the peak, which is the release) — but they should not
  be cited as if they agree.
- **Ken Burns effect: convention vs. crutch.** No two named practitioners were found
  disagreeing directly in this sweep's source set — the "lazy artist" framing and the
  "respected documentary tool" framing were reported side-by-side in the same tertiary
  source without named advocates on either side. Flagging this as an area where the sweep
  did not find primary voices to cite by name; treat the disagreement as real but unattributed.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"The decisive moment means catching peak physical action."** Cartier-Bresson's own
  definition is about composition and event-significance resolving together at once, not
  about timing a jump or a gesture at its most extreme point. This is the most-repeated
  misreading in photography and is directly contradicted by his own words.
- **"Ken Burns invented the pan-and-zoom-on-a-still technique."** The technique long
  predates his documentaries (it's a standard rostrum-camera/animation-stand move going back
  to early 20th-century filmmaking); it's named after him purely because of his popularising
  use of it in *The Civil War* (1990), not because he originated it. *(Not directly sourced
  in this sweep's search results — flagging as a commonly-stated caveat worth verifying
  before use, not as a confirmed finding.)*
- **"A still image can't carry tension, only cuts and movement can."** Directly contradicted
  by the entire La Jetée case and the Crewdson/Wall tableau tradition — tension in a still
  comes from unresolved composition, not from motion.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closure | The viewer mentally fills the gap between two images | Lets you cut a beat entirely and trust the audience to supply it |
| Gutter | The blank space/cut between two panels or shots | Names *where* the storytelling work actually happens — not in the frames, between them |
| Decisive moment | The instant composition and event-meaning lock together | A concrete target when choosing which candidate still to keep |
| Tableau | A single staged frame built to imply a whole story | The Crewdson/Wall model for making one still do a scene's work |
| Implied motion | A frozen pose that reads as mid-action | Lets a still feel kinetic without literal movement |
| Photo-roman / roman-photo | A story told in captioned still photographs | The direct historical ancestor of BadCode's comic-plus-VO form |
| Moment-to-moment / action-to-action / subject-to-subject / scene-to-scene / aspect-to-aspect / non sequitur | McCloud's six ways one panel can relate to the next | A checklist for what kind of jump a cut or panel change is making, and whether it's the right one |
| Establisher / Initial / Peak / Release | Cohn's narrative-role grammar for panel sequencing | A pacing tool — tells you which panel should be the "peak" the sequence is building to |

## 7. Open questions

- What does the primary Sobchack and Schantz scholarship (not the Senses of Cinema
  secondary summary) actually argue in full — this sweep read a summary of their claims,
  not their original texts.
- Is there a named, citable critical essay (not a wiki page) making the "Ken Burns effect
  as crutch" argument explicitly, with reasoning about why it fails? This sweep could not
  find one within budget.
- Cartier-Bresson's book-title mistranslation claim (*Images à la sauvette* → "The Decisive
  Moment") needs a primary bibliographic check.
- Neil Cohn's empirical critique of McCloud deserves a deeper read — his narrative-grammar
  model (Establisher/Initial/Peak/Release) may be the more directly useful pacing tool for
  BadCode's comics and could be its own future brief.
- Nothing was found quantifying how long a *specific* narrated still (as opposed to a
  generic video shot) can hold before attention drops — the duration guidance in this
  brief is generic editing advice, not still-image-under-narration research specifically.
