# 02 - Lighting design

## 1. The short answer

- Light is a decision about the story, not decoration on top of it — where it comes from and what it hides *is* the meaning. (P)
- Three-point lighting (key/fill/back) is a teaching diagram, not a rule. Working DPs routinely drop the fill entirely and let one side of the face go dark. (P)
- A lighting **ratio** (how much brighter the key is than the fill) is the actual control knob for mood: low ratio (roughly 2:1) reads calm/natural, high ratio (8:1+) reads dramatic/morally ambiguous. (P, with numeric conventions graded lower — see §2)
- **Hard light** (small/distant source, sharp-edged shadow) reads as harsh, exposing, unforgiving. **Soft light** (large/close source, gradual shadow falloff) reads as flattering, gentle, safe. This is optical fact, not taste. (A)
- Where light hits the face carries meaning before a word is spoken: front = flat/honest, side (45°) = classic dimensional modeling, three-quarter and back = mystery/threat, under-lighting = horror/unnatural, silhouette = anonymity/myth. (P)
- **Motivated light** — light the eye can trace to a source that's plausibly in the scene (a window, a lamp, a screen) — is what makes a stylized frame still feel *true*. Ungrounded light reads as "lit," which breaks the illusion for a modern eye trained on realism. (P)
- The single biggest practical finding for BadCode: **darkness reads as depth, not as void, only when there is one small, deliberately-placed bright detail inside it** — an eye-light, a rim, a practical. Pure uniform blackness with nothing lit reads as a technical failure (a crushed file, dead pixels), not as mood. (P, converging from Willis, Deakins, Young — independently)
- Underexposing on purpose is a legitimate creative choice, not a mistake to fix in post — but every named source who does it also says: decide it at capture/generation time and commit, don't try to rescue exposure after the fact. (P)
- Darkness is not neutral on skin: dark skin tones need *less* light than the reflex "add fill" reaction, because overexposing them destroys the very tonal richness that reads as dignity and presence on screen. (p — one practitioner, but stated forcefully and repeatedly)
- Rim light and silhouette are the tool for making a *black* subject readable against a *black* background — separation by edge-light, not by lightening the whole frame. (P)
- Night-for-night (lit to look genuinely dark) versus day-for-night (bright material graded to look dark) are different problems: the first fights noise/mud, the second fights giveaway shadows and blown skies. BadCode is always doing the equivalent of night-for-night. (P)

## 2. The findings

**Gordon Willis rejected fill light on principle, and used the shadow itself as the subject's moral information.** Grade **P**. On *The Godfather*, Willis lit faces from directly above or hard from the side, and refused the industry-standard practice of adding a fill source to illuminate the shadow side of an actor's face. Brando's eyes disappearing into shadow under the overhead key was originally a practical fix (it hid the prosthetic aging makeup) but Willis kept and extended the technique because it let him withhold information from the audience exactly when a character was concealing something. Coppola on the effect: "the lens was very selective in what it showed, and each sequence was like building something out of bricks." Willis was nicknamed "Prince of Darkness" by fellow DP Conrad Hall for this refusal to fully illuminate a face. Source: [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/gordon-willis-the-godfather), [TCM](https://www.tcm.com/articles/021611/cinematogra-phy-by-gordon-willis), [British Cinematographer](https://britishcinematographer.co.uk/gordon-willis-asc-the-godfather-trilogy/). *(Note: none of the fetched secondary sources carried Willis's own verbatim technical explanation of how he protected shadow detail from crushing to mud — the "how" below is inferred from the ratio/exposure literature, not from Willis's own words. Flagged unverified in sweep.)*

**Roger Deakins: commit to the dark look at the point of capture, don't try to fix it later.** Grade **P**, one named practitioner, direct quote from his own public forum. Asked whether to expose a low-key scene for a balanced/flat image and darken it in the grade, Deakins answered: "I'm totally with you here. I try to shoot the image the way I want it to be in the final film. Make the decision when you shoot not in post." Source: [rogerdeakins.com forum, "About low key lighting"](https://www.rogerdeakins.com/forums/topic/about-low-key-lighting/).

**Deakins treats lighting ratio as scene-specific, not a house rule.** Grade **P**. On his own forum he has said he sees no reason to lock to a fixed ratio across a film — every shot needs its own contrast decision, adjustable with a meter or false-colour monitoring. Source: [rogerdeakins.com forum, "Lighting Ratios"](https://www.rogerdeakins.com/forums/topic/lighting-ratios/) *(title/topic confirmed via search index; full thread text not independently re-fetched — unverified in sweep for exact wording)*.

**Deakins deliberately crushes shadow detail as a contrast decision, not an accident.** Grade **P**. He has described having ample shadow detail available on well-exposed low-ASA stock, then choosing a skip-bleach print process specifically to push blacks down and increase contrast — i.e., "muddy" versus "clean" black is a decision made *after* detail exists, by choosing to discard it for effect. Source: [rogerdeakins.com forum](https://www.rogerdeakins.com/forums/) (aggregated via search; not independently re-fetched, **unverified in sweep**).

**Bradford Young: underexposing dark skin makes it read richer, not worse — overexposing it is the actual disservice.** Grade **p** (single practitioner, but a repeated, deliberate, widely-quoted position). Young, in his own words: "When you underexpose [dark brown skin tones], they pop and resonate and shine in a particular way that you're not going to see when a face is lit in a conventional way," and "you're doing black folk a great disservice when you overexpose their skin." His technique is to bounce light rather than aim it directly, and to lean on practicals — a lamp already in the scene, moonlight through a window — rather than adding unmotivated instruments. He frames this explicitly as continuing work by cinematographers Arthur Jafa and Malik Sayeed. Source: [Colorlines](https://colorlines.com/articles/cinematographer-bradford-young-lighting-dark-skin-and-subversive-power-black-church), corroborated by [CBC Arts](https://www.cbc.ca/arts/selma-cinematographer-bradford-young-s-refreshing-approach-to-lighting-black-skin-1.3270970).

**Three-point lighting is taught as a formula but practiced as a starting point most DPs deviate from.** Grade **P**, converging secondary consensus (not one named individual, but many, including Deakins by demonstrated practice — see above). The scheme (key = main light source and its direction; fill = softens/lifts the shadow key creates; back/rim = separates subject from background by edge-lighting hair/shoulders) is real vocabulary, but treating the three lights as mandatory on every setup is a film-school simplification. Deakins is repeatedly cited as an example of a DP who strips to a single motivated source and lets darkness carry the frame instead of filling it in. Source: aggregated secondary consensus, e.g. [Wolfcrow](https://wolfcrow.com/what-is-three-point-lighting-and-why-do-we-use-it/) (**content-mill, low grade — vocabulary only, not evidence**).

**Lighting ratio has conventional numeric bands, but the numbers themselves are pedagogical shorthand, not measured science.** Grade **p/myth-adjacent** — treat carefully. Common convention: 2:1 (key twice as bright as fill) reads as moderate, naturalistic contrast; 4:1 reads as dramatic, common in television drama and fashion; 8:1 and beyond reads as noir/horror-level stylization, often paired with near-absent fill. These bands are a widely repeated teaching convention with real optical basis (log-scale light falloff is genuinely how contrast perception works) but the specific numbers are not independently verified against a named cinematographer's own stated ratios in this sweep — treat as **useful vocabulary, not a rule to follow literally**. Source: [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/lighting-ratios/) (**content-mill — low grade**), cross-referenced against [Wikipedia: Lighting ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting_ratio) (tertiary).

**Emmanuel Lubezki treats artificial light as a trust-breaker and builds production around natural/motivated sources instead.** Grade **P**, one named practitioner but stated across multiple productions and interviews (*The Revenant*, *Birdman*, *The Tree of Life*). Lubezki has said artificial light "looks fake" to him and "takes people out of the story," and describes deliberately scheduling shoots around the sun, moon, and available practicals rather than lighting instruments. On *The Revenant*: "We wanted to make a movie that was immersive and visceral. The idea of using natural light came because we wanted the audience to feel, I hope, that this stuff is really happening." Source: [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/cinematography-insights-emmanuel-lubezki/) quoting Lubezki, corroborated by [Cinema Escapist](https://www.cinemaescapist.com/2018/12/emmanuel-lubezki-mexican-cinematographer/). *(Secondary sourcing — direct Lubezki interview not independently re-fetched this sweep, flagged unverified in sweep for exact attribution of the Variety quote.)*

**Hard vs. soft light is an optical fact about source size relative to subject, not a style label.** Grade **A**. A light source's apparent size (relative to the subject, i.e. distance matters as much as physical size) determines shadow edge sharpness: small/far source = hard light, sharp shadow edge, reveals texture and flaws, reads as exposing or aggressive; large/near source = soft light, gradual shadow transition, flatters and smooths, reads as gentle or safe. This is basic physical optics (inverse relationship between angular source size and penumbra width), not a matter of practitioner opinion — every lighting textbook and working DP agrees on the mechanism even where they disagree on when to use it.

## 3. What survives our constraints

BadCode generates a still, or an 8-second clip, from a text/image prompt — there is no set, no instruments, no ability to add a second light. What survives:

- **Everything about composition and reading of a *given* lighting state survives fully.** Judging whether a generated frame has a motivated source, a legible key direction, and a usable ratio is a pure judgment call we can make on any still — no crew needed. This is the actual job: not designing light with instruments, but *selecting or regenerating* until the model's output embodies these principles.
- **Ratio-as-mood-language survives as a brief to judge against**, not as a light meter. We can't dial "4:1" into a generative model directly (that's prompt-craft, out of scope here), but we can *look at a candidate frame* and say "the fill is too even, this needs to look more like 8:1" and know to reject/regenerate.
- **Motivated light survives and matters more for us, not less.** Because a generated frame has no real physical light source, the *visible logic* of where light comes from is the only thing anchoring it as a believable scene rather than a rendered object. A near-black frame with one lamp, one screen-glow, one shaft of window light reads as real; the same frame lit "evenly from nowhere" reads as CG. This is the single most actionable finding for our register.
- **Willis's "withhold the face" technique survives directly onto a still held under narration** — a character's eyes lost in shadow is exactly as effective held for four seconds as it is moving.
- **Young's "underexpose, don't overexpose" principle survives as a judgment rule for any character render**, especially since BadCode's whole visual register already lives in near-black.
- **What does NOT survive**: anything that depends on *building* the ratio physically — placing a second unit, bouncing a source, metering a face, adjusting on the day. We cannot add fill; we can only accept or reject what the model gave us, or redescribe the scene and regenerate. Similarly, Lubezki's location-and-schedule discipline (waiting for the right hour, the right weather) has no equivalent — we don't control "when" a generated scene is lit, only what we describe.
- **Night-for-night vs. day-for-night survives as a diagnostic**, not a shooting choice: when a generated "night" frame looks murky/noisy, that's the night-for-night failure mode (equivalent to underlit real film stock); when it looks like a daytime frame with a dark filter slapped on (blown-out sky, hard un-nighttime shadows), that's the day-for-night failure mode. Knowing the two failure signatures helps us name what's wrong with a candidate frame and ask for the other one.

## 4. Contested

- **Fill light: essential vs. a crutch.** Classical three-point teaching (and most run-of-the-mill professional practice) treats fill as necessary to keep shadow detail printable/visible. Gordon Willis explicitly rejected this on *The Godfather*, using hard key with no fill to let one side of the face vanish entirely, arguing the missing information *was* the story. Both are real, coexisting practices — the disagreement is about whether shadow-without-detail is a technical failure to correct or a narrative tool to exploit. BadCode's own aesthetic sides with Willis, but should know it is taking a side, not stating a consensus.
- **Underexposure as a fixed rule vs. scene-by-scene.** Bradford Young states underexposure as close to a general principle for lighting dark skin. Deakins, on lighting ratios generally, explicitly refuses any fixed rule and insists every shot needs its own contrast decision. These aren't strictly opposed (Young is talking about skin tone specifically, Deakins about ratio generally) but they represent two different postures — "I have a strong default" vs. "there is no default" — worth naming as different temperaments among top-tier DPs, not a single practitioner consensus.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"Three-point lighting is how you light a shot."** It's a teaching diagram for beginners to understand light *roles* (key/fill/back), not a checklist every professional frame satisfies. Multiple top DPs (Deakins, Willis) build acclaimed work by deliberately omitting fill or back light.
- **"There's a correct lighting ratio for a given genre" (e.g., "comedy = 2:1, horror = 8:1").** The numeric bands are a pedagogical convention repeated by content-mill sites, not a rule any named cinematographer states as binding. Genre correlates loosely with contrast in practice, but treating it as a lookup table misrepresents how any of the sourced practitioners actually work — they describe deciding ratio per shot, per story beat.
- **"Underexposing = a mistake you fix in the grade."** Widely assumed by amateurs; explicitly contradicted by Deakins ("make the decision when you shoot, not in post") and by Young's entire technique, both of whom treat underexposure as an intentional, committed creative choice.
- **"Dark scenes just need more fill light so you can see what's happening."** This is the instinct several of these practitioners are pushing back against directly — more fill reads as flat, cheap, and destroys the exact tonal richness (per Young) or narrative withholding (per Willis) that made the shadow worth having.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Key light | The main, brightest light shaping the subject | Establishes direction and the base "story" of where light comes from |
| Fill light | A secondary, weaker light that softens the key's shadow | Controls how much of the shadow side stays visible — or doesn't |
| Backlight / rim light | Light from behind the subject, edging silhouette or hair | Separates a dark subject from a dark background without brightening either |
| Lighting ratio | How many times brighter the key is than the fill | A single number that maps roughly to how "dramatic" vs. "natural" a frame reads |
| Hard light | Small/distant source, sharp-edged shadow | Texture, exposure, harshness — nothing is flattered |
| Soft light | Large/near source, gradual shadow falloff | Flattery, gentleness, safety |
| Motivated light | Light the eye can trace to a plausible source in-scene (lamp, window, screen) | Makes a stylized or dark frame still feel physically true, not "lit from nowhere" |
| Practical | A light source that is itself visible in the frame (a lamp, a candle, a screen) | Doubles as both light source and set dressing — grounds the scene fast |
| Low-key lighting | Overall dark frame, high ratio, small pools of light | Mystery, threat, moral ambiguity — BadCode's default register |
| High-key lighting | Overall bright frame, low ratio, minimal shadow | Openness, safety, sometimes irony when paired with dark subject matter |
| Silhouette | Subject rendered as a dark shape against a brighter background, no interior detail | Anonymity, myth-scale, universality — the subject becomes "anyone" |
| Night-for-night | Actually shooting/lighting a scene dark to look like night | The failure mode to watch for is noise/mud, not underexposure itself |
| Day-for-night | Shooting bright and grading/filtering down to fake night | The failure mode to watch for is a blown sky or shadows that don't match true night |

## 7. Open questions

- No verified, primary, first-person account was found in this sweep of *how* Gordon Willis technically protected shadow detail from crushing into mud on film stock (versus simply choosing to let it crush) — his ASC interviews or the American Cinematographer archive would likely settle this but weren't reached within budget.
- The exact numeric ratio conventions (2:1/4:1/8:1) trace only to secondary/content-mill sources in this sweep; no named DP was found stating "I use an 8:1 ratio for X reason" verbatim. Worth a follow-up pass specifically through American Cinematographer interviews if BadCode ever needs to defend a specific number rather than the general mood-mapping.
- Deakins forum quotes on "Lighting Ratios" and shadow-crushing via skip-bleach were surfaced only through the search engine's summarization of the forum, not independently re-fetched and read in full — the exact wording should be treated as approximate until re-verified.
- Not researched this sweep, flagged for a follow-up: the specific craft of avoiding "digital mud" (banding, macroblocking, noise) when a *generated* (not filmed) image is asked for near-black tones — this is a genuinely new problem (a diffusion/video model's failure mode in dark regions, not a film stock's), and no cinematography-craft source can speak to it since it's a property of the generator, not of light. This would need to sit in a different brief (image-model behavior), not this one, per the assignment's tool-agnostic boundary.
