# 09 - Shot progression and sequence design

## 1. The short answer

- A scene is not a pile of pretty shots — it is a small argument, and every shot should be a step in that argument, not decoration.
- Pick a **direction** for shot size across the scene (wide→close is the default "we are moving toward the truth" shape) and hold it; random size-jumping between shots reads as chaos because the viewer can't tell what the change means.
- Establish **geography** early (one wide, one time only) so every closer shot afterward can spend its whole budget on emotion instead of re-explaining where people are.
- Decide what the audience is **not** allowed to see yet, and for how long — a reveal is a withheld shot, not a special effect.
- Suspense beats surprise: tell the audience the bomb is under the table before it goes off, don't just detonate it (Hitchcock).
- Coverage (shoot everything, decide in the edit) and a **designed shot list** (each shot has a job, decided before the day) are opposite philosophies — Fincher's stated preference is to know exactly which shots he needs and not let the format default to over-shooting.
- End a scene on a **button** — one shot, often a small one, that closes the emotional or informational transaction the scene opened. It's punctuation, not a random last frame.
- Set a **pattern** (a repeating shot rhythm, size, or cutting tempo) specifically so you can **break** it once, at the moment that matters — the break only reads as a break because the pattern existed.
- A montage sequence is itself a small scene with its own shape (setup → escalation → resolution shot), not just "some clips set to music."
- Two adjacent shots don't need a literal relationship — juxtaposition alone manufactures meaning in the viewer's head (Kuleshov). This is the mechanism a designed sequence exploits on purpose.
- None of this requires a crew: it is a decision about **order and selection**, which survives fully onto a stack of stills or single AI-generated clips.

## 2. The findings

**Suspense is created by giving the audience information the character doesn't have, not by withholding it — "surprise" and "suspense" are different tools with different payoffs.** Grade **P** (single named source, but the account is a canonical, oft-cited, primary-recorded interview, and its logic is airtight and unrebutted in the craft literature). Hitchcock, to the American Film Institute (1970): two people at a table, an unseen bomb goes off — that's 15 seconds of *surprise*. Same scene, but the audience is shown the bomb and told it goes off in 5 minutes — same dull conversation is now "fascinating" for 15 minutes, because the audience is "participating in the scene." His corollary rule: **if you show the bomb, it must not go off** as the audience expects — the suspense payoff is the anticipation and the earned relief/subversion, not the bang itself. Multiple secondary write-ups (Medium, No Film School, TheScriptLab) all cite the same AFI interview as the sole primary source. https://medium.com/@nathan.baugh/the-bomb-under-the-table-12c83ce8259a ; https://nofilmschool.com/screenwriting-lessons-on-suspense

**Two adjacent shots generate meaning the individual shots don't contain — the Kuleshov effect.** Grade **A (mixed)** — genuinely a documented 1910s–20s experiment by Lev Kuleshov (and popularized by Pudovkin's later account of it), replicated informally many times since, but the *original* footage is lost and what's cited today is largely secondhand description, so treat the specific "hunger/grief/desire" story as illustrative rather than a precise replicated dataset. The robust part — that viewers infer relationship and emotion from adjacency that isn't in either shot alone — is now also supported by modern eye-tracking/cognitive film studies (not sourced here, flagged as a gap). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect

**Eisenstein's montage theory: meaning comes from the *collision* between shots (thesis/antithesis → a third idea in the viewer's head), not from smooth continuity.** Grade **P** — this is film-theory doctrine, traceable to Eisenstein's own writing (*Film Form*), not an experiment; it is a director's stated method, hugely influential, but a theory/manifesto, not measured evidence. Practically: a designed sequence can use one deliberately jarring juxtaposition to force an idea into existence that neither shot states on its own — this is the mechanism behind "the reveal" and behind political/ironic intercutting. https://nofilmschool.com/sergei-eisenstein-soviet-montage-theory

**Walter Murch's "Rule of Six": a good cut should ideally satisfy six criteria in descending weight — Emotion (51%), Story (23%), Rhythm (10%), Eye-trace (7%), 2D plane (5%), 3D space/continuity (4%).** Grade **p** — one practitioner's own heuristic, published in his book *In the Blink of an Eye*, explicitly presented as his personal working ratio, not a study. It is widely taught (StudioBinder, film schools) but the numbers are Murch's opinion, not measured. The load-bearing insight for sequence design: continuity (matching eyelines, matching physical space) is the thing you're *most* allowed to sacrifice, and emotional truth is the thing you're *never* allowed to sacrifice. https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/walter-murch-rule-of-six/

**A scene needs a geography shot — one wide/establishing shot that fixes where things are — so that every subsequent tighter shot can be read without re-orienting the viewer.** Grade **P** — universal practitioner convention, taught identically across every film-grammar source (Columbia's Film Language Glossary, MasterClass, standard continuity-editing textbooks). The 180-degree rule is the specific technical discipline that keeps a geography, once established, legible shot to shot (camera stays on one side of the action axis so left/right relationships don't flip). https://filmglossary.ccnmtl.columbia.edu/term/180-degree-rule/

**The classic master-scene coverage pattern is: one master (wide, full scene, both/all actors) shot first, then closer shots of each subject cut into it in the edit** — this is *the* industry-standard scene-building method, and it is coverage-first (decide-later) by design. Grade **P** — this is simply how most professional sets have worked for decades; it is descriptive, not a claim about what's best. https://www.masterclass.com/articles/understanding-the-180-degree-rule-in-cinematography

**Fincher's stated position is close to the opposite: he wants each shot to justify itself rather than defaulting to a full coverage pass, and treats "shoot it every possible way and decide in post" as something to resist, not a safety net.** Grade **p** *(unverified in sweep — the clearest single-sentence Fincher quote naming this by name did not surface directly in the search results; what's attested is a documented pattern across multiple interviews of Fincher favoring a small number of pre-decided, wide-as-possible setups over exhaustive coverage, plus his famously high take-count on the *chosen* setups rather than many different setups)*. See the Bong Joon-ho/Fincher conversation for the fullest primary account of his method. https://nofilmschool.com/bong-joon-ho-david-fincher

**Shot-size progression across a scene (wide → medium → close as the scene intensifies) is standard grammar for building escalation; editors additionally shorten shot duration to raise intensity and lengthen it for relief.** Grade **P** — near-universal craft consensus, taught as the "V strategy" (wide shots first, progressively closer) in shot-listing guides, and as duration-based pacing in editing guides. Not a single named auteur's claim — this is trade-wide convention. https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/how-does-an-editor-control-the-rhythm-of-a-film/

## 3. What survives our constraints

**Survives fully — this is all selection and sequencing, not production:**
- Deciding shot *size progression* for a scene before generating anything (e.g., "this 6-shot beat goes WS → MS → MS → CU → CU → WS button") is a pure planning decision. Costs nothing, applies to stills, single clips, or archive footage equally.
- The geography shot: generate or source one wide/establishing frame per new location before any close shots of it. On a still-comic this is literally "panel 1 of a new location is wide."
- Reveal timing / suspense-vs-surprise: entirely about *what information is in which panel/shot and in what order* — directly portable to a scroll comic (control what's above vs. below the fold) or a narrated video (control what's said before vs. after a cut).
- The button/punctuation shot: pick or generate one closing image deliberately, rather than letting the sequence just stop.
- Pattern-and-break: repeat a visual rhythm (same shot size, same cut length, same camera behavior) across 3-4 shots on purpose, then break it once. Works identically whether the "shots" are stills under narration or AI clips — it's a structural choice made at storyboard time.
- Kuleshov/montage-collision: juxtaposing two generated images or clips to manufacture a third meaning costs nothing extra — it's just sequencing what you already have.
- Murch's priority order (emotion > story > rhythm > eye-trace > 2D > 3D continuity) is *more* useful to us than to a film crew, because we have no continuity to protect anyway (no actor, no set) — it tells us explicitly not to worry about matching, and to worry about emotional truth of the cut instead.

**Dies or needs heavy translation:**
- The master-scene coverage method (shoot everything wide, then closer, then decide) assumes a shooting day and an editor choosing after the fact. We generate one shot at a time and each one costs a credit — we cannot "cover" a scene and pick later at the same cost a crew can. Fincher's opposite philosophy (decide the shot before you roll) is the one that actually matches our economics: **decide the shot list first, generate to the list, don't generate speculative alternates "just in case."**
- 180-degree-rule mechanics (camera physically staying on one side of an axis between two actors over a shooting day) assume continuous blocking and multiple set-ups of the same live action — we're not tracking a moving camera across takes, we're generating discrete frames. The *outcome* it protects (don't flip left/right relationships between shots of the same space) still matters and must be enforced by hand/prompt-independent visual check, not by an on-set rule.
- Coverage-of-performance criteria in Murch's rule (eye-trace within a take, 3D continuity of an actor's motion across cuts) mostly don't apply — there's no performer to track continuity for.

## 4. Contested

**Coverage vs. no-coverage.** The master-scene method (wide master + closer inserts, decide in the edit) is the industry default and assumes shooting extensively is cheap relative to guessing wrong. Fincher's camp argues the opposite: decide precisely, shoot fewer distinct setups (even if each is repeated many times for performance), because ambiguity in the shot list produces a worse film, not a safer one — per his own account in the Bong Joon-ho conversation, he wants shots as wide/complete as possible rather than being "forced into coverage" that fragments a moment he'd rather hold in one take. Neither side is "right" in the abstract; they're optimizing for different costs (crew time vs. directorial certainty). *(unverified in sweep — Fincher's exact framing beyond this secondary characterization not directly retrieved)*.

## 5. Myths — never cite

- **"Always cut wide-to-close, every scene, every time."** Widely repeated as if it were a rule; it's a default shape, not a law — inversions (close-to-wide, to delay geography and heighten disorientation or reveal) are a standard and deliberate craft choice, not an error.
- **"The Kuleshov effect proves editing overrides everything, including performance."** The popular retelling overstates it into "editing is all that matters" — the actual claim is narrower: adjacency shapes *inferred* emotion/meaning, not that shot order can substitute for a story having content.
- **"More coverage is always safer."** Repeated as received wisdom in film-school shot-listing guides; contradicted directly by Fincher's stated method and by the basic economics of anyone without a crew — more options in the edit room is not automatically a better film, and for us it isn't even affordable.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Master shot | The one wide shot with everyone/everything in frame | A geography anchor every closer shot can borrow from |
| Coverage | Shooting a scene from many angles/sizes to decide later | Flexibility in the edit — at the cost of deciding nothing up front |
| Shot list (as argument) | A pre-decided, justified sequence of shots, each with a reason | Forces you to know *why* a shot exists before you spend a credit on it |
| 180-degree rule / axis | Keep the camera on one side of an imaginary line between subjects | Left/right positions don't flip and confuse the viewer between shots |
| Reveal | The shot that releases withheld visual information | Controls when the audience learns something, for maximum effect |
| Suspense vs. surprise | Audience knows something a character doesn't, vs. audience is shocked with them | Suspense sustains tension over time; surprise spends it in one hit |
| Button shot | The closing shot of a scene, chosen to punctuate it | Gives a scene a felt ending instead of just stopping |
| Pattern and break | Repeat a visual rhythm, then deliberately violate it once | The violation reads as significant only because the pattern was real |
| Montage sequence | A short, self-contained run of shots compressing time/change | A designed unit with its own setup→escalation→resolution shape |
| Kuleshov effect | Meaning inferred from adjacency of two shots, not from either alone | Lets you manufacture a third idea just by sequencing, free of cost |
| Intellectual montage (Eisenstein) | Deliberately colliding unrelated images to force an idea | A tool for irony/argument — put two things next to each other on purpose |
| Rule of Six (Murch) | Priority order for judging a cut: emotion > story > rhythm > eye-trace > 2D > 3D | Tells you what to sacrifice first when a cut can't satisfy everything |

## 7. Open questions

- What does a *designed* shot-list method look like specifically for a 5-8 shot BadCode scene where every "shot" is an independently generated still or 8-second clip rather than coverage of one continuous action? This brief gives the principles; a worked BadCode example (take one existing comic scene and re-derive its shot list against this method) would prove or break it.
- Is there empirical (not just practitioner-testimony) research on shot-size progression's effect on perceived tension, or is "wide-to-close = escalation" purely inherited craft convention? Not settled here — everything found was practitioner-consensus grade, not experimental.
- The exact primary Fincher quote on refusing coverage did not surface cleanly in this sweep's budget — worth a direct WebFetch of the Bong Joon-ho/No Film School conversation and/or the Believer Magazine interview in a follow-up pass to upgrade that claim from p/unverified to a solid p.
