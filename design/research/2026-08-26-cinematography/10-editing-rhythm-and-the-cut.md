# 10 - Editing rhythm and the cut

## 1. The short answer

- When judging a cut, ask "does this feel right emotionally" before "is this technically continuous" - continuity is the thing you sacrifice first, not last.
- A hard cut between two unrelated images is never neutral - the viewer's brain automatically invents a relationship between them. Use that on purpose or it uses you.
- Fast cutting doesn't automatically mean "exciting" and slow cutting doesn't automatically mean "boring" - each produces a different *kind* of tension, and unbroken contrast between the two is what makes either land.
- A single still or clip held two seconds too long dies; held exactly long enough it becomes the moment people remember. There is no fixed "right" length - it's set by how much information/emotion is in the frame, not a stopwatch rule.
- Cutting on movement (a hand starting to move, a head starting to turn) hides the cut and carries energy through it - the eye tracks motion, not frame boundaries.
- Two shots placed side by side make an argument even if neither one does alone - juxtaposition is editorial opinion, not neutral sequencing. This is the whole toolkit of satire and political documentary.
- The average length of a cut has been getting shorter across a century of commercial film - real, measured, data-backed - but shorter is a trend, not a law; some of the most gripping modern work runs long, patient takes.
- Sound arriving before or lingering after the picture cut (a J-cut / L-cut) smooths a transition and makes two shots feel like one continuous thought even when the images are unrelated.
- There's no consensus on how to read Adam Curtis's editing - practitioners praise the same juxtaposition technique documentary theorists call intellectually dishonest. Know both readings before you use the technique for argument.
- The famous "Kuleshov effect" as usually described (a single unchanging shot of a face read as three different emotions depending on what it's cut against) was never rigorously demonstrated in a controlled, replicated form - it is a teaching legend built on a thin original claim.

## 2. The findings

**Walter Murch's Rule of Six is an explicit, weighted, ordered priority list, not a vibes-based "trust your gut."** Grade: **p** (single practitioner's stated framework, though hugely influential and widely taught). In *In the Blink of an Eye*, Murch names six criteria a cut should satisfy, in this order, with percentage weights he assigns to each: **Emotion (51%)**, **Story (23%)**, **Rhythm (10%)**, **Eye-trace (7%)**, **Two-dimensional plane of screen / composition (5%)**, **Three-dimensional space of action / continuity (4%)**. His rule for when they conflict (they usually do, in real footage): let go from the bottom of the list upward - sacrifice 3D continuity first, then screen composition, then eye-trace, and so on, protecting Emotion at nearly any cost. Murch is an Oscar-winning editor (*Apocalypse Now*, *The English Patient*, *The Godfather Part II*) and this is his own retrospective codification of practice, not a study of other editors. Widely misquoted as an unordered checklist of six equal "rules" - it is explicitly a ranked, weighted list where the bottom two items are frequently and deliberately broken. Source: StudioBinder's summary of the book (https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/walter-murch-rule-of-six/) — content-mill-adjacent but the percentages match how the book is quoted elsewhere; treat the numbers as Murch's own and the packaging as secondary.

**The Kuleshov effect, as commonly told, rests on a demonstration that was never run under controlled conditions and has resisted faithful replication.** Grade: **myth in its popular form / A (mixed) for the narrow lab finding that does replicate.** The legend: Lev Kuleshov allegedly intercut one unchanging shot of actor Ivan Mosjoukine with a bowl of soup, a dead child, and a woman on a divan, and audiences praised his "acting" as hunger, grief, and desire respectively depending only on what came before. No footage of this exact experiment survives, there is no original written report with data, and the story reaches us through students' secondhand accounts decades later. Prince & Hensley (1992) tried to run a controlled version and found viewers did **not** reliably attribute emotion to the neutral face based on the paired shot - they could not validate the effect as originally claimed. Literary critic Norman Holland is cited as saying the experiment has "passed into the mythology of film." Later, better-controlled work (Ildirar & Ewing 2018; and 2020s fMRI/behavioral studies using real film footage) does find a real, measurable effect - but a narrower, weaker one: viewers with prior film-literacy do construct inferred links between neutral shots and adjacent context shots, first-time/naive viewers largely do not, and the effect is closer to "context biases interpretation of ambiguous imagery" than "an actor's face reads three different emotions from nothing." Sources: Prince & Hensley replication discussion (https://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/24986/), PLOS One reexamination with fMRI (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0308295).

**Average shot length (ASL) in commercial film has measurably and steadily fallen over a century, and this is real quantitative film-history data, not folklore.** Grade: **A** (large-sample statistical analysis, cross-validated across two independent datasets). Film scholar Barry Salt pioneered treating mean shot length as a measurable style variable starting in his 1974 *Film Quarterly* paper "Statistical Style Analysis of Motion Pictures," and built a database now holding roughly 10,000 measured films, hosted on Cinemetrics (https://cinemetrics.uchicago.edu). Cornell psychologist James Cutting used Salt's data plus his own coding of 150 Hollywood films (1935-2005) to show ASL has dropped from roughly 12 seconds per shot around 1930 to roughly 2.5 seconds in recent commercial film, and linked shot-length patterns to "attentional synchrony" - the claim that editors are increasingly using cut rate itself to steer and hold audience attention, not just to tell story beats. Sources: Cutting's Cornell research page (http://cutting.psych.cornell.edu/curresearch.htm), Salt via Cinemetrics (https://cinemetrics.uchicago.edu/articles). Caveat *(unverified in sweep)*: a 2023 Humanities Bulletin piece specifically argues ASL-as-a-single-number risks an "ecological fallacy" when used to characterize a whole film or era - worth flagging that the trend is real but summarizing any one film by its ASL alone oversimplifies.

**Eisenstein's montage and Hollywood continuity editing are opposed theories of what a cut is for, not two techniques on a spectrum.** Grade: **A (mixed)** — this is settled film-theory history, though "theory" not empirical science. Continuity editing (the dominant Hollywood system) is built to make the cut *invisible*: match cuts, the 180-degree rule, eyeline match, shot/reverse-shot all exist so the audience never notices editing happened and experiences one continuous world. Soviet montage theory, developed by Eisenstein (and Kuleshov, Pudovkin, Vertov), does the opposite on purpose: it wants the cut *noticed*, because meaning is produced by the collision of two shots against each other - Eisenstein called this "intellectual montage," where two unrelated images juxtaposed generate a third idea that exists in neither shot alone (a technique borrowed explicitly from dialectics). Continuity editing subordinates the cut to the story; montage theory treats the cut itself as the argument. Source: Wikipedia's Soviet montage theory entry (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_montage_theory), corroborated by film-theory course summaries (Fiveable).

**Fast cutting does not simply "increase" tension and slow cutting does not simply "decrease" it - each produces a different kind of tension, and the honest claim is that pace choice is genre- and intent-dependent, backed mainly by practitioner consensus rather than controlled study.** Grade: **P** (practitioner consensus, no controlled experimental literature found in this sweep) *(unverified in sweep beyond trade/craft sources)*. Fast, short cuts read as urgency, chaos, physical danger - "the viewer has little time to process," which itself is the point. Slower cutting and held/long takes are the standard tool for **psychological** dread and suspense - the classic argument (attributed across horror/thriller editing craft writing) is that suspense requires the audience to sit in uncertainty, which a fast cut relieves too quickly by resolving the shot. The strongest version of this claim in the trade literature isn't "slow beats fast" or vice versa, it's that **contrast** is what creates the felt effect - a calm, held passage makes the fast cut that follows land harder, and vice versa. No named academic study was found asserting fast cutting *reduces* tension as a general law; treat this as strong craft consensus, not measured science.

**J-cuts and L-cuts (sound leading or lagging the picture cut) are a named, standard technique for making a hard image cut feel continuous.** Grade: **P** (universal practitioner/craft terminology, mechanically well-defined, not really "contested"). An L-cut: the outgoing shot's audio continues to play over the incoming shot's picture. A J-cut: the incoming shot's audio starts before its picture arrives, leading the cut. Both are named for the shape they make on an edit timeline. Function: they decouple the visual cut from the audio cut so the ear and eye change scenes at different moments, which reads to the brain as continuity rather than a jump, even across a hard visual discontinuity. Sources: Wikipedia J-cut (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_cut), Wikipedia L-cut (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L_cut), corroborated across every craft source found (StudioBinder, Adobe, PremiumBeat).

**Adam Curtis's juxtaposition-as-argument method sits in a real critical lineage but is explicitly contested as a form of "lying" by editing.** Grade: **p** (named critical claim, single-source framing found in this sweep) *(unverified in sweep — could not reach a named academic's full argument directly, only a secondary summary)*. Curtis uses montage, archival collage, and juxtaposition of unrelated footage against his own narration to build an argument without ever filming an interview or a scene himself - critics place this in "an asynchronous, discontinuous and elliptical tradition" running from 1920s Soviet montage through Chris Marker's essay films to Curtis. The critique found in this sweep: because montage/juxtaposition works by exploiting the same mechanism as the (contested) Kuleshov effect - the viewer unconsciously invents a connective link between two shots the editor placed together - "the very act of editing these shots together becomes a form of lying," i.e., the persuasive power comes from an inference the audience supplies, not a fact the footage states. Curtis's own stated position, per the summary found, is that he disavows grand theoretical aims and describes himself as "fundamentally an emotional journalist." Source: secondary summary citing this critique (https://www.thedriftmag.com/all-roll-is-b-roll/) - the underlying named critic was not independently confirmed in this sweep.

## 3. What survives our constraints

We have no crew, no coverage, no reshoots - a shot is a still or an 8-second clip, judged and kept or discarded. Almost everything in section 2 is about editing, not shooting, so it survives *unusually well* for a cinematography brief:

- **Murch's Rule of Six survives almost entirely.** It is a framework for judging a cut you already have, which is exactly our situation. Emotion-first, story-second, continuity-last is *more* true for us, not less: we have no coverage to fall back on, so when a still doesn't match its neighbor in eyeline, scale, or 3D space, that's fine to break - Murch explicitly says break it first. Only the "eye-trace" and "recreating reality" criteria assume you shot multiple angles of the same action, which we mostly don't have; treat those as advisory when they apply, ignorable when they don't.
- **Juxtaposition-as-argument (Eisenstein / Curtis) is close to our best tool.** It requires no continuity, no coverage, no matched action - just two images and a hard cut between them, which is precisely what a stills-and-narration documentary is built from. This is the technique most worth deliberately studying and using, and section 4's critique (that it works by exploiting inferred meaning, i.e. can mislead) is a real editorial responsibility to hold given our narrator's stated authority.
- **Pace/ASL as a variable survives completely** - cut rate is entirely in our hands in the edit, regardless of how the source frames were made. The Cutting/Salt data is a genuine reference point for what "fast" and "slow" mean in commercial film today (roughly 2.5s average now vs 12s in 1930), useful as a calibration, not a target.
- **J-cuts/L-cuts survive completely and may matter more for us than for a conventional film** - since our images are often static, sound leading or trailing the visual cut is one of the few tools we have to make a hard image change feel like one continuous thought rather than a slideshow. Worth using deliberately on every narrated cut, not just dialogue scenes.
- **Cutting on action mostly does not survive** in its literal form (it requires two takes/angles of the same movement to cut between mid-motion) but the *underlying principle* - hide a cut inside implied or suggested motion rather than on a static frame - can be approximated by matching the compositional energy or implied motion direction between a still and the clip that follows it.
- **Held shots / letting a moment breathe survives directly** - a still held under narration *is* the held shot, and the craft question ("how long is too long") is exactly the same judgment call an editor makes on a long take, just without the option of cutting away to coverage if it fails. This raises the stakes on getting hold-time right per shot rather than on a formula.
- **The match cut / graphic match survives well for image-to-image transitions** (shape, color, or composition rhyme between the outgoing still and the incoming still/clip) but the classic match-cut examples (2001's bone-to-satellite, action carried across a real camera move) assume shot-specific staging we can't commission - we can only select or generate frames that happen to rhyme, we can't stage a match on demand the way a crew can.
- **The Kuleshov-effect caution applies directly to us and is worth internalizing rather than discarding as "just a myth":** even though the classic demonstration doesn't replicate cleanly, the weaker, real finding (context biases how an ambiguous image is read, more for film-literate viewers) is exactly the mechanism our narration + still combinations rely on. Don't claim the strong myth version of the effect as a design principle ("any face reads any emotion if I cut it right") - the honest, survivable version is "an ambiguous or neutral image is genuinely more manipulable by context than a clear one, and audience media-literacy affects how much."

## 4. Contested

- **Fast cutting vs. slow cutting for tension.** No two named theorists were found making opposite absolute claims in this sweep (this is craft consensus more than a documented feud) - but the craft literature itself holds two positions in tension: one camp (action/thriller editing convention) treats rapid cutting as the default tension tool; the other (horror/psychological-suspense editing convention, and the "Kuleshov effect" tradition broadly) treats the *withholding* of a cut - the long take, the refusal to look away - as the stronger tension tool precisely because it denies the audience the relief a cut provides. Both are real, working traditions in commercial film; which one is right depends entirely on whether the tension you want is kinetic/physical or psychological/dreadful.
- **Continuity editing vs. montage theory, as competing claims about where meaning lives.** Classical Hollywood continuity holds that editing should be invisible and meaning lives in the story world; Eisenstein and Soviet montage theory hold that editing is never invisible and meaning is *produced* by the collision itself, so hiding the cut is actively wasting the medium's most powerful tool. These are not reconcilable positions on what a cut is for - they are foundational, competing film theories, and most working editors today use continuity by default and reach for montage-style juxtaposition only when they specifically want the audience to notice the argument being made (documentary, satire, political film - exactly our register).
- **Adam Curtis's method: craft achievement or intellectual sleight of hand.** Critics in his own lineage (Marker, Soviet montage) treat the associative-collage form as a legitimate, serious essay-film tradition. Other critics found in this sweep argue the same technique is structurally a form of "lying," because its persuasive force comes from an inference the viewer supplies (unconsciously linking two unrelated shots) rather than a claim the footage actually supports - i.e., the technique's power and its dishonesty risk are the *same mechanism*, not separable qualities. This is the single most load-bearing disagreement for BadCode specifically, since our narrator's whole voice is built on authoritative juxtaposition.
- **Does the Kuleshov effect exist at all.** Prince & Hensley (1992) found no support for the classic strong claim. Ildirar & Ewing (2018) and more recent fMRI work found a real but narrower, weaker, viewer-literacy-dependent effect. Both are legitimate published findings; they disagree because they are testing different-strength versions of the claim (the film-school legend vs. a bounded psychological effect), not because one study is simply wrong.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"Kuleshov proved that the same shot of a face reads as three different emotions depending on context"** - as a controlled, data-backed demonstration, this never happened; no footage or written data from the original claimed experiment survives, and the one serious attempt to replicate it (Prince & Hensley, 1992) failed to validate it. Cite the narrower, real, later finding instead if you need this idea at all.
- **"Faster cutting is always more exciting / more modern / objectively better filmmaking."** ASL has fallen across film history (real data), but that is a trend in commercial convention, not a quality law - slow, held cutting remains a first-rate tool for dread, grief, and awe, all of which are core BadCode registers.
- **"Continuity editing is the 'correct' way to cut and montage/juxtaposition is a stylistic exception."** These are two different, equally legitimate theories of editing with different goals, not a norm-plus-deviation. Political/documentary/satirical work (our lane) has at least as much claim to montage as the default as commercial narrative film has to continuity.
- **Treating Murch's Rule of Six as six equally weighted, unordered "things to check."** It is explicitly hierarchical (51/23/10/7/5/4) with an explicit sacrifice order. Quoting it as a flat checklist misstates Murch's own point, which is that you almost never get all six and the ranking tells you what to give up.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| term | plain-English gloss | what it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Rule of Six | Murch's ranked list: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, 2D plane, 3D space | Tells you what to protect and what to sacrifice when a cut can't satisfy everything |
| Kuleshov effect | context changes how an ambiguous image reads | Names the mechanism juxtaposition exploits - and its real limits |
| montage (Soviet sense) | meaning made by colliding two unrelated shots | The core technique of political/satirical editing, not just "editing style" |
| continuity editing | cuts built to be invisible, story flows as one world | The opposite instinct to montage - useful to name so you can knowingly break it |
| cutting on action | hiding a cut inside a movement already in progress | The classic way to make a cut disappear (needs matched footage we mostly lack) |
| match cut / graphic match | two shots linked by shared shape, motion, or composition | A transition that reads as a connection, not a jump - buildable from stills |
| average shot length (ASL) | mean seconds-per-shot across a film | A real, measurable pace variable, useful for calibrating "how fast is this cut" |
| L-cut | outgoing audio continues over the new picture | Smooths a hard visual cut into one continuous thought |
| J-cut | incoming audio starts before its picture arrives | Leads the audience into a cut before they see it, softening the jump |
| the held shot | deliberately not cutting, letting an image sit | Lets weight and unease build - the sibling tool to fast cutting, not its opposite-in-quality |

## 7. Open questions

- Is there any controlled experimental (not just trade-craft) literature directly testing "does cut rate change measured physiological tension/arousal"? This sweep found strong craft consensus but no named lab study - worth a dedicated search if this claim needs an A-grade source later.
- Who is the specific critic/theorist behind the "montage as a form of lying" claim about Adam Curtis (the source found was a secondary magazine summary, not a traceable named academic argument) - worth pinning down the primary essay if this critique is going to be cited by name.
- Does the ASL-decline trend (Cutting/Salt) hold specifically for documentary and narrated/political film, or is it measured mostly from narrative commercial film? Our form is closer to essay-film/documentary than to studio narrative, and that distinction wasn't separated out in the data found.
- The 2023 "ecological fallacy" critique of ASL as a whole-film summary statistic was only found as a title/abstract-level reference *(unverified in sweep)* - worth reading in full before leaning on ASL comparisons across films or eras.
