# 17 - Monumentalism - when the grammar of power criticises it and when it glorifies it

## 1. The short answer

- **The grammar is genuinely neutral; the reading is built by what surrounds it.** Soviet monumentalism, Nazi rally architecture, and Deakins' *Blade Runner 2049* interiors are, per Brief 13, "functionally identical" as grammar. Nothing in scale, symmetry or a single hard light source alone tells the eye "this is good" or "this is bad" — that verdict is added by a small set of separable choices below. Treat "monumental = fascist" and "monumental = safe because it's ironic" as equally false.
- **Sontag's checklist is a usable diagnostic, not just theory.** *Fascinating Fascism* (1975) names the specific formal markers of fascist aesthetics: massing people into ornamental patterns, turning people into things, grouping them around an all-powerful leader-figure, alternating "ceaseless motion" with "congealed, static, virile posing," and — the line that matters most for a checklist — glorifying **surrender**, exalting **mindlessness**, glamorising **death**. Grade **p** (single named critic; see §2 for why this doesn't get P despite being the canonical text in the field).
- **Benjamin's frame is about *function*, not appearance: fascism aestheticises politics so the public can feel agency (self-expression, spectacle) while property relations stay untouched.** His prescribed opposite isn't "make it look different" — it's "politicise art," i.e. make the image argue for a change in who owns what. *(Benjamin's own text, paraphrase-verified via secondary sourcing this sweep — see §7.)*
- **The single most reliable formal marker across every fascist-propaganda case researched is the absence of a visible cost.** Cathedral-of-light Nuremberg, Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will*, Stalinist monumental photography — none of them show exhaustion, wreckage, a body that failed, or anyone the system is failing. The frame is clean because showing a cost would break the spell.
- **Satire that borrows power's own grammar to indict it reliably gets read as celebration by part of any large audience — this is not a hypothetical risk, it is a documented, repeated event.** *Starship Troopers* is the canonical case: Verhoeven built the propaganda-reel aesthetic on purpose, quoting *Triumph of the Will* directly, and was still accused of being neo-fascist by mainstream critics on release. *American Psycho*'s director has said on the record she is "mystified" that Wall Street traders adopted Patrick Bateman as an aspirational icon. This pattern (dubbed "aspirational reading" in film criticism) recurs across *Fight Club* and *The Wolf of Wall Street* too.
- **The counter-tradition (films that use monumental scale to indict power) does it by adding cost, agency-asymmetry, and a visible outside — never by softening the scale.** *Metropolis* keeps the machine city terrifying while showing the workers who feed it; the dystopia films researched (*Brazil*, and the architecture literature more broadly) borrow fascist-era iconography specifically so the borrowing itself reads as an accusation, once paired with a protagonist who is crushed rather than exalted by it.
- **Camera behaviour is a legible tell, independently of what's in frame:** a camera that glides up a structure in slow, worshipful reveal reads differently from one that holds static and lets a small human cross the frame at the bottom. Sontag's "ceaseless motion vs. congealed virile posing" pairing is itself evidence that fascist cinematography has a *recognisable rhythm*, not just a recognisable geometry — grade **p**, inference from her text, not independently tested.
- **This is a live, unresolved risk for BadCode specifically, not settled house doctrine** — the checklist in §1/§3 is a working tool to test frames against, not proof that following it guarantees safety; audience misreading happens even to directors who did everything the checklist recommends (Verhoeven).

## 2. The findings

**Sontag's *Fascinating Fascism* names specific, checkable formal features of fascist aesthetics.** — Grade **p** (single named critic; the essay is the most-cited text in the field but this sweep did not find a second, independent named critic proposing the same taxonomy from scratch — treat as canonical-but-uncorroborated). Direct formulation from the essay: fascist art is built on "the massing of groups of people; the turning of people into things; the multiplication of things and grouping of people/things around an all-powerful, hypnotic leader figure or force," staged through choreography that alternates "between ceaseless motion and a congealed, static, 'virile' posing." Her sharpest line for a checklist: "Fascist art glorifies surrender; it exalts mindlessness; it glamorizes death." — Susan Sontag, "Fascinating Fascism," *New York Review of Books*, 1975 — [nybooks.com/articles/1975/02/06/fascinating-fascism](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1975/02/06/fascinating-fascism/)

**Benjamin's argument is that fascism's aestheticisation of politics is a *substitution*: spectacle standing in for a change in property relations.** — Grade **A (mixed)** — this is a primary theoretical text, not an empirical claim, so "grade" here means confidence in what the text actually argues, not whether the argument is true. Paraphrase-verified this sweep, not independently confirmed against the full primary German or a scholarly translation: "Fascism attempts to organize the newly proletarianized masses without affecting the property structure... The logical result of Fascism is the introduction of aesthetics into political life," to which Benjamin opposes "Communism responds by politicizing art." *(unverified in sweep — full primary essay text not directly re-read; sourced via Wikipedia's "Aestheticization of politics" summary page, itself citing the essay's epilogue.)* — Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935), epilogue — [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aestheticization_of_politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aestheticization_of_politics)

**Speer's "Cathedral of Light" at the Nuremberg rallies was a specific, documented engineering solution to a specific propaganda problem: the stone grandstand wasn't finished in time, so 130-152 anti-aircraft searchlights simulated a colonnade of light 20-40m into the sky to fake architectural permanence.** — Grade **A (mixed)** on the historical facts (well-documented, multiple tertiary/secondary sources converge, no primary Speer memoir text re-verified this sweep), described by at least one historian as "the single most dramatic moment of the Nazi Party rallies... not a military parade or a political speech but the Lichtdom." This is the concrete case for how monumental *absence-of-visible-structure* (light standing in for mass) can manufacture a feeling of permanence and scale with zero real architecture — the same trick a generated frame does by definition. — Wikipedia "Cathedral of Light," Nuremberg museum audioguide — [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Light), [museen.nuernberg.de/audioguide/interim/en/3_4.html](https://museen.nuernberg.de/audioguide/interim/en/3_4.html)

**Verhoeven's *Starship Troopers* deliberately reconstructed Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* propaganda-reel grammar to satirise fascism, and a documented portion of the contemporary critical audience read it as sincere fascist celebration instead.** — Grade **A (mixed)** as a reception-history case: Verhoeven's intent is corroborated by his own on-record statements (he called the result "ironically... a fascist utopia" and was "tremendously disappointed" to be called neo-Nazi by the *Washington Post*); the misreading is corroborated by named contemporaneous critical reaction (the *Washington Post* accusation, a *New York Times* review dismissing it as "raunchiness tailor-made for teen-age boys" rather than engaging the satire) — two independent named outlets plus the director's own testimony is real corroboration, not SEO-echo. — reported via SlashFilm, Far Out Magazine, summarising Verhoeven's own interviews and contemporaneous reviews — [slashfilm.com](https://www.slashfilm.com/1278671/vicious-satire-starship-troopers-too-smart-its-own-good/), [faroutmagazine.co.uk](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/anti-fascist-leanings-paul-verhoeven-starship-troopers/) *(secondary aggregation; primary interview transcripts not independently re-verified this sweep)*

**American Psycho's director Mary Harron has stated on the record that she is "mystified" that Wall Street traders adopted Patrick Bateman as an aspirational figure, despite the film being built, in her words, as "a gay man's satire on masculinity."** — Grade **p** (single named practitioner, direct on-record quote, not cross-corroborated by a second filmmaker this sweep, but it is her own primary testimony rather than a third party's interpretation — stronger than a typical p). — Reported via Deadline, Fortune, Variety Australia, AOL Finance, all covering the same 2025 press cycle around Harron's remarks — [deadline.com/2025/04/american-psycho-mary-harron-trump-film-legacy](https://deadline.com/2025/04/american-psycho-mary-harron-trump-film-legacy-1236368612/), [fortune.com/2025/04/16/american-psycho-director-wall-street-bros](https://fortune.com/2025/04/16/american-psycho-director-wall-street-bros)

**"Aspirational reading" — audiences admiring and emulating exactly what a film intends to critique — recurs across *The Wolf of Wall Street* and *Fight Club*, not just *American Psycho* and *Starship Troopers*.** — Grade **p**, and a weaker p than the two cases above: sourced to general film-criticism roundups (ScreenRant, Collider) rather than a named director's own testimony for these two titles specifically, so treat as suggestive pattern-recognition, not a proven case, until a primary Scorsese or Fincher statement is found. *(unverified in sweep)* — [screenrant.com/wolf-wall-street-movies-glorify-satirize-bad-behavior](https://screenrant.com/wolf-wall-street-movies-glorify-satirize-bad-behavior/), [collider.com/fight-club-and-other-movies-that-were-misinterpreted-by-fans](https://collider.com/fight-club-and-other-movies-that-were-misinterpreted-by-fans/)

**Dystopian-film architecture (*Metropolis*, and by extension the *Brazil* / totalitarian-retro-futurist lineage) is academically documented as deliberately quoting fascist-era monumental iconography specifically so the quotation itself functions as an indictment.** — Grade **A (mixed)** — this is a peer-reviewed/academic source (ResearchGate paper, not independently re-read in full this sweep, summary only) rather than a content-mill claim, but it's a single paper, not a cross-corroborated field consensus. The paper's framing: dystopian-film production design (citing *Brazil*'s Ministry of Information specifically) uses "Art Deco elements consistent with the overall retro atmosphere," explicitly referencing "the iconography and architecture of fascism in Europe, in the footsteps of the pioneering *Metropolis*." — "Architectural Dystopian Projections in the films Metropolis, Brazil, and The Island," ResearchGate — [researchgate.net/publication/258098204](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258098204_Architectural_Dystopian_Projections_in_the_films_Metropolis_Brazil_and_The_Island) *(summary only, full paper not re-read this sweep — unverified in sweep)*

## 3. What survives our constraints

**Fully survives (still image or single static/simple-move 8-second clip, no crew):**
- Every item on the formal-tests checklist in §1/§6 is a framing, staging, or presence/absence decision — none requires blocking across a shooting day or continuity between takes. "Is there a visible cost in this frame" is answerable by what you choose to put (or not put) in one still.
- Speer's light-as-fake-structure trick transfers directly — it is *already* the technique of implying architecture without building it, which is exactly what a generated frame does by necessity, not by cheat.
- Sontag's "clean frame, no cost" marker and its inverse (a visible cost: wreckage, exhaustion, a body that failed) are pure composition/prop decisions.
- Camera worship-vs-observation (slow glorifying reveal vs. static clinical hold) is achievable within an 8-second generated clip's one simple move.

**Survives partially:**
- Sontag's "ceaseless motion vs. congealed posing" choreography of *masses* — we can imply this with one or two figures and their posture, but the actual mass-choreography effect (hundreds of synchronised bodies) that made Riefenstahl's imagery work is a crowd-control/blocking problem no BadCode shot has the budget for. Use it as a posture cue for the figures we do have, not a literal restaging.
- The Verhoeven test (satire reads as celebration to part of any audience regardless of authorial intent) is a fact about *reception*, not about a single frame — no formal choice fully immunises a shot against misreading. What survives is discipline: pair every monumental frame with an unambiguous cost or victim in the same piece (per `docs/marketing/the-reader.md`'s existing rule to never raise a fear without naming a beneficiary — this is the same rule, restated for imagery).

**Does not survive at all:**
- Genuine mass-choreography (Riefenstahl's actual thousands of coordinated bodies, or Speer's 130+ physical searchlights) — these are real-world logistics no still or 8-second AI clip can replicate at true scale; anything we do is a suggestion of mass, not mass itself, and should be treated and captioned as such internally.
- Testing our own reception the way Verhoeven's reception was tested — we have no preview audience, no critics' screening, no way to pilot a frame against a real crowd before it ships. The checklist in §6 is the substitute for that test, not a guarantee equivalent to it.

## 4. Contested

- **Does satirical intent surviving in the text matter if a meaningful fraction of the audience reads it straight?** Verhoeven himself treats the misreading as a failure worth being "tremendously disappointed" about, implying intent alone isn't sufficient defence. Harron's "mystified" response to Wall Street's embrace of Bateman reads the same way — both directors built defensible, arguably airtight critiques on the page/shot list and still lost part of the audience. Nobody in the sources found this sweep argues the opposite position (that misreading is the audience's fault and no filmmaking discipline is owed) — this is worth flagging as a one-sided finding, not a real two-camp debate, and treated as a warning rather than a "contested" item with genuine opposing named voices.
- **Whether borrowing propaganda's own grammar is ever the *right* choice versus a needlessly dangerous one** is not resolved by anything found this sweep — no named critic argues "never quote fascist aesthetics, even to indict them," but the volume of misreadings (Starship Troopers, American Psycho, Wolf of Wall Street, Fight Club) is real evidence for caution that this brief cannot resolve with a citation, only flag as an open editorial risk for Kai/Jack.

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"If the film's intent is satirical, the imagery is safe."** Directly contradicted by Verhoeven's and Harron's own accounts of their films' reception — intent does not travel with the image.
- **"Monumental scale + symmetry + a dwarfed human is inherently fascist grammar."** Contradicted by Brief 13's own finding (same grammar, opposite politics in Soviet propaganda vs. Deakins' *Blade Runner 2049*) and by the counter-tradition (Metropolis, Brazil) using the identical vocabulary to indict, not celebrate.
- **"Beauty and menace are opposites, so a beautiful monumental frame can't be a warning."** Sontag's entire thesis is the reverse — fascist aesthetics *is* the equation of beauty with power; a beautiful frame with no visible cost is exactly the failure mode to guard against, not a safe zone.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Aestheticisation of politics (Benjamin) | Substituting spectacle/beauty for an actual change in who holds power or property | Names the trap precisely: a gorgeous frame that changes nothing is doing fascism's job, not ours |
| Massing / ornamental pattern (Sontag) | Turning many people into one visual pattern rather than showing them as individuals | A test: if the humans in frame read as decoration, the shot is drifting toward celebration |
| Congealed vs. ceaseless motion (Sontag) | Fascist choreography alternates frozen "heroic" poses with synchronised mass movement | A posture/motion cue for how figures should (and shouldn't) hold themselves in our frames |
| Visible cost | Something on screen paying the price of the power being depicted — wreckage, exhaustion, a named victim | The single fastest formal fix if a monumental frame is reading as too admiring |
| Aspirational reading | An audience adopting a satirised figure/system as a role model instead of a warning | The name for the exact risk this whole brief exists to manage |
| Cathedral-of-light trick | Implying massive permanent structure using only light/absence, no real material mass | Licenses the thing we already have to do (fake the scale) as a real, named technique, not a shortcut |

## 7. Open questions

- No second named critic independently proposing Sontag's taxonomy was found this sweep — is there one (an art historian or film theorist working separately from her, e.g. on Soviet or Italian Futurist propaganda specifically) who would upgrade §2's headline claim from p to a genuine P?
- Benjamin's epilogue was read only via a Wikipedia summary this sweep, not the primary translated text (Zohn or Underwood translations) — worth a direct read before this checklist is treated as settled, since the "politicising art" prescription is doing real work in §1 and deserves primary verification.
- Whether Verhoeven or Harron have said anything prescriptive — not just diagnostic — about what a satirist *should* have done differently, which would turn §4's one-sided finding into an actual contested debate with a second position.
- The counter-tradition claim (Metropolis/Brazil) rests on one ResearchGate paper's summary, not a full read — worth pulling the full PDF and checking whether it names specific devices (camera position, character blocking) rather than just production-design references, which would sharpen §6's checklist considerably.
- Nothing in this sweep tested the checklist in §1 against an actual BadCode frame (e.g. a Gazette-column or server-hall shot already shipped) — the natural next step is applying it to 3-4 existing panels as a worked example before it's trusted as house doctrine.
