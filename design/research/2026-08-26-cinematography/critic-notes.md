# Critic notes — cinematography briefs 01–14

## 1. Gaps — what a fifteenth brief would have to cover

1. **British social realism is entirely absent.** All 14 briefs draw their named-practitioner
   evidence from an arthouse/Hollywood-DP canon — Kubrick, Anderson, Deakins, Tarkovsky, Ozu,
   Fincher, Hitchcock, Iñárritu. `docs/marketing/the-reader.md` names the target as a
   working-class UK reader drifting right. Not one brief cites Ken Loach, Alan Clarke, Andrea
   Arnold, Shane Meadows, or the British social-realist visual grammar (handheld intimacy,
   council-estate framing, unglamorous naturalism) that is the closest existing register to
   that reader's own visual vocabulary. This is the single biggest miss in the corpus relative
   to who BadCode is actually for.
2. **Awe as a distinct emotional target has no research base.** The house register (monumental
   machine architecture, human-scale contrast, near-black vastness) is explicitly an awe
   register, but no brief cites the actual awe literature (Keltner & Haidt's
   vastness-plus-need-for-accommodation model is the standard citation). Brief 13 gets close
   via Soviet monumentalism and brutalism but frames it entirely as "oppression," never as
   "awe" — a related but distinct emotional target BadCode also wants (the narrator is in awe
   of its own mistake, not just condemning it).
3. **No brief flags the fascist-aesthetic risk.** Brief 13 documents that Soviet monumental
   propaganda and Deakins' *Blade Runner 2049* brutalism use "functionally identical" grammar
   to signal institutional power — but never extends the comparison to Nazi/Speer monumental
   architecture and Riefenstahl's cinematography, which is the more famous and more dangerous
   precedent for exactly the near-black/symmetry/scale grammar BadCode has adopted. A house
   register built on "monumental machine dwarfing a human" needs at least one brief asking
   when that grammar reads as critique of power versus a celebration of it — undiscussed
   anywhere in the corpus.
4. **Sound-image relationship is only touched in passing.** Brief 10 covers J-cuts/L-cuts
   mechanically but nothing addresses silence, drone, score entrances, or the sonic register of
   dread — surprising given BadCode's films are narrated and scored, and sound is one of the few
   levers fully under our control (unlike blocking, coverage, actors).
5. **On-screen text/typography as a visual device is missing.** BadCode uses captions, comic
   speech bubbles, and Gazette-column graphics constantly; no brief covers kinetic type,
   caption placement as composition, or how text competes with/reinforces a frame's focal
   point — a real gap given how much of the actual deliverable is text-over-image.
6. **Machines and architecture as the sole "character" have no dedicated craft base.** Brief 07
   explicitly flags this as reasoned-by-analogy, not sourced ("no architectural-photography or
   industrial-cinematography practitioner" was reached). For a project whose visual subject is
   very often a machine, server hall, or Gazette apparatus rather than a person, this is a real
   hole — industrial/architectural cinematography (how *Metropolis*, *Alien*'s Nostromo, or
   real industrial photography stage scale and menace for non-human subjects) is untouched.
7. **The "digital mud" / generator-specific failure mode was flagged and dropped.** Brief 02's
   own open questions name this directly: "avoiding banding, macroblocking, noise... in a
   *generated* near-black tone... a genuinely new problem... would need to sit in a different
   brief." No fifteenth brief picked it up. This is the single most concrete, most
   BadCode-specific unresolved question in the whole set, and it was correctly identified but
   never answered.
8. **Narrator/VO delivery craft is missing.** Brief 11 covers image/VO *timing* (don't let
   narration describe what's on screen) but nothing covers vocal pacing, pause, or breath —
   craft that exists (radio drama, audiobook narration, documentary VO) and is directly
   relevant to a project built around a single dominant narrator voice.

## 2. Laundering

**The systemic pattern, not just isolated cases:** across nearly every brief, "practitioner
consensus, Grade P" is repeatedly built by citing two or three SEO/content-mill sites
(StudioBinder, PremiumBeat, Filmmakers Academy, Wolfcrow, CineD, NoFilmSchool) that plausibly
copy each other, and calling the resulting agreement "convergence" or "corroboration." These
sites are frequently flagged by the briefs' own source tables as "content-mill — low grade,"
yet the finding built on top of them still gets stamped **P**, the second-highest grade in the
scheme. Convergence between three restatements of the same uncredited claim is not independent
corroboration; it may be a single unverified claim propagating through the SEO ecosystem. This
happens enough times that it deserves naming as a structural problem, not a one-off:

- **01, foreground/midground/background = depth**, Grade P, sourced only to two StudioBinder
  pages. No controlled study, no named DP. The brief admits "trade wisdom, not measured fact"
  in its own §2 caveat, but the headline grade is still P.
- **07, camera-height-as-status ("low angle = powerful")**, Grade P, sourced to a single
  flagged-low-grade StudioBinder page, upgraded to P via the unevidenced assertion "the claim
  itself is standard film-school material so treated as P via corroboration, not taken on
  StudioBinder's authority alone" — no second source is actually named. This is the clearest
  single instance of grade-laundering in the corpus, and it is directly contradicted by real
  empirical data sitting three files away (see §3, Contradictions, item 1).
- **09, the master-scene coverage method**, Grade P, sole citation is MasterClass — a
  content-mill-adjacent editorial product, explicitly flagged as such in the brief's own source
  table, but still used as the entire evidentiary basis for a P-grade claim.
- **12, "depth reads through separated layers, not resolution"** and **"single focal point is
  necessary for a composition to read as 'about' something"**, both Grade P, each built on
  three interchangeable photography-education blogs (StudioBinder/PremiumBeat/Filmmakers
  Academy; Lens Lounge/Photography Life/Photzy) with zero named authorities.
- **13, noir's grammar "descends directly from German Expressionism"**, Grade P, with the brief
  itself admitting "none cited here is a single named landmark academic text — treat as strong
  practitioner/historical consensus rather than a single hard citation." An admission that
  undercuts the grade sits right next to the grade rather than lowering it.
- **06, Ozu's stillness discipline**, headline Grade **P**, resting on two sources one of which
  the brief's own text calls "a curated blog piece, treat framing language as p-grade" — the
  top-line grade doesn't reflect the internal downgrade.

**Numbers with thin provenance repeated as if solid:** Walter Murch's Rule of Six percentages
(Emotion 51% / Story 23% / Rhythm 10% / Eye-trace 7% / 2D 5% / 3D 4%) appear in three separate
briefs (08, 09, 10), each individually marked "unverified in sweep — secondary summary, not the
primary text," sourced every time to the same StudioBinder page, never to Murch's own book. No
single brief overclaims this, but the corpus-level effect is that a single unverified secondary
paraphrase gets repeated with the same precise decimal weights three times, which reads as far
more solid in aggregate than any one instance earns. (See also Redundancy, below.)

**Executive-summary hedging loss:** in most briefs, §1 ("The short answer") states findings
as flat, confident claims, and only §2 carries the A/P/p grading and the caveats. A reader who
only reads §1 — which is the realistic failure mode for anyone using these briefs fast — gets
none of the epistemic hedging. Concrete instance: brief 13's §1 states "Symmetry plus a single,
hard, motivated light source in a mostly-dark frame is the fastest route to 'monumental and
inhuman'" as a flat design rule, with no hint that brief 01's own findings (Kubrick vs.
Anderson) show that identical symmetry can read as safe and whimsical depending on everything
else in the frame. Brief 13's §1 doesn't cite or contradict brief 01 — it simply never carries
the caveat forward, which functions as laundering-by-omission at the corpus level even though
neither individual brief is wrong on its own terms.

## 3. Contradictions

1. **Low angle = power, unqualified, vs. the actual empirical picture on angle and trust/
   credibility.** Briefs 07 and 13 both state "low angle reads as dominant/powerful, high angle
   as weak" as settled, P-grade convention with no scope caveat. Brief 05 — built on real
   controlled studies, not content-mill convergence — shows this is significantly more
   contested: Baranowski & Hecht (2018, A-grade, peer-reviewed) found eye-level produces the
   *highest* trust in direct address, with low- and high-angle reducing it equally; McCain,
   Chilberg & Wakshlag (1977) found the *opposite* direction (higher angle raised competence
   ratings) for a TV speaker. **Brief 05 is far better evidenced** — it names four separate
   studies and explicitly reconciles why they diverge (narrative-potency judgments vs.
   direct-address trust judgments are different psychological constructs). Briefs 07 and 13
   should be read as stating the folk version of a convention brief 05 shows is only reliable
   inside a specific context (dramatic narrative with other characters/story cues present).

2. **The Kuleshov effect: solid vs. mostly myth vs. real-but-narrow.** Brief 09 grades the
   classic Kuleshov account "A (mixed) — genuinely a documented... experiment... replicated
   informally many times since" without mentioning that a named controlled replication attempt
   failed. Brief 10 gives the fuller, more critical picture: Prince & Hensley (1992) tried to
   run a controlled version and found viewers did **not** reliably attribute emotion as
   claimed — the classic story is explicitly graded "myth in its popular form" — while a real
   but much narrower and viewer-literacy-dependent effect survives in modern work. Brief 14
   independently reaches the same corrected picture via a different, stronger primary source
   (Cao et al. 2024, PLoS One, fMRI). **Briefs 10 and 14 are clearly better evidenced than 09**
   on this specific claim; 09 should be corrected to match before any of the three is treated
   as authoritative on its own.

3. **Eye-level: the safe/trustworthy default vs. the signature of a boring shot.** Brief 05
   frames eye-level as the empirically best choice when the goal is credibility ("the
   highest-trust... option in the one controlled study that measured it directly"). Brief 12,
   independently, lists eye-level-by-default as compounding "flatness" when nothing else in the
   frame is doing work, implicitly warning against reaching for it as a safe default. Neither
   brief cross-references the other, and BadCode's narrator explicitly wants both credibility
   *and* visual excitement at once — this is a real, unresolved design tension the corpus
   surfaces but never names as a tension.

4. **Symmetry's meaning: fixed vs. context-dependent.** Brief 01's central, well-evidenced
   point (via the Kubrick/Anderson comparison, its own explicit Myths section) is that symmetry
   has no fixed emotional valence — "any claim that symmetry has one fixed emotional meaning
   should be treated as a myth." Brief 13's short answer nonetheless states "symmetry... in a
   mostly-dark frame is the fastest route to 'monumental and inhuman'" as a working formula
   with no hedge. Not a hard logical contradiction (13's frame does add darkness + hard light,
   which is doing real work) but 13 states a symmetry-driven formula in exactly the register 01
   warns against treating as reliable.

## 4. Banned content

**No violations found.** No brief leaks AI-prompt vocabulary (no "cinematic, 8k, trending on
artstation"-style phrasing), no brief offers prompt-engineering advice, and no brief cites an
AI-image or AI-video blog as a craft authority. Brief 12 cites two arXiv papers on AI-image
uncanniness and deepfake detection, but uses them diagnostically (naming the tells of a bad
generation) rather than generatively (how to prompt one) — this is on the right side of the
line and consistent with the tool-agnostic brief this whole corpus was assigned. This is a
clean pass across all 14 files.

## 5. Redundancy — repeat once in the distillation, not fourteen times

- **Foreground/midground/background depth layering** — near-verbatim in 01, 07, 12, 13.
- **Motivated light** (light traceable to a plausible in-scene source) — 02, 12, 13, and
  implicit in 03's warm/cool-contrast discussion.
- **Camera height as a variable distinct from camera angle/tilt** — 04, 05, 07 each make the
  same distinction-with-no-citation point independently.
- **Murch's Rule of Six**, full percentage breakdown — 08, 09, 10, all from the same secondary
  source, never independently verified against the primary text in any of the three.
- **Kuleshov/montage-as-collision** — 09, 10, 14 all cover essentially the same ground; 10 and
  14 have the better-evidenced version (see Contradictions #2) and should be the versions kept.
- **The 180-degree rule / triangle / axis-of-action as a multi-shot coverage discipline that
  mostly doesn't apply to us** — 07, 08, 09 each independently reach and state the same
  conclusion ("this is a coverage problem, we don't shoot coverage").
- **Ozu** (stillness, tatami eye-line, 360-degree space, ignoring the axis) — appears
  substantively in 05, 06, 07, 08. Worth one consolidated Ozu entry rather than four partial
  ones.
- **Deakins as the default named practitioner** — cited in 8 of 14 briefs (02, 03, 04, 06, 07,
  09, 12, 13), overwhelmingly via his own public forum. This is good sourcing discipline
  (primary, named, on the record) but the corpus leans on one figure so heavily that "what does
  Deakins say" risks functioning as a stand-in for "what is craft consensus" — worth flagging
  in the distillation as a single-source-dependency risk, not just a redundancy.
- **Negative space / off-screen implied space** — 01 and 12 both cover this at length
  (Bazin's centripetal/centrifugal framing in 12 is the stronger, more citable version).

## 6. The constraint test (§3 engagement)

All 14 briefs took §3 seriously — every one produced a genuine survives/partial/dies breakdown
rather than a token paragraph, and several (07, 10, 13) go further and flag real unresolved
tensions (e.g. 13's explicit ethical flag about faking vérité authenticity codes on a true
subject). This is a genuine strength of the batch and should be said plainly rather than
manufacturing a false "which ones failed" story.

That said, three briefs are structurally the least useful *in practice*, not because they
engaged poorly but because their core subject matter is intrinsically about multi-take,
crewed, continuous-shooting-day craft that has a hard floor on how much can survive no matter
how well the brief tries:

- **07 (Blocking and staging)** — a large fraction of its evidence base (the triangle, Deakins'
  rehearsal-discovered blocking, coverage-cutting discipline) is explicitly conceded as "not
  our problem" in its own §3. What's left and genuinely useful (depth staging, proxemics,
  frame-within-a-frame) is real but already substantially covered by 01, 12 and 13 — so its
  net *new* yield is smaller than its length suggests.
- **09 (Shot progression / sequence design)** — same shape: the master-scene coverage
  discussion (its longest single finding) is conceded as inapplicable, and the brief's own open
  questions admit the hardest version of the actually-relevant question — "how does geography
  survive across independently-generated images with no pixel-level consistency" — was
  identified but not resolved.
- **08 (Continuity grammar)** — the strongest empirical material (Magliano & Zacks, Smith &
  Henderson) survives excellently and is genuinely valuable, but roughly half the brief's
  vocabulary (the 180-rule, the 30-degree rule, eyeline match across multiple angles) is
  multi-shot coverage machinery that the brief itself repeatedly says "dies, mostly."

By contrast, **01 (composition), 11 (the static frame), 12 (the boring shot), and 14 (visual
motif)** are the most naturally suited to the format — their source material was already about
single-frame or arranged-sequence craft, not about directing a shoot day — and their §3
sections read as close to 1:1 with §2, i.e. almost nothing needed translating. These four are
the most useful briefs to hand a first-time reader of the corpus.

## 7. The top 20 — ranked, most improves a shot tomorrow first

1. One small, deliberately placed bright detail inside near-blackness reads as depth; uniform
   blackness with nothing lit reads as a technical failure, not mood. — **[02, P]**
2. Motivated light — the eye can trace it to a plausible in-scene source (lamp, window, screen)
   — is what makes a stylized or dark frame still feel physically true. — **[02/12/13, P]**
3. Depth layering (foreground/midground/background) is the single fastest fix for a frame that
   reads flat, regardless of resolution or detail. — **[01/07/12, P]**
4. One human-scale figure, visibly small against architecture, is what turns "big building"
   into "building that could crush you" — size alone doesn't do it. — **[13, p]**
5. A frame-within-a-frame (doorway, window, structure) narrates containment or being-watched
   with zero dialogue. — **[01/07, P]**
6. Never treat symmetry, or any single geometric device, as having one fixed emotional
   meaning — Kubrick's dread and Anderson's whimsy use the identical device. — **[01, P/A]**
7. Complementary colour contrast is real optical fact — save it for one deliberate "pop"; it
   reads as an effect the moment it's noticed twice. — **[03, A]**
8. A restricted, deliberately limited palette is a net advantage for us specifically: fewer
   places for a generative model to drift off-brief between separately-made frames. — **[03, P
   + our translation]**
9. Eye-level shots measured the *highest* trust of any camera angle in the one controlled
   direct-address study — the default for any beat where the point is "believe this." — **[05,
   A]**
10. The overhead/God's-eye framing reads as omniscience, irony or dread depending on context —
    cheap to generate and structurally suited to a narrator who already knows how it ends.
    — **[05, P]**
11. A hard cut between two unrelated images is never neutral — the brain infers a relationship
    on its own; the real (narrower, literacy-dependent) Kuleshov effect. — **[10/14, A]**
12. Deep, converging perspective (corridor, colonnade, row of columns receding into shadow) is
    the fastest single-frame signal of institutional power. — **[13, P]**
13. Plan the colour/visual through-line for a whole piece *before* generating a single frame —
    we can't fix drift in a shoot the way a DP can fix it in coverage. — **[03/14, P]**
14. The bookend (first and last image echoing or inverting each other) is the highest-value,
    lowest-cost structural device available to our format — no motion, no crew required.
    — **[14, p/P]**
15. An objective correlative (one concrete object standing for the abstract idea) fails the
    instant a narrator explains what it represents — directly matches the house rule "the moral
    is never stated." — **[14, p]**
16. Camera angle's effect on power is scene/context-dependent, not universal — low angle can
    raise perceived dominance in a story context while simultaneously *lowering* measured trust
    in direct address; know which one you're optimizing for before you pick. — **[05, A]**
17. In a body of work built mostly from static shots, a single moving shot lands with outsized
    force — the economy only works if the surrounding stillness is real, not cheap. — **[06,
    our synthesis from P-grade evidence]**
18. A push-in is the one camera move an 8-second AI clip executes cleanly, and it matches
    exactly the psychological beat it's best at: narrowing attention toward a realization.
    — **[04/06, p]**
19. There is no fixed safe shot duration — cut the instant a still or clip stops earning its
    place; content sets the length, not a stopwatch. — **[11, p]**
20. Never borrow documentary/vérité authenticity codes (grain, wobble, abrupt cut) for invented
    footage of a real subject without knowing you are manufacturing the signal "this really
    happened" for something that didn't — a live ethical flag for BadCode's factual,
    political-economic subject matter. — **[13, A-mixed / ethical]**
