# 07 - Blocking and staging

## 1. The short answer

- Put subjects on different depth planes (near, mid, far), not side by side on one plane — depth reads as space; a flat line-up reads as a photo of a stage.
- A doorway, window, mirror, or wall panel dividing the frame is doing character work, not decor — isolate someone inside a smaller frame and the composition itself says "alone" or "trapped."
- Camera height is a status signal: look up at a subject (low angle) and it reads as powerful; look down (high angle) and it reads as small or vulnerable. This is the single most reliable, most overused tool in the kit — use it once, deliberately, not as a default.
- Distance between two figures in frame is not neutral — it maps roughly to real interpersonal distance (touching, arm's-length, across-a-room, across-a-street), so the gap itself signals intimacy, tension, or estrangement before anyone speaks.
- One shot that changes its own composition (someone crosses the frame, sits, turns their back) can do the narrative work of three separate cuts — cheaper, and it keeps the sense of continuous real space.
- Whoever is closer to camera / larger in frame / facing camera generally reads as dominant; whoever is smaller, farther, or turned away reads as subordinate or excluded — combine two or three of these cues, don't rely on one.
- The "triangle" (three fixed camera positions around a group, forming a triangle) is a continuity discipline, not a composition rule — break it and shots stop cutting together, not because the picture looks wrong in isolation but because eyelines and screen direction contradict each other.
- Whether a shot is "in the triangle" or shot from outside it is a *choice about coverage*, largely irrelevant to us — we don't generate multiple angles of continuous action to cut between.
- Architecture and machines can be staged exactly like actors: which structure is closer to camera, which one is lit, which one blocks the other, which one dominates the vertical frame, all carry the same status/power grammar as two people in a two-shot.
- A held static frame that a figure walks into or out of turns entry/exit into punctuation — the empty frame before or after the figure is part of the sentence, not dead space.
- None of this needs a performer who can act — it needs a decision about geometry, distance, and who/what occupies which part of the frame, which is exactly the kind of decision that can be specified before a single frame is generated.

## 2. The findings

- **Deep staging (figures on multiple depth planes) reads as more spatially real than flat, lateral staging.** Grade: **A (mixed)** — this rests on David Bordwell's textbook analysis (*Film Art*, and his writing on Gregg Toland/Orson Welles' deep-focus technique), which is widely taught but is film-theory description of technique, not a controlled perceptual study. Bordwell contrasts this with **planimetric composition** — his term for a frontal, flat staging where characters sit at 90°/180° to a perpendicular background, deliberately suppressing depth cues (objects of known size placed at equal distance, bases aligned) to make the shot feel graphic and stage-like rather than spatial. Both are legitimate choices; the point is that flatness is not a failure mode, it's a register (Wes Anderson and Yasujirō Ozu use it on purpose). Source: [Bordwell/Toland analysis via Dong Liang's summary](https://www.dliangthinks.me/writing/staging/); [Deep Space glossary, Filmmakers Academy](https://www.filmmakersacademy.com/glossary/deep-space-deep-staging/).

- **Blocking can substitute for editing — moving a figure within a static frame reframes the composition without a cut.** Grade: **P** — practitioner/scholarly consensus, best evidenced by extreme cases: Tsai Ming-liang's cinema, built on zero camera movement and pure blocking, and Hou Hsiao-hsien's static tableaux, where distance and entry/exit *are* the entire visual language. Dong Liang's essay frames staging as attention control achieved by "positioning figures centrally or peripherally, making bodies bigger or eliminating them from view entirely" — an alternative to cutting, camera movement, or close-ups, which are each described as more "interventionist." Source: [A Crash Course on Cinematic Staging](https://www.dliangthinks.me/writing/staging/) *(unverified in sweep against Bordwell's original text — this is a secondary summary)*.

- **Camera height (low angle = power, high angle = weakness) is a real, teachable, widely-agreed convention.** Grade: **P** — this is closer to textbook/practitioner consensus than a single controlled study; it's taught in every film-craft primer and traceable to classical Hollywood and expressionist practice (Welles, again, is the standard reference for extreme low angles reading as menace/power in *Citizen Kane*). Source: [StudioBinder, High Angle Shot](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/high-angle-shot-camera-movement-angle/) — **flagged low-grade source** (content-mill), the claim itself is standard film-school material so treated as P via corroboration, not taken on StudioBinder's authority alone.

- **The "triangle" is a coverage discipline for cutting multiple angles of the same dialogue together, not a composition law.** Grade: **p** — one named, concrete case: editor Lisa Churgin (in a podcast, cited on Roger Deakins' own forum) describes cinematographer Sławomir Idziak mixing "in the triangle" and "out of the triangle" setups on *Gattaca*, and the footage "didn't cut together so they had to reshoot it." This is evidence about *continuity between multiple shots of the same action*, which is not how we work (we generate one shot, we don't cut between three angles of the same blocking). Source: [Roger Deakins forum — Reading Recommendations for Camera Blocking](https://www.rogerdeakins.com/forums/topic/reading-recommendations-for-camera-blocking-scene-design/).

- **Deakins himself rejects blocking as rule-following: "the only real rule is 'if it feels right.'"** Grade: **p** — one named practitioner, one forum post (Deakins posts on his own forum under his own name, so attribution is solid, but it's an informal post, not a published interview). He does affirm one concrete rule of thumb — it's hard to cut two close-ups together when both characters look the same screen direction — while noting exceptions exist. Source: [rogerdeakins.com forum, same thread as above](https://www.rogerdeakins.com/forums/topic/reading-recommendations-for-camera-blocking-scene-design/).

- **Deakins won't finalize blocking or lighting before seeing a rehearsal — staging is discovered with the actors, not imposed on them.** Grade: **p** — one named practitioner (Deakins), reported secondhand via a search summary rather than a direct quote pulled from primary text; treat as directionally right but re-verify the exact wording before quoting him directly. Source: same Deakins forum thread *(unverified in sweep — summary, not primary quote)*.

- **A frame-within-a-frame (doorway, window, mirror, wall panel) dividing or containing a character carries legible subtext: isolation, entrapment, being watched.** Grade: **P** — broad practitioner/critical consensus with a canonical, specific, checkable example: John Ford's final shot of *The Searchers*, John Wayne's character framed and left outside a cabin doorway as it closes — one of the most-analyzed compositions in film criticism, standing for exclusion/not-belonging. Source: [StudioBinder, Frame Within a Frame](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-frame-within-a-frame-definition/) — **flagged low-grade source**, but the Ford example is independently well-documented in film scholarship generally, so the *example* is trustworthy even where the summarizing blog is not.

- **Physical distance between two figures in frame maps onto real-world interpersonal distance zones, and audiences read it that way.** Grade: **A** for the underlying anthropology — Edward T. Hall's proxemics (*The Hidden Dimension*, 1966) is a well-established, if dated, framework: intimate (touching), personal (~1.5–4 ft), social (~4–12 ft), public (12 ft+). Grade drops to **P** for the *film-specific* application (shot size mapped to these zones — extreme close-up/close-up ≈ intimate, medium shots ≈ personal, long shots ≈ social, extreme long shots ≈ public) — this is a standard film-studies teaching device, not a study of how audiences actually perceive filmed proxemics. Source: [Understanding Film wiki, Proxemic Patterns](http://understandingfilm.wikifoundry.com/page/4.+Proxemic+Patterns); [Filmmakers Academy glossary](https://www.filmmakersacademy.com/glossary/proxemic-patterns/).

- **Ozu staged actors frontally but kept eyelines deliberately off-kilter so they never quite address the camera** — a specific, named technique for making a flat, planimetric composition feel alive rather than posed. Grade: **p** — one director's identifiable, widely-discussed practice (his "360-degree" continuity-breaking editing and graphic-match cutting are well documented in film scholarship), reported here via a secondary summary rather than a primary Bordwell/Ozu-scholarship citation. Source: [A Crash Course on Cinematic Staging](https://www.dliangthinks.me/writing/staging/) *(unverified in sweep)*.

## 3. What survives our constraints

We don't direct actors, we don't rehearse, and we don't cut between multiple camera angles of the same continuous action. That kills a specific, large chunk of classical blocking theory — everything about *cutting together* coverage of one staged action (the triangle rule, eyeline-match discipline across multiple shots, "don't cross the line" as a multi-shot continuity rule) is close to irrelevant, because we generate one shot at a time and either keep it or regenerate it, we don't intercut angles of it.

What survives, fully, because it's a **single-frame composition spec**, not a blocking-over-time discipline:

- Depth staging (near/mid/far placement) — specify it directly in the shot description: what's in the foreground, what's in the mid-ground, what's in the background, and at what relative scale.
- Camera height / power geometry — pick the angle and commit; this is a one-shot decision, not a rehearsal outcome.
- Frame-within-a-frame / architectural division — this is scene-dressing plus camera placement, entirely specifiable up front: "the machine's threshold frames the figure," "the console divides the two structures."
- Proxemic distance between figures (or between a figure and a structure) — pick the gap deliberately; it's a single compositional parameter.
- Planimetric/flat staging as a deliberate register — also a single-frame choice, and genuinely useful for BadCode's near-black, monumental-architecture register, which already leans toward frontal, graphic composition over naturalistic depth.
- Entrance/exit as punctuation — this needs *motion*, which for us means our one available lever: an 8-second clip with one camera move, or a figure/element entering or leaving a held frame. It survives in a reduced form: we can specify "the figure begins outside frame and enters" or "the frame holds after the figure leaves," we just can't build a scene where that beat pays off against three other shots — it has to carry its whole meaning alone, in one clip.

What genuinely dies, or needs heavy translation:

- Anything about *changing* blocking mid-shot to avoid a cut (the "one shot does the work of three" claim) partially survives only within a single 8-second generated clip with one camera move — we can ask for a push-in that reveals a second figure, say, but we cannot choreograph a performer crossing a room in reaction to dialogue, because there's no performer and no dialogue being read live.
- Rehearsal-discovered blocking (Deakins' method of not lighting until he's seen actors move) has no equivalent — we never discover blocking by watching a body improvise; we specify geometry and then judge the still or clip that comes back. The honest translation is: treat every generation as the "rehearsal," and iterate the composition spec the way a director would iterate blocking notes, rather than expecting the first generation to be right.
- The triangle/coverage discipline is not usable at all in its original form (it exists to solve a multi-shot cutting problem we don't have) but the underlying idea — decide up front whether a shot reads as "with" a subject or "against" them, and don't accidentally contradict that in a sequence of separate shots cut together — is worth carrying into how we plan the sequence of stills/clips in a comic page or short, even though no single generation call needs it.

## 4. Contested

- **Rule-of-thumb blocking discipline vs. "if it feels right."** The Deakins-forum thread surfaces both sides in one place: editor Lisa Churgin's account of the *Gattaca* reshoot argues for a hard rule (commit to inside-or-outside the triangle, because mixing them is provably uncuttable), while Roger Deakins, in the same thread, states his only real rule is "if it feels right," treating fixed formulas as training wheels to be discarded once you understand why they exist. Both are talking about the same craft from opposite ends: Churgin is describing a failure mode she watched happen (a concrete, falsifiable claim — the footage didn't cut), Deakins is describing a mature practitioner's relationship to any rule once the underlying logic is internalized. Neither is wrong; they're answering different questions ("what stops footage from being usable" vs "how should an experienced DP think about rules day to day").

## 5. Myths - never cite

- **"Low angle = powerful, high angle = weak" is a fixed, universal code that works every time.** It's a strong, well-taught convention (P grade above), but treating it as a law rather than a convention is the myth — context, lens, and what's in the rest of the frame can override or mute it, and using it on every "important" subject flattens it into a cliché rather than a choice. The finding is real; the "it always works" version of it is not.
- **"Never cross the line" as a rule about a single generated shot.** The 180-degree line/triangle discipline exists to keep multiple cuts of the *same continuous action* spatially coherent when intercut. It is not a rule about how to compose one shot in isolation, and importing it as a checklist item for a single still or one 8-second clip is a category error — there is nothing to "cross the line" against when there's no second angle being cut against it.

## 6. Vocabulary worth having

| Term | Plain-English gloss | What it buys you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deep staging | Figures placed at different distances from camera, not side by side | Makes a frame feel like real space, not a lineup |
| Planimetric / flat staging | Figures frontal, parallel to a flat background, depth cues suppressed | A deliberate graphic, stage-like register — matches BadCode's monumental-architecture look |
| Frame within a frame | A doorway/window/structure forming a smaller frame around a subject | Free subtext — containment, isolation, being watched — with no dialogue |
| Proxemics | The real-world distance zones people read as intimate/personal/social/public | Lets you set the emotional temperature of a two-shot by the gap alone |
| Triangle (staging) | Three fixed camera positions around a group, used to keep multi-shot coverage cuttable | A continuity tool for multi-shot scenes — mostly not our problem, but explains why some three-hander shots look "off" if ever attempted |
| Power geometry | Height, size, closeness, and facing direction used together to signal status | A stackable toolkit — use two or three cues together, not one alone, for a reading that doesn't feel like a gimmick |
| Screen direction / eyeline | Which way a subject is facing or looking relative to the frame edge | Determines whether two figures (or a figure and a structure) read as confronting, ignoring, or acknowledging each other |

## 7. Open questions

- No source in this sweep gives a controlled, empirical (non-anecdotal) test of the low-angle-equals-power convention's actual strength or its failure conditions — it's taught everywhere but the sweep found no study measuring how reliably audiences read it, or when it stops working. Settling this would need a search into perception/media-psychology literature specifically, not film-craft writing.
- The Ozu eyeline claim and the "Deakins won't finalize lighting before rehearsal" claim both came through secondary summaries rather than primary text. Verifying them would mean going to Bordwell's own writing on Ozu directly, and searching Deakins' forum/interviews for a direct quote rather than a paraphrase.
- Nothing here addresses staging for **architecture/machines as the sole subject** with any named practitioner citation — the section 3 translation for that case (treat structures like actors: closer/larger/lit = dominant) is reasoned by analogy from the power-geometry and frame-division findings above, not sourced from an architectural-photography or industrial-cinematography practitioner. A follow-up sweep aimed specifically at architectural photography (e.g., how monumental/brutalist architecture is conventionally shot for scale and dominance) would firm this up rather than leaving it as an inference.
- How much of the "blocking as substitute for editing" claim holds for a *single* generated shot with no follow-on cut was not tested against any source that discusses one-shot-only filmmaking (as opposed to long-take cinema, which still exists inside a larger cut film) — worth a targeted look at single-image / single-shot narrative craft (e.g., photojournalism single-frame storytelling) rather than long-take film theory.
