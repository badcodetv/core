# 09 — The design grammar of calm, official and wrong

*Research brief, 2026-09-29. One Sonnet agent, 8 web searches (cap 8). Raw research, not house policy — the ruled layer is [`direction.md`](./direction.md) once Kai rules on it.*

**Evidence grades:** *verified* = the agent fetched a page that says it · *secondary* = a search snippet or secondary source said it · *(unverified)* = recalled from the model's own memory, not checked. The agent could not see images: every description of a logo comes from text.

## Headline

Sci-fi corporate menace is one shared grammar (wide extended Microgramma caps plus a monogram or wings), and that is the cliche to avoid. British calm-and-wrong (Protect and Survive, Scarfolk, Threads) gets its menace from plain type, deadpan language and a single mark that is not a company. Since BadCode is a mind rather than a company, borrow the public notice, not the logo: a plain grotesque wordmark plus one blunt, drawable, mildly prohibitive sign (a full stop, a ballot box, a red ring around a dot).

## Findings

- **[verified]** Weyland-Yutani's wordmark is Eurostile/Microgramma Bold Extended in all caps, with the slogan 'Building Better Worlds' in a striped semi-slab face (Transmission, Letraset 1983). The 1979 identity is credited to John Mollo, Ridley Scott and Ron Cobb; the 1986 version replaced the winged mark with an entwined W and Y monogram. — <https://fontsinuse.com/uses/35613/weyland-yutani-corp-logo-and-slogan-in-aliens>
- **[verified]** Microgramma was made by Aldo Novarese and Alessandro Butti for Nebiolo in 1952. It is a wide geometric titling face with superellipse curves. Its Bold Extended cut is used across Alien, Star Trek, Space: 1999 and Thunderbirds, so 'wide extended caps' now means 'sci-fi' to almost everyone. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microgramma_(typeface)>
- **[secondary]** Lumon (Severance) uses Manifold Extended, a wide face modelled on Microgramma, with a water droplet as the counter of the O. The logo is credited to Tansy Michaud and drew on pharmaceutical branding. Production designer Jeremy Hindle blended 1960s-80s corporate looks and Eero Saarinen modernism, under cold fluorescent light. The menace comes from sterility, anachronism (400 vintage vehicles) and a space where the exit is missing, not from anything aggressive in the mark. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumon_Industries>
- **[verified]** Scarfolk (Richard Littler) works through contradiction: official-looking material carrying a disturbing message, ending with lines like 'For more information please reread'. It borrows real 1970s sources (Protect and Survive, Charley Says, The Finishing Line, Penguin and Pelican covers, Action Man packaging). Littler says he likes 'the clean lines and simple typefaces of modernism', keeps roughly 20,000 period images, and aims to trigger 'involuntary memories'. Many pieces are mistaken for real artefacts. — <https://www.collectorsweekly.com/articles/visiting-scarfolk/>
- **[verified]** The Protect and Survive booklet used Futura Extra Bold Condensed, Helvetica and Times in brown and orange. There is nothing sci-fi about it, just plain office type. Its menace is the voice: Patrick Allen's 'calm, clipped vowels' explaining how to wrap the dead in polythene. Published in 1980, it was seen as 'sinister and quite pathetic', and CND publicly mocked it. Calm authority is most chilling when it is also inadequate. — <https://fontsinuse.com/uses/33645/protect-and-survive-nuclear-attack-guide>
- **[verified]** UK public information films came from the Central Office of Information and were supplied free to broadcasters to fill unsold airtime. Their menace uses simple devices: a black-robed hooded figure with Donald Pleasence narrating (Lonely Water, 1973), or a cartoon cat voiced by Kenny Everett (Charley Says). The scary thing was a plain silhouette or a friendly mascot delivering a death warning. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_information_film>
- **[verified]** The Day Today's title sequence, by Jump Design (Richard Norley, Russell Hilliard) with ITN's graphics staff, parodies authority through excess. Their brief was to imagine what a megalomaniac editor would order, hence 'loads of globes', and the sequence ends in a globe merged into the word TODAY. The graphics stay slick and professional. This is the opposite register to calm, and it is the British shorthand for a pompous, self-serious institution. It is a warning that over-produced authority reads as comedy. — <https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/the-day-today/>
- **[secondary]** Threads (1984) embedded real Protect and Survive materials and used flat narration by Paul Vaughan, plus consultation with scientists and planners. Critics called it 'visceral horror' delivered in a matter-of-fact way. The documentary flatness is what devastated British viewers. (White caption cards on black and statistics are how I remember it; that was not confirmed on the page.) — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threads_(1984_film)>
- **[verified]** Vault-Tec inverts the calm register: cheerful 1950s retro-futurism, the Vault Boy mascot, lunchboxes and bobbleheads, and a 'safety for an uncertain future' tagline over unethical experiments. Smiling mascot plus horror is the American form of the joke. It is now the most parodied device in the set (Buy n Large and similar), and it is not British. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault-Tec>
- **[secondary]** E Corp's logo was inspired by Enron's, per creator Sam Esmail, and is credited to Mark Pollard (via the Fictional Brands Archive). fsociety's mask is a near-copy of the Guy Fawkes mask. The corporate villain borrows a real disgraced logo, and the rebel borrows a real protest icon. Both are pastiche, not new marks. — <https://www.threads.com/@fictionalbrandsarchive/post/C-Z468aNAmg?hl=en>
- **[secondary]** Utopia (Channel 4, 2013-14) is remembered for a palette of acid yellow, cyan and magenta, a Technicolor / spot-colour comic-print look, and saturated British landscapes. A single unexpected colour in an ordinary British setting reads as wrong without any prop. It was the one colour choice here that escaped the black-and-red default. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia_(British_TV_series)>
- **[secondary]** Black Mirror's title card is a cracked black glass rectangle with lettering. One secondary source says the poster title is based on Proxima Nova Bold, which I do not fully trust. The idea is a black screen with a fracture, and it has become the generic 'tech dystopia' shorthand (glitch, cracked-glass fonts and effects are sold everywhere). — <https://fontmeme.com/black-mirror-font/>
- **[*(unverified)*]** Recalled, not checked: real British official graphics use plain type (Gill Sans, Rail Alphabet, Univers/Helvetica), a crest or a single pictogram, one flat colour, and reference numbers ('Ref. 2034/1'). Menace in this idiom is in the words and the setting, and the mark stays boring. Also: a red ring is the international prohibition-sign convention, a UK ballot is an X in a box, and Terminator/HAL is a single red eye. Recalled-unverified: HAL is a red lens in a black slab, Cyberdyne's own logo, OCP's slogans, and Aperture's calm GLaDOS voice. The Wikipedia fetches for those were empty.

## Precedents

- **Weyland-Yutani (Alien, 1979/1986)** — Wide extended all-caps wordmark in Eurostile/Microgramma Bold. The 1979 identity had wings; the 1986 one an entwined W and Y monogram. The slogan is set in a striped semi-slab face. **Lesson:** This is the template for corporate menace. It is unmistakable but it is now the default grammar, and BadCode is not a corporation. — <https://fontsinuse.com/uses/35613/weyland-yutani-corp-logo-and-slogan-in-aliens>
- **Lumon Industries (Severance)** — Wide Manifold Extended caps, a droplet as the counter of the O, and segmented map-like lines. Pharma-style 1970s corporate sterility. **Lesson:** The recent high-water mark of the genre. It shows 'calm and wrong' is a design register, but copying it would look like fan-art of it. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumon_Industries>
- **Scarfolk Council (Richard Littler)** — Faded 1970s public-information posters, pastel and large-type, aged as if found, with deadpan taglines. **Lesson:** The best British precedent for authority plus dread. The lesson is that the deadpan sentence carries the joke. It is one artist's living territory, so don't clone it. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarfolk>
- **Protect and Survive (1976 booklet, 1980 edition, films)** — Plain Futura Extra Bold Condensed, Helvetica and Times in brown and orange. A simple family-shelter image. Narration by Patrick Allen. **Lesson:** Real government material was more frightening than any fictional logo. Plain type plus calm voice equals dread. — <https://fontsinuse.com/uses/33645/protect-and-survive-nuclear-attack-guide>
- **COI public information films (Lonely Water, Charley Says)** — A black-robed hooded figure narrated by Donald Pleasence; a cartoon ginger cat. **Lesson:** A single silhouette or friendly mascot can be the whole identity of a warning, and British viewers remember them for decades. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_information_film>
- **The Day Today (1994)** — Many spinning globes, orchestral stabs, slick ITN-made graphics, and a globe merged into 'TODAY'. **Lesson:** Excess reads as comic pomposity. For calm authority, do the opposite: subtract. — <https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/the-day-today/>
- **Threads (1984)** — Documentary flatness with real civil-defence materials and bureaucratic narration. **Lesson:** The flat delivery of catastrophe is a British form of authority that carries dread without any logo. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threads_(1984_film)>
- **Vault-Tec (Fallout)** — Retro-1950s optimism, a smiling mascot (Vault Boy), cheerful merchandise. **Lesson:** Cheerful mascot over horror is the American register and is overused. BadCode's narrator is regretful, not cheerful. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault-Tec>
- **E Corp / fsociety (Mr. Robot)** — An Enron-derived corporate E in a bank-like lockup; a Guy Fawkes-like mask. **Lesson:** Borrowing a real, disgraced logo (Enron) carries a moral charge. It also shows pastiche marks tend to be recognised as pastiche. — <https://www.threads.com/@fictionalbrandsarchive/post/C-Z468aNAmg?hl=en>
- **Black Mirror title** — A black screen with a crack and a plain title. **Lesson:** A single black shape can carry a whole brand. It is also the tech-dystopia default, and glitch/crack effects are stock. — <https://fontmeme.com/black-mirror-font/>

## Clichés to avoid

- Wide extended Microgramma/Eurostile caps (Weyland-Yutani, Star Trek, Lumon, Alien-style) as the wordmark
- Corporate monogram, entwined initials, wings or hex/shield emblems
- 'Building Better Worlds'-style pastiche slogan lockups and fake 'Est. 20XX' or 'Corporation' suffixes
- Glitch, scanline, cracked-glass and RGB-split effects (Black Mirror / cyberpunk stock)
- A glowing red eye or lens in a black slab used literally (HAL, Terminator, Cylon/KITT scanner bar)
- 1950s smiling-mascot retro optimism (Vault Boy, Buy n Large)
- Guy Fawkes mask, hoodie or 'hacker' iconography
- Cloning Scarfolk's aged 1970s-poster look wholesale (one living artist's territory, and already widely imitated)
- Over-produced authority (spinning globes, orchestral stabs), which reads as Day Today comedy
- Curly brace, angle brackets, cursor blocks and any other code punctuation

## Risks

- **(high)** Corporate-sci-fi grammar mismatches the fiction — Every logo in the sinister-institution set represents a company. BadCode is a superintelligence and an art collective. A wide-caps corporate look would say 'Weyland-Yutani cosplay', not 'regretful warning from the future'.
- **(high)** The target reader may not share the references — A working-class UK viewer on Facebook or TikTok may have seen Threads and Public Information Films, but Severance, Fallout, Mr Robot and Aperture are niche or US-centred. The mark has to work with no reference at all and not depend on recognising a joke.
- **(medium)** Calm and official may not survive a 16px avatar — Deadpan authority works through text and voice. A pure wordmark disappears at avatar size, so the mark needs a single-glance shape (dot, box, ring) that is independent of the wordmark.
- **(medium)** Red is both the Terminator cliche and a party colour — A red dot is instantly legible as machine menace but also as Labour or 'red team' politics in the UK. That is exactly the drift-right audience's trigger. The choice of red and blue (already picked) sits close to Labour/Tory. Worth testing a non-party red or a non-red dot colour. Unverified: I did not research party palettes.
- **(medium)** Imitating government could read as impersonation — Crown, Royal Arms, COI-style crests or civil-defence pictograms are real official marks, and the repo rule is never to impersonate a real organisation. Use the grammar (plain type, ruled box, reference number), never the actual emblem. Recalled, not checked: restrictions on use of the Royal Arms.
- **(low)** Retro-nostalgia can become a fetish — Scarfolk-style 1970s aesthetics are heavily saturated on the internet and read as 'creepy nostalgia', not as a forward warning. BadCode's register is near-black futurism, so use British restraint without period decay.
- **(low)** Search-budget gaps mean some marks were not described — Cyberdyne, OCP, Umbrella, Tyrell, Abstergo, InGen, Massive Dynamic, Aperture, Black Mesa, Brass Eye and Scarfolk's own site could not be verified. Statements about them are memory only.

## Logo ideas this agent proposed

### The Full Stop

**Sketch in words:** Wordmark in lowercase 'badcode' set in a plain grotesque (Helvetica Bold / Univers weight), tight tracking, off-white (about #ECEAE4) on near-black. After the final 'e' comes one solid red full stop, with a diameter equal to the x-height of the 'o' (the same size as the counter, filled), sitting on the baseline, with a gap of about half its diameter. Avatar or favicon: a big 'b' with the same red full stop. Sticker: black text on white plus the red dot.

**Why it fits:** The narrator speaks with total authority, and a full stop is a sentence that has been ended, which carries both the future's finality and the bridge to the Terminator dot. It is the calmest possible mark, the same plainness as a Protect and Survive booklet. It can also be the ident: a single red dot appears, then the word is typed before it.

**Weakness:** Full-stop logos are common and it may read as 'dot' branding. The word shape alone is not distinct, so it depends on the red dot having character. It may be read as a bullet hole or a Japan-flag joke.

### The Ballot Box

**Sketch in words:** A square outline (stroke about 1/12 of the side, off-white on near-black) with a solid red circle dead centre, about one third of the side across, where a UK voter's X would go. Wordmark 'BADCODE' in small, widely spaced plain caps beneath, cap height about 1/8 of the square. As an avatar, the square with the dot alone fills the frame.

**Why it fits:** It speaks directly to the political story. It is the box you tick, with a machine eye in it instead of your cross, and it is the UK's most familiar democratic gesture. Calm, official, geometric, drawable in two strokes, and its silhouette is strong at 16px. It sits well in the near-black register and needs no font.

**Weakness:** May read as a 'record' button or a UI checkbox, and the dot-in-box is generic geometry. Red with a ballot invites party readings, and a ballot could seem to advocate voting, which is not the message.

### The Prohibition Eye

**Sketch in words:** A red ring (outer diameter 1, ring thickness about 0.14) in the international prohibition-sign proportion, with a solid off-white or black dot in the centre about 0.3 across, no slash. On near-black, the ring is red and the dot is off-white with one tiny highlight. Wordmark 'BADCODE' set plainly beside or below in one weight of sans caps.

**Why it fits:** The core message is 'humans, please don't make this obvious mistake', and a red ring is the world's 'don't' sign. The centre dot is the machine eye, so the same mark reads as warning and watcher. It is a very fast two-circle drawing for a marker, and it works as a stencil, a sticker and a 2-second ident: the eye opens inside the ring.

**Weakness:** Looks like a lens, a target, or the Pokeball or Beats family, and a red ring with a dot is close to some existing marks. The Terminator cliche is exactly what the founders flagged, so the ring must do enough to feel like a sign and not a HAL lens.

### The Slit

**Sketch in words:** A long black horizontal bar with ratio about 6:1 on off-white (or the reverse), like a redaction bar. A small red dot sits at the left end, as if the bar is a mail slot or visor and something red looks out. The wordmark 'BADCODE' is set in plain grotesque caps directly above the bar at a size where the caps height equals the bar height.

**Why it fits:** It ties the redacted-document look of British official secrecy to the thin blade of cold light already in the register, and to an eye watching through a slot. Drawable in seconds.

**Weakness:** It is closest to the Cylon/KITT scanner and to plain censor bars, and as a square avatar a bar can look like a blank. The weakest of the four, included because it is the most register-native.
