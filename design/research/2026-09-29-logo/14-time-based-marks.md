# 14 — Logos that tell time or change over time

*Research brief, 2026-09-29. One Sonnet agent, 8 web searches (cap 8). Raw research, not house policy — the ruled layer is [`direction.md`](./direction.md) once Kai rules on it.*

**Evidence grades:** *verified* = the agent fetched a page that says it · *secondary* = a search snippet or secondary source said it · *(unverified)* = recalled from the model's own memory, not checked. The agent could not see images: every description of a logo comes from text.

## Headline

Every durable identity that changes over time freezes almost everything and lets exactly one thing move (Doomsday Clock: face fixed, hand moves; Whitney: word fixed, W moves; MTV: proportions fixed; On Kawara: format fixed, date moves). So an "N BC" countdown works only if the frame and a red dot stay fixed and only the number ticks. I found no prior art for a fictional "Before Collapse" count used as a brand device, but "BC" already reads as Before Christ, and Terminator shows a doom date that keeps slipping.

## Findings

- **[verified]** The Doomsday Clock is a fixed image with one variable. Martyl Langsdorf drew it for the June 1947 Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists cover, set at seven minutes to midnight. It has been on every cover since and is now the Bulletin's website logo. The face never changes and the hand position is the news, reset in an annual January announcement. As of 27 January 2026 it stands at 85 seconds to midnight, the closest ever, and the Bulletin cites AI among the reasons. Bierut's 2007 Pentagram redesign moved it from seven to five minutes and made it the centrepiece of the identity; one search snippet says it is 'four dots and two lines'. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomsday_Clock>
- **[verified]** The Bulletin page confirms the 2026 setting (85 seconds), the farthest-ever setting (17 minutes in 1991) and that AI now sits inside the clock's scope. So BadCode's future-warning frame sits right next to an existing, extremely famous doom-countdown mark, and any clock-face or midnight imagery would invite direct comparison. — <https://thebulletin.org/doomsday-clock/>
- **[verified]** Long Now Foundation: the logo is a capital X with an overline (vinculum), meaning 10,000 in Roman numerals. The Foundation writes years with five digits (02026) to head off the Y10K problem, so the date format itself is a piece of identity. The 10,000-year clock shows a five-digit year. The mark is a numeral plus a bar, which is simple to draw and easy to remember, and it works because it is a rule, not decoration. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Now_Foundation>
- **[verified]** The Climate Clock (Golan, Hofstadter, Carpenter, Boyd; New York, Sept 2020) counts down to 1.5C of warming, started at '7 years and 102 days', and the deadline moves: it extends when emissions fall. A second figure, the percentage of energy that is renewable, was added as 'a tinge of optimism'. This is the best precedent for a countdown that is not fatalist. It is also a warning: a deadline-clock mark already exists in the climate space. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_Clock>
- **[verified]** Whitney Museum 'responsive W' (Experimental Jetset, 2013): a zig-zag line that folds to fit any artwork, like a carpenter's folding ruler. The only fixed element is the word 'Whitney' in the upper left, and the designers say 'in order to show movement, you actually need a fixed element'. The system reduces to five instruction sentences, in the manner of Sol LeWitt. A Hyperallergic piece titled 'The Whitney Museum's New Logo Goes Nowhere' shows the approach was criticised (I saw only the title). — <https://www.jetset.nl/archive/whitney-museum-identity>
- **[verified]** MTV (Manhattan Design, 1981; Frank Olinsky, Patti Rogoff, Pat Gorman, under Fred Seibert): the logo was 'constantly branded with different colors, patterns, and images', while 'its general shape and proportions' stayed constant. The big block M was drawn by Rogoff and the spray-painted 'TV' by Olinsky. This is the model for a 2-second ident that can be re-skinned every time and still be the same mark. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTV>
- **[secondary]** Fully generative identities give up the single mark. MIT Media Lab (E Roon Kang and Richard The, 2011) uses an algorithm that makes 40,000+ variants from three shapes, one per person. Casa da Musica (Sagmeister, 2007) takes six profiles / 17 planes of the building and fills the facets with colours sampled from any image. Both are celebrated and hard to remember as one thing, and neither is used as a channel avatar in the sources I saw. For a tiny avatar this is the wrong end of the spectrum. — <https://www.fastcompany.com/1663378/mit-media-labs-brilliant-new-logo-has-40000-permutations-video>
- **[verified]** On Kawara's Today (Date) paintings started 4 January 1966: white lettering of the day's date, in the language of the country he was in, on a dark ground, in eight fixed horizontal sizes. A painting not finished on its day was destroyed, and each one is boxed with a local newspaper clipping. Nearly 3,000 were made in more than 112 cities. A date can be a whole identity when the rule is severe and the variable is only the date. Backgrounds got darker and the typeface moved to Futura over time, so even a rigid system drifts. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Kawara>
- **[secondary]** Factory Records used catalogue numbers as identity. Saville put the emphasis on the FAC number, 'usurping the idea of branding'. FAC 51 was the Hacienda, FAC 61 a lawsuit with Martin Hannett, FAC 98 a hair salon, FAC 99 a dental operation. A counter that gets attached to everything, not only records, turns a boring serial into a personality. BadCode's site already labels the GPOM story BC-000 (from project memory), so a count-based numbering scheme would need reconciling with that. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Saville_(graphic_designer)>
- **[verified]** Fictional calendars as brand devices exist, but I found none that is a countdown logo. Nine Inch Nails' Year Zero (42 Entertainment, 2007) is set in 2022, which the fictional government renames 'Year 0', but the identity was the ARG artefacts (USB drives, 'I AM TRYING TO BELIEVE', the Presence's hands), not a date stamp. Warhammer 40k uses dates like 0.123.456.M41 (check number, year-fraction, year, millennium; only year plus millennium in casual use), read from a search summary. Fallout fixes its Great War on 23 Oct 2077. Homeworld uses BHL/AHL, which is the same Before/After structure (read from a search summary). In one broad search for 'Before Collapse' as a dating system, nothing matched. That is weak evidence that the space is open, not proof. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_Zero_(game)>
- **[verified]** A countdown to a fixed date has a shelf-life problem, and franchises have hit it. The Terminator's Judgment Day started as 29 August 1997, then moved to July 2003, April 2011, October 2017 and 'the early 2020s'. Before Present (BP) dating had a similar problem: because the 'present' moves, scientists froze the epoch at 1 January 1950. And 'BC' is already Before Christ, so '8 BC' is a real historical year and would read as ancient history to a lay reader. That is potentially a joke ('the world before the collapse is ancient history'), but also a confusion and a search-engine problem. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminator_(franchise)>

## Clichés to avoid

- Clock faces, hands, hourglasses, midnight: the Doomsday Clock owns them.
- Seven-segment red LED countdown digits (the Climate Clock and every bomb-timer prop).
- Film-leader '10, 9, 8...' countdowns.
- Glitch, binary rain, Matrix green, circuit-board patterns.
- A calendar-page icon.
- Skulls and radiation trefoils.
- A ticking sound as a logo.
- '2034' as a big apocalyptic tagline.
- A red dot with a lens ring, which is HAL's eye, and a red eye on a chrome skull, which is the Terminator.

## Risks

- **(high)** 'BC' reads as Before Christ — Working-class UK readers will see 8 BC as a Roman-era year, or as a religious reference, unless it is explained once. Nothing else pairs BC with a small number on a modern channel, but 'BC' is also British Columbia and a common initialism, so search and handle collisions are likely. Fix: subtitle it 'Before Collapse' the first time and treat the misreading as a joke, not something to fight.
- **(high)** A yearly-changing mark breaks recognition — Whitney and MTV work because one element is fixed. An avatar that shows a different number each January has to keep the frame, the colour and a red dot identical, or it stops being one mark. Old avatars and old videos will also show stale numbers. That is fine, even good, in an archive, but broken in a live profile.
- **(medium)** A hard doom date invites the doomer reading and can fail — Terminator had to keep moving its date. The-reader.md says never to raise automation fear without naming the beneficiary, so a bare countdown could feed fatalism. Climate Clock's deadline moves with behaviour, which is better. In the BadCode fiction the number can be 'held' or 'reset' by the good branch. If 2034 passes and the world is fine, the warning worked, and the brand should say so.
- **(medium)** Too close to the Doomsday Clock and Climate Clock — Both are well-known and both count down to catastrophe, and the Doomsday Clock now names AI as a factor. BadCode's difference is that the countdown is in a character's voice (a future AI) and pairs with a fiction, not an institution. Do not draw a clock.
- **(medium)** Numbers alone are not unmistakable — A numeral on a black square looks like a jersey number or a notification badge. The mark needs something that is only BadCode: the red dot and the near-black, thin-light register.
- **(low)** Generative or fully dynamic systems are out of reach for two people — Casa da Musica and MIT Media Lab needed tooling and give up a single mark. Keep the variable to one number.

## Logo ideas this agent proposed

### The Stamp

**Sketch in words:** A near-black square with rounded corners at about 8 percent of the width. A big white heavy-grotesque numeral (currently 8) is centred, at about 60 percent of the height. Under it, in small widely spaced white capitals, sits 'BC'. In the top-right corner is one solid red dot (#cc2b37) whose diameter is about 12 percent of the width, like a status LED. Every January the numeral drops by one (8, 7, 6...); the square, the BC and the red dot never change. The 2-second ident is the numeral flipping down one step with the red dot pulsing once.

**Why it fits:** It is the On Kawara and Whitney rule in practice: one moving element and a fixed frame. 'BC' is both the initials and 'Before Collapse', the number is the countdown, and the red dot is the Terminator eye without copying a lens ring. It can be drawn in a few seconds and rubber-stamped or stencilled.

**Weakness:** The numeral is generic and could pass for a notification badge. 'BC' reads as Before Christ until it is explained. It goes stale each January. The 8 is the only part that carries the story, so it must be explained once.

### Eight Lights

**Sketch in words:** On a near-black field, one horizontal row of eight equal circles, each about 60 percent of the gap between centres. Seven are thin white outlines and the rightmost is solid red. Each January the rightmost outlined dot is removed, so the row shrinks and the red dot is always the last. In 2033 only one red dot is left on the black, and that is the Terminator dot. No text or numeral at all.

**Why it fits:** A countdown drawn with no language. The mark converges on the red dot, which makes the endpoint itself the logo. It echoes the 'tiny LED points of light' already in the visual register and the Doomsday Clock's dots, without a clock face. It is easy to draw with a marker.

**Weakness:** Eight dots at 16 pixels turn to mush, and a row of dots looks like a loading indicator or a Morse code strip. It loses one dot a year, so the mark keeps changing until it is a single dot. In 2034 it prompts 'nothing happened'. It has no letters, so it only works alongside the wordmark.

### The Catalogue Number

**Sketch in words:** No pictogram. The whole identity is a fixed line of tracked-out monospace capitals in white on near-black: 'BC 008' followed by a single red full stop (a period, the size of the stem width). The 008 is a zero-padded years-to-collapse count and each release carries the number of the year it came out (BC 008 in 2026, BC 007 in 2027). Sleeves, video end-cards and stickers repeat the same line, and the site archive reads as a countdown from top to bottom.

**Why it fits:** Factory Records and Peter Saville: a serial number can be the brand. The Long Now's five-digit year is a zero-padded date that is easy to imitate. A stencil is very cheap, works on a record sleeve and in a channel banner, and makes the archive itself a timeline.

**Weakness:** It is a wordmark with no image, so it is weak as a tiny avatar. Site labels already use BC-000 for GPOM, so the numbering would clash with an existing scheme. A new number sequence implies a release schedule.

### The Deadline

**Sketch in words:** A white numeral (8 now, then 7...) on a near-black square, with a single thin red horizontal line above it, the length of the numeral's width and about one-fifth of a stem thick, floating a stem-width above the top. It is the Long Now's overline logic, and a literal dead line. A small red dot on the baseline right of the numeral is optional. The numeral changes each January, but the red line and the black square never do.

**Why it fits:** It gets the Long Now trick (a numeral plus an overline is the entire mark) and a pun that suits the voice: 'deadline'. The horizontal red line also rhymes with the house register's thin blade of cold light, turned sideways.

**Weakness:** The pun is verbal and needs reading. A bar over a number also looks like a mathematical mean or a Roman numeral thousand, so it is a near-echo of Long Now's mark. It has no BC in it, so it is not unmistakably ours until the name sits beside it.
