# 06 — Warning symbols, and warnings meant to survive time

*Research brief, 2026-09-29. One Sonnet agent, 8 web searches (cap 8). Raw research, not house policy — the ruled layer is [`direction.md`](./direction.md) once Kai rules on it.*

**Evidence grades:** *verified* = the agent fetched a page that says it · *secondary* = a search snippet or secondary source said it · *(unverified)* = recalled from the model's own memory, not checked. The agent could not see images: every description of a logo comes from text.

## Headline

Every warning that has ever worked is a colour-and-shape grammar with a slot for a meaning that gets taught, and the two best-tested ones (biohazard, the 2007 radiation sign) succeeded by being memorable before being meaningful. The strongest BadCode move from this angle is to borrow the grammar (a triangle or roundel, black on near-black, one red light) but put something in the pictogram slot that no standard sign contains, a single red dot. The dot then reads as "warning" at a glance and as BadCode only after you learn it.

## Findings

- **[verified]** Biohazard symbol (Charles Baldwin, Dow Chemical, 1966). Baldwin listed six criteria: striking, unique and unambiguous, quickly recognisable and easily recalled, easily stencilled, symmetrical so it looks the same from any approach, and acceptable across ethnic groups. His stated aim was 'memorable but meaningless, so we could educate people as to what it means.' Dow's artists drew 40+ candidates. The winner scored highest in national testing for uniqueness and memorability, and was presented in Science in 1967. The '24 symbols shown to 300 people in 25 US cities' figure comes from secondary write-ups (99% Invisible and similar), not the Wikipedia page I fetched. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biohazard_symbol>
- **[verified]** The radiation trefoil was doodled at UC Berkeley's Radiation Laboratory in 1946, originally magenta on blue. The magenta (Martin Senour Roman Violet) was chosen because it was expensive and unlikely to appear on other signs. The blue then clashed with blue information signs and weathered badly, so the US went magenta on yellow and the international version is black on yellow. Geometry is fixed: centre circle radius R, inner blade 1.5R, outer blade 5R, 60 degrees between blades. Lesson: a warning colour was chosen for rarity, then lost to the wider sign system, and the shape survived because it was simple and constructible from a compass. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_warning_symbol>
- **[verified]** ISO 21482 (revealed 15 Feb 2007): 50 candidate symbols were first tested on pupils at the Vienna International School (80+ nationalities, including pre-literate children). Five went forward to Gallup-run testing in 2004 with 1,650 people in 11 countries. All five conveyed 'caution', but only the one containing a skull conveyed 'danger of death'. The winner is a red triangle holding trefoil-plus-waves, a skull and crossbones, and a figure running away from them. It supplements the trefoil, is for sealed Category 1-3 sources only, and is banned from transport containers, vehicles and doors. Lessons: an abstract mark says 'attention' but not 'death', and a tiny pictorial sentence (source, death, run) does the rest. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_21482>
- **[verified]** Skull and crossbones: a memento mori from the late Middle Ages, military insignia from the mid-18th century (Frederick the Great's Hussars, 1741), a standard poison label from about the 1850s-80s, and the GHS acute-toxicity pictogram today. It decayed as a warning: 99% Invisible says it now reads as 'treasure, blockbuster movies, or Halloween'. Mr Yuk was created at Pittsburgh Children's Hospital in 1971 after paediatrician Richard Moriarty judged the skull to mean 'pirates' to children. He tested angry, dead and sick faces on children, and the 'sick' green face ranked least appealing. Peer-reviewed studies in 1982 and 1984 then suggested the stickers may not deter toddlers and could attract them. Mr Yuk is trademarked. Lesson: a warning's meaning drifts with the audience, and one aimed wrongly becomes a lure. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Yuk>
- **[verified]** ISO 7010 fixes a shape-and-colour grammar that lets you read the category before the content: yellow triangle with black border for warning, red circle with slash for prohibition, blue circle for mandatory, green rectangle for safe condition, red square for fire. Each pictogram gets a registered code (W, P, M, E, F). It is designed to need 'as little as possible' words. Lesson: the frame does most of the work, and the pictogram slot is the only part that has to be learned. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_7010>
- **[verified]** Kinneir and Calvert (UK road signs, from the late 1950s) built 'a code of carefully chosen shapes and colours' plus the Transport typeface (based on Akzidenz Grotesk, first used on the Preston By-pass in 1958). Calvert's pictograms were drawn from life: the schoolchildren sign is a girl leading a boy, modelled on herself, and the cattle sign is a cow called Patience. Lesson: the most authoritative warning system in Britain carries a human, slightly tender picture inside a hard frame. That is the same 'nurturing under the authority' register BadCode wants. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Calvert>
- **[verified]** Sandia 1993 (Trauth, Hora, Guzowski; WIPP marker panel, published November 1993) recommended messages in levels: I Rudimentary ('something man-made is here'), II Cautionary (dangerous materials are buried below), III Basic (what, why, when, where, who), IV/V Complex (records, diagrams). The sample text says 'This place is not a place of honor... no highly esteemed deed is commemorated here... nothing valued is here. What is here was dangerous and repulsive to us.' Principles include: mark the site, be truthful, use redundancy, multiple channels (language, pictographs, diagrams), and low-value materials so nobody wants to salvage it. Lesson for a logo: design Level I first. It must work with zero language and read as 'someone put this here on purpose and it is bad news'. Everything else is an upgrade on top. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-term_nuclear_waste_warning_messages>
- **[secondary]** The Sandia teams proposed marker concepts that communicate by menace rather than by words: Landscape of Thorns, Spike Field, Menacing Earthworks (lightning-bolt-shaped berms visible from the air), Forbidding Blocks, Black Hole (black basalt or concrete slab), Rubble Landscape, Lighting Field, Vitrified Wasteland. The reasoning was a 'gestalt message': jagged, chaotic, uninviting forms read as 'stay out' across cultures. The panel also insisted the markers be purely functional and not honorific art. I did not verify which team proposed which concept, so treat any attribution to a single team as unchecked. Lesson: sharp, irregular, dark and unornamented reads as danger without training, and a beautiful monument attracts visitors. That echoes the house worry that monumental beauty can read as celebration. — <https://archive.org/details/long-term-warning>
- **[verified]** The wilder proposals treat the warning as a story, not a sign: Thomas Sebeok's 'atomic priesthood' (a body that keeps the knowledge alive through ritual and myth, like a religion), and Bastida and Fabbri's 'ray cats' (engineered cats that change colour near radiation, transmitted through songs and folklore). 99% Invisible adds Gregory Benford's point that almost nothing outside organised religion lasts 10,000 years. Lesson: the warning that survives is the one people keep retelling. BadCode's whole premise, a warning from the future carried by stories and songs, is this idea, and the logo should be the cheapest retellable object in it. — <https://99percentinvisible.org/article/beyond-biohazard-danger-symbols-cant-last-forever/>
- **[verified]** Factory Records FAC 1 (Peter Saville, 1978): the first poster, for the first night at the club (not the label). Saville took the 'Use Hearing Protection' sign from a workshop door at art school, combined it with Tschichold-style ranged-left typography, and used yellow, black and white. Because the club was called The Factory and was loud, the borrowed sign was literally true. He has said industrial signage was apt because Manchester was the first industrial city. Ben Kelly's Hacienda interior then used yellow-and-black hazard stripes on the structural pillars. When the site was later developed into flats, the developers reused the stripes in their marketing. Lessons: (a) a borrowed hazard sign works when the warning is true of the thing, (b) you only need one sign, not the whole stripe system, (c) hazard stripes are now a property-marketing and building-site cliche. — <https://www.timesensitive.fm/episode/peter-saville-on-capturing-nowness-through-design>
- **[secondary]** Other hazard-sign identities: Throbbing Gristle's lightning-flash logo (Industrial Records) is reported, per a search snippet quoting Genesis P-Orridge, to derive from 'Warning! High Tension Cables' pylon signs. I tried to confirm this on Wikipedia and could not. The Designers Republic built the WipEout and Warp look partly on mock-corporate marks, trademark symbols and regulation-style typography. Both are secondary claims only. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Designers_Republic>
- **[secondary]** UK legal position, low-confidence and NOT legal advice. A UK sign-supplier article says standard safety symbols such as the ISO 7010 set are 'typically considered public domain', with caveats for custom, bespoke or branded variants. A search on trade-mark grounds only returned generic points: UK marks can be refused for lacking distinctive character, or if contrary to public policy. From memory and unchecked: the ISO standard document itself is copyright, the Health and Safety (Safety Signs and Signals) Regulations 1996 govern real workplace signage, and imitating official road signs on the highway is regulated. Practical reading: pastiche is very likely fine, but a mark that IS a standard sign cannot be protected as ours, and it must not be used as a real safety sign. Anything we register has to be distinctive in its own treatment, not in the borrowed triangle. Mr Yuk is trademarked, so do not go near it. Worth a short IP-lawyer check before any registration. — <https://parrotsigns.co.uk/articles/are-safety-signs-copyrighted-understanding-the-legal-landscape>

## Precedents

- **Biohazard symbol (Charles Baldwin, Dow, 1966)** — A three-lobed, threefold-symmetric ring shape made of overlapping circles. Tested from dozens of candidates for uniqueness and recall, and intentionally arbitrary. **Lesson:** Aim for memorable but meaningless, then teach it by repetition. Keep it stencilable and symmetrical. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biohazard_symbol>
- **Radiation trefoil (Berkeley, 1946)** — A central disc with three 60-degree blades, magenta on blue at first and now black on yellow. **Lesson:** Constructible from a compass, colour chosen for rarity, and the colour later collided with other signs. The shape outlived the colour. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_warning_symbol>
- **ISO 21482 supplementary radiation symbol (2007)** — A red triangle with trefoil and waves, a skull and crossbones and a figure running away from them. **Lesson:** Abstract marks said 'caution' only. The skull was what said 'death' in tests across 11 countries. A tiny pictorial sentence beats an abstract sign. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_21482>
- **Mr Yuk (Pittsburgh, 1971)** — A fluorescent green face with a sour expression and tongue out, tested against angry and dead faces on children. **Lesson:** Meaning drifts, and a warning can become an attraction for the wrong audience. It is also trademarked, so keep well clear. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Yuk>
- **Kinneir and Calvert UK road-sign system (from 1957)** — Shapes-and-colours code (red-bordered warning triangles, blue and white boards) with Transport lettering and hand-drawn pictograms such as the child pair and the cow. **Lesson:** The most trusted warning system in Britain is authoritative and tender at once. A cool frame around a human picture. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Calvert>
- **Sandia/WIPP marker panel (1993)** — Not a logo but a family of menace-first structures (spikes, thorns, black slab, jagged earthworks) plus a tiered message text. **Lesson:** Design the zero-language, first-glance layer first, keep it plain and un-honorific, and add detail in tiers. — <https://archive.org/details/long-term-warning>
- **Factory Records FAC 1 poster (Peter Saville, 1978)** — A yellow, black and white poster built around a real 'Use Hearing Protection' sign copied from a workshop door, set in left-aligned modernist type. **Lesson:** One borrowed sign, used because it was literally true of the product (a loud club called The Factory), was enough to carry an identity. — <https://www.timesensitive.fm/episode/peter-saville-on-capturing-nowness-through-design>
- **The Haçienda hazard stripes (Ben Kelly, early 1980s)** — Yellow-and-black diagonal hazard striping painted on the club's structural pillars and carried into its promotional material. **Lesson:** A whole hazard-stripe system is strong but becomes a cliche and gets recuperated: developers reused it to sell flats on the same site. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Haçienda>
- **Throbbing Gristle / Industrial Records flash** — A lightning flash inside a circle, reported to derive from pylon 'high tension' warning signs. Not confirmed on the pages I fetched. **Lesson:** A single lifted hazard glyph can become a whole label's badge and a wearable, sticker-able emblem. Treat the derivation as unconfirmed. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throbbing_Gristle>
- **Skull and crossbones** — A skull above two crossed long bones. Used as memento mori, regimental badge, poison label, GHS toxicity pictogram and pirate flag. **Lesson:** Universal and instantly readable but so worn by pirates, Halloween and merch that it has lost its warning force and cannot be ownable. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_and_crossbones>

## Clichés to avoid

- Full-bleed yellow-and-black hazard tape stripes as the identity (Hacienda did it in 1982; it is now used by building-site branding, developers' flats and every 'industrial' techno label)
- Skull and crossbones or any skull (Halloween, pirates, metal merch; the warning meaning has decayed)
- Biohazard or radiation trefoil pastiche, including 'radioactive glow' green or neon (gamer, energy-drink and metal-band territory)
- The bare exclamation-mark triangle or the warning-sign emoji as a logo (reads as a browser error or a content-warning UI)
- Distressed 'CLASSIFIED / DANGER / RESTRICTED' rubber-stamp lettering (spy-thriller and Call of Duty)
- The running-man exit pictogram in green (fire exit)
- Glitched, chromatic-aberration or scanline treatment to say 'AI'
- A literal HAL/Terminator eye with lens rings and a glossy highlight (copies someone else's mark)

## Risks

- **(high)** Yellow-and-black warning colour fights the established near-black register — The house look is near-black frames and one thin cold light. A yellow triangle reads as 'caution wet floor' or building-site workwear, not superintelligence. Any warning-sign idea has to keep the ISO shape grammar but drop the yellow, for example a near-black plate with a hairline bright outline and a red dot, or black-on-white for print.
- **(high)** A triangle at avatar size looks like a platform UI element — From memory and unchecked: at 32 to 64px a triangle with a centred mark can read as a sensitive-content, error or play-button icon on YouTube, TikTok and Spotify, and a triangle-plus-dot can be read as an all-seeing-eye or Illuminati mark. It needs testing at 16px and against a black avatar circle crop before anything is committed.
- **(medium)** Threat can tip into fear-mongering for this audience — the-reader.md rules say never raise automation fear without naming the beneficiary, and the contempt is for the mistake, not the people. A pure hazard mark says 'danger' but not who is doing it. The Mr Yuk result is a reminder that a warning can attract the wrong people. Pair the mark with the copy line that names the decision-maker, and keep some warmth (the Calvert lesson).
- **(medium)** A warning that only means 'warning' has no brand — ISO 21482 testing found abstract marks say 'caution' and nothing more. If the mark is only the standard frame, it is generic. The distinctive part has to be our own pictogram, and it has to be taught through repetition (the Baldwin method), which costs time and sends the channel's first videos to build the association.
- **(medium)** Legal and standards confusion — A mark that is literally a standard safety sign may be unregistrable as a trade mark and could confuse if used near real hazards. Do not put it on anything that pretends to be an actual safety sign or road sign. Also the ISO 21482 rules forbid the supplementary symbol on doors and vehicles. Get a quick IP check before registering.
- **(low)** Monumental menace can read as celebration — Sandia insisted markers be un-honorific. If the mark is beautiful and heroic, it can glorify the power it warns about. Keep it plain, stencil-cheap and slightly unwelcoming rather than glossy.

## Logo ideas this agent proposed

### The Empty Slot

**Sketch in words:** Use the ISO warning grammar but put nothing standard in the pictogram slot. Draw an equilateral triangle with slightly rounded corners, point up, about 10% border thickness, in off-white on a near-black square. Inside, centred on the triangle's centroid (about 40% up from the base), one solid red dot with a diameter about 20% of the triangle's width. Nothing else, no exclamation mark. Marker version: triangle, dot, done. Set the wordmark in a plain grotesque (Transport-like) small below it, or leave it off on the avatar.

**Why it fits:** It uses Baldwin's 'memorable but meaningless' method and the ISO 7010 grammar (triangle means warning), so a stranger reads 'warning' instantly, and the red dot supplies the Terminator/HAL point of view without copying either. It is a warning that will not say what it is warning about, which is on-brand for a superintelligence that knows exactly what and is making you ask. It works one-colour in a stencil and at 16px.

**Weakness:** Triangle-plus-dot is close to platform UI icons, all-seeing-eye and Illuminati marks, and to dozens of 'warning' logos. It can be mistaken for a real hazard sign. It is only distinctive if the dot's treatment (a hair of cold light on one edge, the exact red, the proportions) is kept rigid, and even then it may not be legally protectable for the triangle alone.

### Level I / II / III (the Sandia ladder)

**Sketch in words:** One identity at three sizes, in the order Sandia used for messages. Level I is a single red dot on near-black (avatar, favicon, video ident frame 1). Level II puts a thin white outline square (a 'plate') around the dot, with the dot sitting low and centred so the plate looks like a monolith with one light on it (channel banner, sticker). Level III is the same plate with a small line of stencilled plain-English text under it, for example the sentence 'THIS PLACE IS NOT A PLACE OF HONOUR' adapted into BadCode's own warning line, plus the wordmark (record sleeve, end-card). The video ident is the three levels appearing in sequence over two seconds: dot, then plate, then plate with words.

**Why it fits:** It converts Sandia's tiered-message idea into a brand system: the mark works with zero language (Level I) and gains meaning as the viewer engages. It also matches the existing near-black, tiny-LED, single-blade register, so nothing new has to be invented in the world of the comics.

**Weakness:** A red dot on black is what a dead pixel, a recording indicator, a notification badge and every 'Terminator eye' already looks like, so Level I alone is not ownable. The idea is a system, not one draw-in-five-seconds mark, and the middle plate may just look like a black square with a dot.

### Us, Running

**Sketch in words:** A big solid red dot on the left, about a third of the frame height, and to its right a small white stick-figure mid-stride, about a quarter of the dot's height, running away from it toward the right edge. Proportions echo ISO 21482's running figure but the figure is generic and childlike, closer to Calvert's schoolchildren than to the exit-sign man. Both sit on near-black with no frame. Marker version: circle, then a running stick person. One colour red plus white.

**Why it fits:** It puts the whole story in one glyph: the machine that already knows how it ends, and a small human being told to run. That is the 'humans, please don't make this obvious mistake' line, and it has the nurturing undertone because the figure is small and running, not dead. It steals the ISO 21482 insight that a tiny pictorial sentence beats an abstract sign.

**Weakness:** It looks like the green fire-exit man, or a running-away-from-the-sun gag, and can read as 'the AI is chasing us', which leans toward fear rather than the decision-makers' fault. The pictogram is inherently narrative, so it is harder to shrink to a favicon than a lone dot, and running figures are common in games and film posters.

### Fork Warning (a hazard glyph made of a git graph)

**Sketch in words:** Invent a Baldwin-style symmetrical stencil mark from the story's own metaphor. A central solid red dot with three short spokes at 120 degrees (one straight up, two down-left and down-right), each ending in a small open ring. Every spoke is the same length, about 2.5 times the dot's radius, and the whole thing sits in a thin ring outline roughly 5 times the dot's radius. It is symmetrical, stencil-cuttable in one plate, and from far away looks like a hazard symbol; up close it is one commit forking into three branches. Off-white on black, the red dot the only colour.

**Why it fits:** It follows Baldwin's own criteria (striking, unique, symmetrical, stencil-friendly, meaningless until taught) and gives BadCode a glyph nobody else owns that is still built from the central metaphor. The fork also encodes the good branch and the bad branch. A sticker or a stencil on a wall would look like a hazard sign you have not seen before, which is a good place to be.

**Weakness:** Three spokes at 120 degrees are dangerously close to the biohazard and radiation trefoils and to the Mercedes star, so it may look like a knock-off hazard symbol. It is also legible as a git-graph only to coders, who are not the audience, so for most people it is a meaningless hazard glyph that has to be taught, and the audience is a working-class UK viewer with no code knowledge.

### The Black Plate

**Sketch in words:** A plain near-black rectangle, portrait, about 3:4, with a razor-thin light rim on the top and left edges only and a single small red dot placed in the lower third, offset one third from the left edge. No text. Wordmark, when needed, is a separate line of small caps below. It is the Sandia 'Black Hole' marker and the 2001 monolith at once: unornamented, unwelcoming, purposeful. Marker version: draw a black rectangle and put a red dot near the bottom.

**Why it fits:** Sandia's panel explicitly favoured the un-honorific, low-value, unadorned marker, and this is the opposite of a glossy monumental logo, so it answers the concern that scale-and-beauty reads as celebration. It also drops straight into every existing frame, since the world is already made of dark plates with one light.

**Weakness:** It is the most likely to fail as an avatar because a black rectangle with a dot loses its edge on a black profile circle and looks like a blank or broken image. It also risks reading as generic 'dark mode minimalism'. It needs a light border or coloured backing to exist at all, which weakens the purity.
