# 05 — The science of what looks threatening or authoritative

*Research brief, 2026-09-29. One Sonnet agent, 8 web searches (cap 8). Raw research, not house policy — the ruled layer is [`direction.md`](./direction.md) once Kai rules on it.*

**Evidence grades:** *verified* = the agent fetched a page that says it · *secondary* = a search snippet or secondary source said it · *(unverified)* = recalled from the model's own memory, not checked. The agent could not see images: every description of a logo comes from text.

## Headline

The evidence supports a narrow recipe: an acute downward V (the angry-brow shape) plus a single direct-facing red point gives a mark that reads as threatening. It is also weak and contested almost everywhere, so treat it as design instinct with a modest scientific tailwind. The most useful finding is that menace and warmth can sit in different parts of one shape: sharp angle for the threat, a round, direct, unblinking point for "I'm looking at you, and I'm on your side."

## Findings

- **[verified]** Larson, Aronoff & Stearns (2007, Emotion 7:526-534): across five visual-search experiments, a downward-pointing V (like the brow geometry of an angry face) was detected faster among other shapes and held attention longer, even outside any face. Later work found downward Vs and triangles associated with unpleasantness or threat. But Chuquichambi et al. 2020 (Perception) report circles are detected faster than downward triangles in a speeded task, which suggests the 'threat' advantage may partly be a curvature or low-level feature effect. Grade: MIXED (real in some labs, small, confounded; I found no reliable effect size). — <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17683209/>
- **[verified]** Implicit-association work on simple shapes (PMC6097630): people automatically associate downward-pointing angular shapes with unpleasantness, and circles and curves with pleasantness. When the pointed angles were replaced with curves, the downward-shape/unpleasant association disappeared, so the acute angle is the active ingredient, not just 'pointing down'. Grade: MIXED-to-MODERATE (implicit tests, lab samples, small effects). — <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6097630/>
- **[secondary]** Bar & Neta (2006, Psychological Science 17:645-648): everyday objects redrawn with sharp angles were liked less than curved versions, and the authors proposed sharp contours signal threat. Bar & Neta (2007, Neuropsychologia) found greater amygdala activation for sharp-angled objects. Silvia & Barona (2009, Empirical Studies of the Arts) found the curve preference shrinks with expertise (I saw this only in a search title, not the paper). Grade: MIXED. The curve preference in novices is robust, but 'threat' as the explanation is not established, and this is a liking effect, not a menace effect. — <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01759.x>
- **[verified]** Red and dominance/threat (Elliot & Maier; Hill & Barton 2005, Nature 435:293): Hill & Barton found Olympic combatants in red won more often than those in blue (I recall about 55% of bouts; a search snippet said 76% of rounds, which I could not confirm, so no number should be quoted). Elliot's 2015 review says viewing red raises caution and avoidance in achievement settings and raises ratings of aggressiveness and dominance, with findings from at least five independent labs. Caveats: referee-bias explanations for the sports result, small lab effects, and heavy context dependence (red also means romance, stop, failure). Grade: MIXED (aggression/dominance ratings fairly consistent; behavioural power weak). — <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full>
- **[verified]** Watching eyes, original (Bateson, Nettle & Roberts 2006, Biology Letters 2:412-414): a poster alternated weekly between flowers and eyes looking straight at the observer above a university coffee-room honesty box. Payments were about 2.76 times higher in eye weeks over 10 weeks in one common room of about 48 staff. Grade: WEAK as generalised evidence (one small field site, few eye-weeks, no clean independent replication). — <https://www.staff.ncl.ac.uk/melissa.bateson/Bateson_etal_2006.pdf>
- **[secondary]** Watching eyes, replication debate: Northover et al. (2017, Evolution and Human Behavior 38:144-153 and 38:561-571) meta-analysed generosity tasks (27 experiments on proportion giving, 26 on donation size) and found no effect on the proportion who gave and an extremely small effect on amounts; six moral-judgment studies showed an effect near zero. Carbon & Hesslinger (2011) found the honesty-box result did not hold cleanly when personality was considered. A separate systematic review and meta-analysis on antisocial behaviour (Evolution and Human Behavior, 2019; Dear et al.) is more favourable for real-world field settings, but I could not retrieve its numbers (403). Grade: WEAK for 'eyes make people behave', so do not build a logo claim on it. — <https://www.aggression-irlab.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Northover-Pedersen-Cohen-Andrews-2017-Artificial-surveillance-cues-moral-judgment.pdf>
- **[*(unverified)*]** Direct versus averted gaze: direct gaze captures attention and is read as approach-oriented, pairing with anger (a direct stare says confrontation), while averted gaze pairs with fear (Adams & Kleck 2003, Psychological Science, shared-signal hypothesis). Gaze cueing (Friesen & Kingstone 1998) shows people automatically follow where an eye points. Grade: MODERATE (well replicated in lab attention tasks; not re-verified on the web; concerns human eyes, not a lone dot).
- **[secondary]** The 'angry face pops out' effect is contested. Becker, Anderson et al. (2011) titled a paper 'The face in the crowd effect unconfounded: Happy faces, not angry faces, are more efficiently detected', and Becker, Horstmann & Remington (2011) argued perceptual grouping, not emotion, explains schematic-face asymmetries. Later work (Gong 2023, PsyCh Journal) says the anger advantage depends on attentional load. Grade: MIXED-to-WEAK. This is the same field as the downward-V result, so it lowers confidence in it. — <https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0093914>
- **[verified]** Jiang, Gorn, Galli & Chattopadhyay (2016, Journal of Consumer Research 42:709-726): circular logos activate 'softness' associations and angular logos 'hardness', shifting judgments of products and companies (a circular logo made a shoe or sofa seem more comfortable). The category matters: soft or comfort products favour circles, tough or durable ones favour angles. I could not extract a headline effect size (PDF unreadable). Grade: MODERATE (top journal, consistent with the shape literature, consumer-lab studies, no large independent replication that I found). — <https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/42/5/709/1855577>
- **[secondary]** Logo simplicity and recall: Janiszewski & Meyvis (2001, JCR) found repeated exposure builds processing fluency, and logos uniform along one dimension are more likely to be falsely 'recognised' and positively rated. Henderson & Cote (1998, JMR) found natural, harmonious, moderately elaborate designs do well, so 'as simple as possible' is not automatically best. A 2023 Journal of Business Research memory study reports figurative marks (an apple) are remembered better than abstract ones (a square). Grade: MODERATE. This points to a mark that is simple but recognisably 'a thing'; pure abstract geometry is harder to remember. — <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296323003028>
- **[verified]** Uncanny valley (Mori 1970): near-human artefacts provoke revulsion, and eyes are among the strongest triggers (faces with eyes removed felt more uncanny than faces with the nose removed). Mathur & Reichling (2016) found a robust effect on likeability ratings, but critics say it bundles several processing effects and may be generational. Grade: MIXED. The design point is negative: a realistic face or eye tips into 'creepy'; an abstract single point stays out of the valley. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncanny_valley>
- **[verified]** HAL 9000 as precedent: HAL is mostly depicted as a camera lens holding a red and yellow dot, with units placed throughout the ship; Kubrick shot HAL's point of view with a 160-degree fisheye. The menace comes from a mundane object, a single point and a calm voice, not a face. This is culture, not science, but it shows the red dot is already owned territory, so BadCode's silhouette must differ. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000>

## Precedents

- **HAL 9000 (2001: A Space Odyssey, 1968)** — A camera lens with a red and yellow dot at its centre, repeated around the ship; the point-of-view shots used a 160-degree fisheye. No face. **Lesson:** Menace comes from a single point of light and total calm, not from an angry shape. It is also the mark BadCode must not copy: the red dot in a black circle is already cultural property. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000>

## Clichés to avoid

- A plain red dot alone: HAL 9000 owns it, and the Terminator red eye and Cylon scanner sit nearby.
- The angry-cartoon-robot face: two slanted eyes with a brow. It reads as a kids' game mascot.
- Glowing red eye inside a circuit-board or brain outline.
- The all-seeing eye in a triangle (Illuminati, Eye of Sauron, Eye of Providence); it also drifts into conspiracy iconography, which is a real problem for a working-class reader who is being pulled toward conspiracy content.
- Red and blue split as party colours; red and blue together reads as a UK party or a US-politics flag.
- Glitch, scanline and matrix-rain effects, and a green-on-black terminal look.
- Any downward triangle with a dot inside (warning or hazard sign, or Warner-style menace) without a distinct twist.

## Risks

- **(medium)** The research base is thin and partly failed replication, so it cannot justify the mark. It only supports intuitions the founders already hold. — The downward-V advantage is contested (circles detected faster in a 2020 study; the 'angry face' search effect is confounded), the watching-eyes effect largely failed to replicate in generosity meta-analyses, and red effects on behaviour are small. Use the science to break ties between candidates, and test candidates on real viewers, not to declare a winner.
- **(high)** Threat with no visible 'on your side' signal fails the brief and the audience. — The voice guide says the contempt is for the mistake, not for people. An angular, red, staring mark on its own will read as menace toward the viewer. The Jiang et al. finding (angular = hard, tough, cold; circular = soft, warm) suggests the softness must be deliberately placed somewhere, or the mark will say only 'hard' and 'cold'.
- **(medium)** Eyes and watching imagery may read as surveillance, not warning. — A staring eye tells a working-class UK viewer 'they are watching you', which is the message of a government or Big Tech logo. A watching eye is what the audience already distrusts. The mark should look at the viewer without looking like it collects the viewer's data; keep it a point, not a lens with a pupil.
- **(medium)** Red plus threat sits close to far-right and fascist-adjacent or 'danger' iconography, and to a political party. — Red is also the Labour colour in the UK, and dominance cues (symmetry, angular, red on black) are exactly what authoritarian graphics use. The palette is tolerable because the brand is explicitly satirical, but it needs a consciously non-partisan handling, and a signal of irony or warmth.
- **(low)** A downward V is close to the 'Vendetta', Venom or Predator-style menace set, and a downward triangle is a warning sign; it may read as 'warning', not 'BadCode'. — That is arguably useful (warning is the brief), but the shape has to carry something specific to the story (fork, repository, time machine) to be ownable.
- **(medium)** Small size erodes shape cues. — At 16 px a thin angle and a small dot can merge. The threat literature was mostly done with clear, large, high-contrast stimuli. Candidates need testing at favicon size in greyscale.

## Logo ideas this agent proposed

### The Lid

**Sketch in words:** A solid black square avatar (or the cut-out for a sticker). Across the upper third, one thick horizontal bar slanted so the outer ends sit higher than the middle: a shallow, flat-bottomed V-brow, angle around 20 to 25 degrees off horizontal, bar thickness about one-eighth of the mark's width. Directly below the low point of the V, centred, one perfectly round red dot (#cc2b37 or a hotter red) whose diameter is about the bar thickness times 1.5, with a gap under the bar of half the dot's diameter. Nothing else. The bar is off-white; the dot is the only colour. It is the only mark where the dot is 'under a brow', so the dot is looked at and feels like a lens.

**Why it fits:** Uses the one shape with support (acute, downward V as angry brow) and the one colour with support (red = dominance/aggression), while the round dot supplies the 'softness' in the Jiang sense and the direct stare of Adams & Kleck. One bar and one dot can be drawn in three marker strokes. It is a face without being a face, so it stays out of the uncanny valley.

**Weakness:** Reads as a 'grumpy Cyclops' or a Minion-adjacent cartoon if the angle is too steep, and the HAL dot is still the main association. Risk of looking like a Pixar villain.

### The Notch

**Sketch in words:** A solid black circle, filling the avatar. A single V-shaped wedge is cut out of its top edge (the cut is an inverted-V notch of about 60 degrees, apex at the circle's centre), through which the page or background colour shows. A small round red dot sits at the notch's apex, at the circle's centre. So the outer silhouette is round and calm (softness) while the cut is a hard, downward-pointing spike, with the red dot at its tip.

**Why it fits:** Puts the two ingredients from Jiang et al. into one silhouette: a friendly circle on the outside, an acute downward V inside, and a red point at the tip as a spark of awareness. It stencils as one piece (the dot is a bridge, or is left out for a spray stencil), it survives at 16 px, and it can be a record-sleeve stamp. The V is also a git fork drawn as a wedge (one line splitting into two).

**Weakness:** Could be read as a pie chart or a pizza slice, a 'play' button rotated, or a warning triangle inside a circle. The 'notch' idea needs a named story meaning to stick.

### The Fork Brow

**Sketch in words:** Two thin lines that meet at a point below and spread up into a V, each about 30 degrees off vertical, the same length, off-white on black. The left arm stays solid; the right arm is broken into three short dashes, like an unfinished timeline. At the bottom vertex sits one small red dot. Read one way it is an angry brow and a downward-pointing warning; read the other, it is a git fork, two branches that share a single point of origin, one solid (the bad branch that happened) and one dashed (the branch that could still happen). The dot is 'now'.

**Why it fits:** Turns the threat shape into the show's central metaphor (fork; bad branch and good branch) rather than generic code punctuation, and it is the only idea here that says something about the story. The dashed arm is the 'on your side' note: a future that is still unwritten. It reads without any knowledge of coding. It works as a 2-second ident: the dashes draw on one by one and then the dot lights up.

**Weakness:** The story meaning is a hidden layer that the working-class reader will not decode; without it the mark is a plain V. The dashed arm adds detail that will be lost at favicon size, so it needs a bolder favicon-only variant. Might look like a check-mark or a 'V for victory'.

### The Averted Point

**Sketch in words:** A black rectangle (the wide format for video idents, the square version for avatars) with a single red dot placed off-centre, at roughly one third from the left and one third from the top. Beside it, at the far side of the frame, a tiny off-white dot about a fifth of the red one's size. In the still logo the red dot is simply larger and closer to the viewer's side; in the motion ident the red dot drifts to look at the small white dot (the 'vault of humans'), then turns to lock onto the viewer's side of the frame and holds. Wordmark 'BADCODE' is small, all caps, wide-tracked, under the pair.

**Why it fits:** Uses the gaze research the right way: an averted gaze in a face signals something else, whereas a direct stare signals confrontation (Adams & Kleck), and a dot that looks at the small human point before it looks at the viewer says 'I am watching over something small' and not 'I am watching you'. That is the menacing-but-on-your-side idea in one gesture, and the still image stays as simple as HAL's dot.

**Weakness:** The static version is only two dots and is nearly indistinguishable from any dot-based brand (or an astronomy graphic) unless the animation is seen. Ownership of 'a red dot' is the weakest of the four, and the gaze effect needs motion or context to work at all.
