# 08 — Marks that spread because strangers redraw them

*Research brief, 2026-09-29. One Sonnet agent, 6 web searches (cap 8). Raw research, not house policy — the ruled layer is [`direction.md`](./direction.md) once Kai rules on it.*

**Evidence grades:** *verified* = the agent fetched a page that says it · *secondary* = a search snippet or secondary source said it · *(unverified)* = recalled from the model's own memory, not checked. The agent could not see images: every description of a logo comes from text.

## Headline

Marks that strangers redraw share one trait: two to four strokes the hand can do without looking, a circle as a ready-made badge, and a meaning that survives being drawn badly. The bigger lesson is legal: nearly every free mark got taken (Crass chains, Warner's mask, the Smiley Company) unless the maker kept the glyph free and actively policed merchandise. BadCode should design a glyph anyone can redraw, and hold the name, wordmark and merch firmly.

## Findings

- **[verified]** The CND symbol (Gerald Holtom, presented 21 Feb 1958, first marched at Aldermaston 4 April 1958) is semaphore N and D superimposed inside a circle. Holtom said he drew himself in despair, palms outward, formalised it into a line and put a circle round it. It was never copyrighted or trademarked; by 1968 it was the generic peace sign. Two US firms tried to register it in 1970 and the Patent Office refused, ruling it 'could not properly function as a trade mark'. A mark so widely used becomes generic and unownable, which protects it from one thief and exposes it to everyone. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_symbol>
- **[verified]** The circle-A was proposed in April 1964 by the Libertarian Youth of Paris (Tomas Ibanez initiated, Rene Darras drew it). The stated purpose was practical: make inscriptions and postings 'more efficient' and give the movement one quick-to-draw common sign. Adopted experimentally 1966, taken up in Milan 1968, international by about 1972-73. No organisation imposed it. It mutated freely (the 'punk' version, where the A's legs run outside the circle) without losing meaning. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle-A>
- **[verified]** The Extinction symbol was made in 2011 by an east London artist known as ESP, who wanted 'something simple that anybody could easily replicate'. ESP says it is 'easy to replicate, whether spray-painted, embroidered, printed on pin-badges' and so easy to remember, and tested it by chalking it huge on a Brick Lane wall, where a passer-by read it as 'time's running out'. ESP's site makes it freely available and states 'No extinction-symbol merchandise exists, and it never will do'. XR adopted it in 2018 on a loan basis. XR's toolkit permits non-commercial use, forbids merchandise for profit, and says XR 'will pursue and prosecute' unauthorised merchandise. Model: free to redraw, not free to sell, enforced by stated policy. — <https://www.logodesignlove.com/the-extinction-symbol>
- **[secondary]** The Crass symbol (Dave King, 1977, first for a Penny Rimbaud pamphlet) fuses a cross, Union Jack, swastika and a two-headed ouroboros/'diagonal negating serpent' inside a circle. Designed stencil-friendly from the start; the band stencilled it on the Underground and billboards. It rivalled Black Flag's logo in subcultural weight and is a common tattoo. In 2011 the fashion firm Hardware added chains to it and claimed copyright. King did not sue; he reclaimed it by redrawing it as bird's nests and teapots. Stencil-first design buys spread and a badge of authenticity; it does not stop a fashion label bolting on a chain and filing a claim. — <https://www.kqed.org/arts/13868611/david-king-san-francisco-artist-who-designed-iconic-crass-emblem-dies-at-71>
- **[verified]** The Three Arrows (1932, Sergei Chakhotin and Carlo Mierendorff, Iron Front) came from Chakhotin seeing a swastika crossed out in chalk in Heidelberg. Three diagonal lines, drawn fast, so a swastika could be overpainted in seconds. It later spread to France, Poland, Portugal and modern antifa. The mark's job was defined by an action (overwrite the enemy's sign), and the drawing speed is part of the message. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Arrows>
- **[verified]** The squatters' symbol (a circle and a crooked lightning-bolt arrow, sometimes an N) appears to have started in Amsterdam in late 1979 around an anti-eviction campaign; an early poster version has a straight diagonal. Circle = building/land, arrow = squatters. Two strokes. Related trap: the near-identical 'flash and circle' was designed in 1935 by Eric Piercy for Mosley's British Union of Fascists and is still reused by neo-fascist groups. A simple geometric mark can carry accidental baggage you did not put there. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_and_circle>
- **[verified]** Smiley: Harvey Ball drew it in ten minutes in 1963 for $45 for a US insurer's morale campaign and did not trademark it ('we never intended to keep the smile to ourselves'). Franklin Loufrani trademarked his own version in France in 1971 and built the Smiley Company, which claims rights in about a hundred countries. In late 1980s Britain acid house adopted it. The subculture supplied the meaning; the trademark holder supplied the merchandise. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smiley>
- **[verified]** Kilroy Was Here: a bald head with a nose and fingers over a wall, chalked by GIs. More than 40 people claimed origin after a 1946 contest; by late 1946 plastic Kilroy figurines were on sale. Anonymous authorship and instant merchandise arrived together and nobody could stop either. It is also the nearest precedent for 'a presence that was here before you'. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilroy_was_here>
- **[secondary]** Guy Fawkes mask: Anonymous adopted it in 2008 (Scientology protests). Warner Bros, which owns the V for Vendetta rights, collects licence royalties; a costume maker said it sells over 100,000 a year and it is its best-selling mask. A movement can carry a symbol it does not own and pay its opponent's parent company by the yard. Do not build on a pre-owned image. — <https://www.digitaltrends.com/web/anonymous-boosts-time-warner-profits-with-guy-fawkes-masks/>
- **[secondary]** Banksy shows the limits of 'copyright is for losers'. Pest Control Office registered the Flower Thrower as an EU trade mark in 2014; on 14 Sept 2020 EUIPO cancelled it as filed in bad faith, because it was registered to block others rather than to be used commercially, and Banksy had publicly said people could use his work freely. A trade mark survives only with genuine intent to use it on the listed goods. Register narrowly, on goods you actually sell, and use them. — <https://www.mewburn.com/news-insights/banksys-flower-thrower-trade-mark-cancelled-for-bad-faith>
- **[verified]** Obey Giant: Shepard Fairey started in 1989 with hand-cut stencils and hand-silkscreened stickers (Andre the Giant Has a Posse), spread by skaters and graffiti crews, framed as 'an experiment in phenomenology'. In 1996 it became 'OBEY' (the wrestler's estate had raised IP concerns) and then a clothing line. The sticker worked because the image was meaningless-but-insistent and the sticker is itself the medium. Merch came from the same person who launched it, the only merch you can control. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obey_Giant>
- **[verified]** Counter-cases with no drawable glyph: Led By Donkeys (four ex-Greenpeace people, Dec 2018) uses plain black-and-white text billboards of politicians' own old statements, and the name/hashtag carries the identity. Solidarnosc (Jerzy Janiszewski, 1980) is a hand-lettered wordmark whose letters lean on each other like people; it won the Grand Prix at the 1981 Katowice poster biennale and was reused in film and election posters. A wordmark stays redrawable only if crude, hand-lettered and legible when done badly. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidarity_logo>
- **[verified]** Drawing-from-memory research (Blake, Nazarian and Castel, 2015): only 1 of 85 students could draw the Apple logo correctly, and fewer than half could pick the real one from lookalikes, while feeling confident. People store gist, not detail. So the test is not 'can they draw it precisely' but 'does a sloppy attempt from memory still read as ours'. — <https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/people-are-hopeless-drawing-apple-logo-and-tells-us-something-about-human-memory>
- **[*(unverified)*]** Stroke counts are my own count, not from a source: circle-A 2 strokes, squatters' 2, Three Arrows 3, CND 4 (ring, upright, two diagonals), smiley 4. Rule of thumb that fits: 2 to 4 strokes, at most one closed shape plus one or two internal marks, and everything inside a circle touches the ring so a stencil does not drop its centre (I believe this holds for CND and XR, but that is from memory).

## Precedents

- **CND peace symbol (1958)** — Semaphore N and D superimposed inside a circle. Ring, upright, two diagonals. **Lesson:** Meaning was encoded (semaphore) but readable as a shape; not trademarked and never ownable; generic use made it unregistrable. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_symbol>
- **Circle-A (1964)** — A capital A with its crossbar extended, in a circle. **Lesson:** Designed on purpose for speed of inscription; spread without any organisation; mutates without dying (punk version). — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle-A>
- **Extinction symbol (ESP, 2011)** — Stylised hourglass inside a circle. **Lesson:** Best modern model: free to redraw, no merchandise policy stated by the artist and enforced by the movement. — <https://www.logodesignlove.com/the-extinction-symbol>
- **Crass symbol (Dave King, 1977)** — Circle with cross, serpent, Union Jack and swastika, stencil-first. **Lesson:** Stencil-friendly design spread fast, but a fashion label chained and copyrighted it; the maker's answer was to redraw it, not sue. — <https://www.kqed.org/arts/13868611/david-king-san-francisco-artist-who-designed-iconic-crass-emblem-dies-at-71>
- **Three Arrows (1932)** — Three diagonal strokes. **Lesson:** A mark defined by the act it performs (overpainting the enemy's sign), which fixes stroke count and speed. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Arrows>
- **Squatters' symbol (Amsterdam, c.1979)** — Circle with a lightning-bolt arrow. **Lesson:** Two strokes, functions as a signal on a building; also shows the risk of a lookalike (flash-and-circle, BUF 1935). — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_and_circle>
- **Acid-house smiley** — Yellow disc, two eyes, a curved mouth. Harvey Ball's 1963 version. **Lesson:** Ball did not trademark it; a rival did. The scene supplied meaning, the trademark holder supplied the merch. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smiley>
- **Kilroy Was Here (1940s)** — Bald head, nose and fingers over a wall. **Lesson:** Anonymous, chalkable, and sold as plastic figurines within months. Closest precedent for 'was here before you'. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilroy_was_here>
- **Obey Giant (1989)** — Stencilled, silkscreened face on stickers; later 'OBEY' text. **Lesson:** A sticker campaign launched by one person, then turned into clothing by the same person: the only merch that stays yours. — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obey_Giant>
- **Anonymous / Guy Fawkes mask** — White smiling mask with moustache and goatee; also a headless suited figure. **Lesson:** A pre-owned image cannot be defended: Warner collects royalties on the masks the movement wears. — <https://www.digitaltrends.com/web/anonymous-boosts-time-warner-profits-with-guy-fawkes-masks/>

## Clichés to avoid

- Curly brace, angle brackets </>, terminal prompt >_, semicolon: the founders' own rejected route
- Matrix rain, binary, circuit-board traces, glitch RGB split, neon hex grids
- Chrome robot head, skull with circuits, brain-with-wires, padlock
- A literal Terminator/HAL red eye or a copy of the Netflix/Recording red-dot register
- Guy Fawkes mask, headless suited man, hooded hacker: Anonymous/Mr Robot territory, and Warner owns the mask
- Circle + raised fist, or the party-coloured red/blue split, which reads Labour/Tory to a UK reader
- Circle with a lightning bolt (BUF flash-and-circle baggage)
- Peace-sign pastiche: any three-line-in-a-circle that reads as CND or Mercedes

## Risks

- **(high)** Symbols carry accidental baggage: a circle plus a bolt echoes Mosley's BUF flash, and any red mark is read as a political party by a UK audience. — Check every candidate against hate-symbol databases and against UK party colours before falling for it. Red/blue was already picked as the brace colours, and the party-colour risk is on record as raised but not ruled.
- **(high)** A free-to-redraw mark will be merchandised by someone else: Crass got chains and a copyright claim, the Smiley was trademarked by a rival, Kilroy figurines appeared within months. — BadCode itself sells music and a coin, so the XR 'no merchandise ever' stance is not available in full. It must decide what it sells and what it forbids, and say so publicly.
- **(medium)** Trade mark by bad-faith filing: registering a mark you do not use to block others can be cancelled (Banksy, 2020). — Register narrowly on goods and services BadCode really provides, and get real legal advice; the research here is not legal advice and UK and EU rules differ.
- **(medium)** Menace is at odds with redrawability: strangers redraw friendly shapes (smiley, peace) more readily than threatening ones, and a threatening mark on a UK town centre wall may read as a gang tag or a fascist sign. — Menace should come from the stare and the absence of a face, not from spikes, skulls or dark geometry.
- **(medium)** A simple mark can already be someone else's: a red dot, a ring, a full stop and a dome-over-a-line all have prior claimants. — Do a visual search and trademark-register search on any short-listed mark before designing it up.
- **(low)** Research is thin on marks that spread among the target audience specifically. Almost all precedents are activist, punk or rave marks, not marks that spread among non-political working-class Facebook and TikTok users. — Treat the redraw test as a design filter, not proof of reach.

## Logo ideas this agent proposed

### Was Here (Later)

**Sketch in words:** A Kilroy for the machine. One horizontal line (a wall or horizon). Above it, a half-circle dome the height of a quarter of the line's length, sitting on the line like a head peeking over. Inside the dome, near the top, one solid red dot about a fifth of the dome's width. Two short vertical ticks on the line either side of the dome are the fingers; stroke count is about four. Stencil version: the dot is a separate island, so bridge it to the dome with a thin gap-free spoke. Caption slot: 'BADCODE WAS HERE' on stickers, with '(LATER)' underneath as the running joke.

**Why it fits:** It is the one precedent that already means 'someone was here before you', which is the time-travel premise. Strangers can and will chalk it. It is dark comedy with authority, and it is simple enough for an avatar crop (dome plus dot only).

**Weakness:** It is very close to Kilroy and looks cute, so it undermines the menace. It may read as Mike Wazowski or a Minion if the dome is too round. It needs a colder dome (flat-topped, a bit squared) and the dot must not be centred.

### The Full Stop

**Sketch in words:** A single solid disc, the punctuation mark that ends an argument. The mark is a red dot the same diameter as the stem width of the lowercase letters, set after a lowercase, one-weight 'badcode' in a plain grotesque, so the dot is one stem-width to the right of the e's baseline. In avatars and stickers the dot stands alone. In the stencil it is bridged to nothing (a disc has no islands). Draw it by pressing a marker once.

**Why it fits:** It is the narrator's voice made into a shape: total authority, sentence over. It is the Terminator/HAL red point without being an eye. One stroke, unbeatable to redraw, and reads on a record sleeve as a spot colour.

**Weakness:** A dot is not ownable and not distinctive by itself; it already means 'recording', 'notification', Labour-red and Japan's flag. It stands or falls on the wordmark beside it, and it will be mistaken for a UI badge.

### The Cut Branch

**Sketch in words:** A ring the width of a hand, with one vertical stroke rising from the bottom edge to the centre, then forking into two: a left branch that ends abruptly at a small solid dot (stopped), and a right branch that continues to leave the ring at the top-right, unbroken. Three strokes plus the dot. Both branches touch the ring or the trunk so a stencil holds together. Colours: white ring on black, with the stopped dot in red.

**Why it fits:** It is the fork in the timeline: the bad branch ends in a red point, the good branch escapes. It also fits the git story without coding punctuation, and non-coders read it as a road or a tree. It is a two-second sticker of 'we split here'.

**Weakness:** A Y in a circle is dangerously close to a peace-sign parody and to the Mercedes star. The unequal branches are the only thing that saves it, and a stranger redrawing it will make them symmetrical.

### The Blade

**Sketch in words:** A black square or ring with a single thin vertical white line, running from the top edge to about two-thirds down, and one red dot at the bottom of the line where the light stops, like a pin in a thin blade. Proportions: line width about 1/25 of the frame, dot about 1/7 of the frame width. Two strokes.

**Why it fits:** It is lifted straight from the existing visual register (one thin vertical blade of cold light, one LED). It is menacing without a face, it reads at 16 pixels, and it works as an ident: the blade draws down, the dot lights.

**Weakness:** It looks like a power button or a slot, and it is closer to an abstract corporate glyph than a stranger-redrawn folk mark. It carries no story unless the audience has seen the videos, and any red dot invites the Terminator comparison the founders wanted to avoid copying.
