# Shape 09: Borrowed craft, the charity appeal grammar played dead straight

I took the grammar of the real UK charity appeal and the rules of the best parody sketches, and followed them like a checklist. The appeal grammar: (1) a first name, said softly, before any fact (the ASPCA "Angel" advert and the Comic Relief and Children in Need appeal films all introduce one individual, not a statistic); (2) one small, concrete, domestic detail of suffering instead of a big abstract one; (3) a price shrunk to something trivial, "for just £X a month"; (4) the ask, then a sign-off line over a logo. The parody rules: play it completely straight with no wink (Chris Morris's Brass Eye charity-appeal spoofs and the SNL take on the sad-animal advert both work by obeying the format to the letter), and let the format itself land the punchline. Applied here: the format does the recognition in two seconds (piano, black and white, "This is..."), the hardship detail is the absurd part, the median-pay card is the format breaking, and the button is the charity sign-off line turned back on the viewer's own situation with no moral stated. Invented name throughout: Crispin (Jack, swap freely: Giles, Montague, Rupert). Nothing about Crispin is a claim about a real person.

## Dialogue 1: The appeal (the whole short)

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SCREEN | [black and white, slow motion, one low piano note, a grey hallway, a man in a good coat standing very still] | 0 |
| NARRATOR | [soft, slow, barely above a whisper] This is Crispin. | 1.5 |
| NARRATOR | Crispin is a Chancellor. [beat] Crispin only earns a hundred thousand pounds a year. | 4.0 |
| NARRATOR | [slow-mo: he opens a wine fridge, it is full, he stares into it] Every evening, he has to look at just one wine fridge. | 3.5 |
| NARRATOR | For just three pounds a month, you could give Crispin a second. | 3.0 |
| SCREEN | [freeze frame, piano stops dead, white text on black, held in silence] Median UK full-time pay: £39,039 a year (ONS, April 2025) | 2.5 |
| NARRATOR | [same soft voice, no change of tone] Sponsor a Chancellor. [beat] Because somebody out there is struggling. | 3.0 |
| **Total** | | **17.5** |

**Buttons (pick one to replace the last line, all about 3s):**

- **A, the sign-off, as written above.** "Because somebody out there is struggling." The charity tagline pointed at the card the viewer has just read. It never says who. Cleanest, least lecture.
- **B, the future voice.** "Where I'm from, we ran this advert for thirty years. Nobody noticed which way round it was." The BadCode narrator breaks the parody voice for one line. Adds our authority, risks a stated moral.
- **C, the nurturing flip.** [still soft] "Crispin will be fine. He always is. Is anyone asking about you?" Care register, but it leans on "you", watch the reader rules.
- **D, the dead-straight ask.** "Lines are open until the Budget. Crispin thanks you. He has never had to thank anyone before." Pure format, no turn, the card carries everything.
- **E, the fine print, SCREEN only (voice ends on the card).** [tiny white text, bottom of the freeze] "Crispin is not a real person. He is not hard to find." Silent button, saves 3 seconds, total voice falls to about 14.5s.

**Why it works.** Every beat is a beat of a real appeal, so the viewer says "oh, it's one of those" inside two seconds, and then the format's own sad logic does the joke: a single named individual, one small domestic detail (one wine fridge, the "just one" is the cruelty), a price shrunk to £3. Nothing is winked at. The narrator plays it with total sincerity, which is the Brass Eye rule: the audience supplies the contempt, the voice never does. The £39,039 card cuts the music and the voice, which is the same trick real appeals use when they go to the silent statistic, only here the statistic indicts the subject of the appeal, not the viewer. The button is the charity's own tagline, so no moral is stated and nobody is told they were fooled. All the contempt sits on Crispin's comfort and the decision-maker's pay, none on a working person.

**Cuts from current.** "Jeremy" goes (it points at a real chancellor). "You could help him afford" becomes "you could give Crispin": shorter, and "give" is the charity verb. "Second wine fridge" is kept but split into a setup (one fridge, every evening) and the ask (a second), which is how appeals build the need before the price. The card is kept verbatim, with the month added because the ONS figure is April 2025, and it now sits in silence before the button instead of being the last thing on screen.

**Risk.** (1) "Only earns a hundred thousand pounds" is the fictional figure for a fictional man: fine, but it must not read as a claim about the real Chancellor's pay. Do not state any real chancellor's salary anywhere ([CHECK] if Jack wants one). (2) The real "£100k doesn't go far" quote is not used anywhere in these lines, so no attribution risk; if Jack wants it as SCREEN text, it needs a source check first. (3) A £3 fridge is not real arithmetic: that is the joke, but some viewers may read it as a sloppy fact, keep the narrator's tone so flat it is obviously the format talking. (4) At 17.5s there is no spare room, any added line must replace one. (5) Buttons B and C are the likeliest to tip into lecture. (6) The format is a UK-wide shared memory but it is a reference to a type of advert, never to a named charity, so no logos, no real charity name, no real shortcode or phone number on screen.

**Protected lines.** None. The only fixed content is the SCREEN median-pay card (£39,039, ONS, April 2025), which survives verbatim in every option; if the figure or date ever changes, the year in the card changes with it and nothing else in the script depends on the number.
