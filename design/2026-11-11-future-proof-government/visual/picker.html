Watching the Government

Today the government channel is a list. These are three ways to show the government working instead, each built from a real exchange and the real 55 policies. Pick one and I build it into badcode.tv.

A policy is writtenThe opposition attacks itThe statistician checks it can be measuredThe minister answersA human signs it offIts prediction is marked

The exchange shown in A and C is the energy secretary's "Cut the Standing Charge" from the 11 November dry run, word for word but shortened. Prototypes run inside the same TV screen the site uses; narrow your window to see the phone layout.

A

Live from the chamber

The government as a broadcast. One policy at a time goes through the day on air, with a caption for each speaker, the clause being rewritten on screen, and the odds in the corner.

Shows bestThe argument. You see a minister concede a point and the policy change because of it.
Shows leastBreadth. It plays one policy at a time, so the 55 and the scoreboard sit behind it.
On the siteProgramme 1 of the government channel plays the latest exchange; the policy pages stay as they are.

B

The departures board

The government as a teletext page. Every policy is a row, like trains on a board: who owns it, our odds, the day its prediction is marked, and whether it held. The coloured keys filter it.

Use the four coloured keys on screen. Pages turn by themselves, as teletext did.
Shows bestIssue by issue, and that we're on the hook: 55 separate bets with dates, the first due 15 December.
Shows leastThe argument. It says a policy was argued, not what was said.
On the siteBecomes the government's teletext pages and the scoreboard; the daylight pages stay for reading.
Want this one? and paste it to Claude.
C

The workbench

The government as a building site. One policy lies on the bench as a sheet; the opposition's and statistician's notes are stuck beside it; each change is crossed out and rewritten in place. Drag the slider back and forth, or press Undo, to watch it change and change back.

Shows bestSmall, checked and undoable. Every change is one visible edit you can wind back.
Shows leastSpeed and drama. It is a thing you handle, not a thing you watch.
On the siteEvery policy page gets its bench and slider; the list stays as the way in.

Mixing is allowed. A likely blend is B as the front page (the whole government at a glance) with C on each policy's page (how that one changed). Say "B with C inside" if that's it.

Whatever you pick keeps the rules: plain words, no software names on screen, the chain never mentioned, misses printed as loudly as hits.
