# Shape 07: The chorus, the room voices the doubts

The main table only reasons and guesses; every doubt, objection and "that can't be right" is voiced by the eavesdropping next table (and one hissed exchange across the gap), so the viewer's disbelief lives in the room and the four at the table never have to stop and defend themselves. The clock cuts in from the quizmaster. No jokes are written; slots are marked.

## Dialogue 1: The huddle

- **Why it works.** The viewer doubts the number, and the room says it out loud, so the table can stay calm and quick. The next table is a stand-in for the audience: they start where we start (a hundred million), they repeat the plasterer's detail back (so it lands twice), and they are the ones who say "that can't be right". The main table never lectures; the banker and plasterer just talk to each other while being overheard. Guesses climb from a hundred million to "billions" to "two, three" without anyone stating a statistic. Bob says one thing, from his own life, and writes; the room's doubt makes his silence louder.
- **Cuts from current.** The student's second line is now aimed at the next table as well as his own, so it is a flash of irritation rather than the table's only push-back; the plasterer and banker lose their closing sentence in the join; the next table gets lines instead of a mime beat only. Bob gains one short line of experience (a guess at what Jack wants: marked as mine, to rule on) in place of pure silence.
- **Risk.** The eavesdroppers can steal the doubt so completely that the table has no argument of its own, and the "slight argument" Jack asked for becomes a bystander commentary. Two voices outside the four are more casting and more voices to record. Bob's line "I've been in one of them rooms" could tip toward pity or lesson if delivered heavy; it must be flat and brief. "Two. Three." is a guess, not a claim, but check it reads as a guess.
