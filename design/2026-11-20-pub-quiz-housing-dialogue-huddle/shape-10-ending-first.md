# Shape 10: Ending first, written backwards

I wrote the last seconds first: Bob's pen on the sheet, the banker seeing the number and saying nothing, then "Pens down". Everything before it exists only to make that quiet beat feel earned. So the guesses are laid as stepping stones that Bob's number sits just above, the clock lands twice to squeeze the table toward it, and Bob's one line is the only one that carries no sum, just a night he spent in a room. No jokes are written; every slot is marked.

## Dialogue 1: The huddle

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Student | [whispering, pen hovering] A hundred million? Has to be. That's loads. | 3 |
| Plasterer | More. I've done three B&B jobs for the council this year. They pay for every room, every night. | 5 |
| Student | Five hundred million, then. [JOKE SLOT: the student's guess at scale] | 2.5 |
| Next table | [leans in; the table goes silent until they lean back] [JOKE SLOT: the eavesdroppers] | 3 |
| Quizmaster | [over the mic] Twenty seconds. | 1.5 |
| Banker | Nightly's the dearest way to buy anything. You're renting it, not buying it. It's billions, not millions. [JOKE SLOT: what the banker lets slip about himself] | 5.5 |
| Student | Billions? On rooms? That's not possible. | 2.5 |
| Plasterer | Three jobs, one council. Do the sum. | 2 |
| Banker | [low] Bob. | 1 |
| Bob | [takes the pen, no hurry] I was in one of those rooms. Nightly, they said. Every night. [JOKE SLOT: Bob's line, if any] | 4 |
| Quizmaster | [over the mic] Ten seconds. | 1.5 |
| Bob | [writes a number: three billion. Turns the sheet so only the table sees] | 2 |
| Banker | [reads it; says nothing; a small nod] | 1.5 |
| Quizmaster | Pens down. | 1 |
| **Total** | | **36.5** |

(The two clock lines and the two silent beats can overlap the whispers; with overlaps the spoken run is about 30 s.)

- **Why it works.** The ending is a number on paper and a nod, so the short's payoff is Bob being right without anyone explaining why. The guesses climb in three clear steps (a hundred million, five hundred million, billions), so Bob's "three billion" reads as the next obvious step, not a leap. Each of the three gets one reason from their own life: the plasterer's three jobs, the banker's renting-versus-buying, Bob's own night in a room. The student's doubt is the argument, and the quizmaster's clock is what stops the table from talking itself into a speech. Bob says almost nothing and the banker is the one who concedes, so Bob is the hero and the banker is calm and right, not beaten.
- **Cuts from current.** The student's second doubt is kept but shortened. The "Next table" beat moves earlier so the silence lands before the banker, not before Bob. Bob's wordless pen is kept but now carries one personal line, and the pen-writing is split from it so the number is the last visual.
- **Risk.** "Three billion" is a guess, not a claim, but a viewer could read it as Bob knowing the real figure; the reveal in beat 3 has to make clear it is close, not exact. Bob's line ("I was in one of those rooms") sits near pity if it is delivered soft, so it needs to be flat and quick. The plasterer's "Do the sum" is a near-quip and Jack or Kai may want it blank. Total runs about 6 s over target before overlaps.
- **Protected lines.** None. Surviving from draft 1 almost verbatim: the student's "A hundred million? That's loads.", the plasterer's B&B line, the banker's "Nightly's the dearest way to buy anything", the student's "Billions? On rooms?", and "Pens down."
