# Shape 09: Borrowed craft, the overheard room

Approach: I stole three structural tricks from films and TV that fit argument into overheard talk. From 12 Angry Men (the argument advances by one person doubting one small thing, and a quiet voice lands the decisive point), from Armando Iannucci's The Thick of It and In the Loop (people talk over each other, and information arrives in fragments you assemble yourself), and from Aaron Sorkin's walk-and-talk (a ticking pressure makes everyone speak in clipped, finishing-each-other's-points rhythm). I applied them with no speeches: every line is cut off, answered or overridden, the clock and the eavesdropping table do the interrupting, and the numbers climb by objection, never by explanation. No jokes; slots only.

## Dialogue 1: The huddle

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quizmaster | [off, over the room] Thirty seconds. Nearest wins. | 2 |
| Student | [whisper, fast] A hundred million. Tops. | 2 |
| Plasterer | [whisper] No. I've done three of them B&B jobs this year, for the council. Every room, every night, they pay-- | 5 |
| Student | [cutting in] Three jobs is three jobs. That's not the whole country. | 3 |
| Plasterer | [flat] Three jobs, and I didn't even get to the north. | 3 |
| Banker | [low] Billions. Say it, nobody's laughing. Nightly's the dearest way to buy anything, you never own a brick of it. [JOKE SLOT: what the banker lets slip about himself] | 6 |
| Student | [too loud, then hushed] Billions? On rooms? [beat] On B&Bs? | 3 |
| Quizmaster | [off] Twenty seconds. | 1 |
| Next table | [a chair scrapes; two heads lean in; the huddle goes dead silent until they lean back] [JOKE SLOT: the eavesdroppers] | 4 |
| Banker | [mouthing, barely audible] Two. Maybe three. | 2 |
| Plasterer | [mouthing] Three. Nearer three. | 2 |
| Student | [one more try] Why would anyone-- | 1 |
| Bob | [quiet, not looking up; the others stop] I've slept in one. B&Bs and nightly lets. They move you every few nights. [JOKE SLOT: Bob's line, if any] | 5 |
| Quizmaster | [off] Ten seconds. | 1 |
| Bob | [takes the pen, writes a number, turns the paper face down] | 3 |
| Quizmaster | [off] Pens down. | 1 |
| **Total** | | **44** |

**Why it works.** It is built like the 12 Angry Men jury room with the clock of a Sorkin corridor: the argument moves because someone doubts one small thing (the student, "three jobs is three jobs"), and the room only settles when the quietest voice speaks from lived experience and everyone else stops. The Thick of It trick is that nobody finishes a sentence, so the viewer assembles the point themselves, and the guesses climb (hundred million, then billions, then two or three) through objection and eavesdropping, never explanation. The quizmaster's countdown is the interrupter, so nobody can lecture, and the eavesdroppers cost the huddle its volume. Bob never argues, he just gives one fact from his own life and the others fall silent: the hero is the one who doesn't need the clock.

**Cuts from current.** Student's "That's not possible" (the doubt now comes as "three jobs is three jobs", which is an argument, not a gasp). The banker's "It's billions, not millions" becomes "Billions. Say it." Plasterer's "They're paying for every room, every night" is cut off mid-sentence. Bob no longer writes without speaking: he gets one line of experience, then the pen. Overshoots the 30 s target by about 14 s as written (44 s with pauses); the quizmaster countdowns and the eavesdrop beat can be trimmed or overlapped to land near 30 s, and the mouthed lines can run under other speech.

**Risk.** Very fragmented: the whisper-and-cut-off style can read as noise in a voice engine, and two overlapping whispers are hard to voice from separate TTS lines. Bob's "I've slept in one" could read as a pity beat if delivered soft; it must be flat and factual. The mouthed guesses need picture, not audio, or they vanish. The quizmaster countdowns add time and may feel like a gimmick. The banker's "you never own a brick of it" edges towards stating the moral, so check it with Kai. "Three jobs this year" is personal experience, not a statistic, but "I didn't even get to the north" is an invented claim of reach and should go if Jack objects.

**Protected lines.** None protected. "Pens down" survives verbatim as the closing line, and the banker's "Nightly's the dearest way to buy anything" and the plasterer's three B&B jobs survive almost as written.
