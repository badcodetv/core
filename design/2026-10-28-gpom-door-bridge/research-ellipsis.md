# Research brief: ellipsis and the elliptical cut (GPOM door bridge)

Problem: cut the Maddie / old-lady door dialogue; go from "kill bots retreat" to "Maddie leads the humans over the hill" with no explanation and no continuity error.

## Techniques

1. **Enter late, leave early.** Credited to William Goldman (Syd Field is also cited). Come into a scene as late as possible and leave once the audience has what it needs. If a scene needs setup, it probably started too early. Agreement scenes are the classic cut: the decision is the payoff, the debate is not. Examples: any scene that opens with the deal already struck. Why it works: the audience prefers to infer the deal from its effects. Sources: [Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/screenwriting-in-late-out-early/), [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/start-late-leave-early).

2. **Psychological ellipsis (skip the decision, show the struggle and the result).** Show a character's troubled face, then cut to them carrying out the action. Why it works: the audience supplies the reasoning and so owns it. This is generic craft description (a secondary source, [elliptical editing explainer](https://ova.it.com/film-editing-technique/what-is-elliptical-editing)). Example: a character stares at the phone, cut to them at the door.

3. **Hold, then hard-cut across a big jump (Lawrence of Arabia).** The match blown out, held long, then a hard cut to the desert sunrise. The long hold on the first image is what lets the jump land. Per Anne V. Coates, the cut was found rather than planned (it was scripted as a dissolve). Sources: [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/06/legendary-editor-anne-coates-lawrence-of-arabia), [BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/anne-v-coates-lawrence-arabia-editor-1925-2018). Lesson: a graphic or action match makes a large jump read as a deliberate rhyme, not an error. Bordwell prefers "graphic match" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut)).

4. **Match cut across time (2001: the bone to the satellite).** Same shape, different era; the audience supplies the meaning (bone as tool, satellite as tool). Source: [Wikipedia, Match cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut).

5. **Juxtaposition makes the meaning (Kuleshov; The Godfather baptism).** Two shots set side by side create a third idea that neither holds alone. The baptism cuts Michael's vow against the killings. Sources: [Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/kuleshov-effect-guide-77088/), [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/4-iconic-editing-techniques/). Why it works: the audience bridges the gap itself, and bridging is the pleasure.

6. **Montage compression (Pixar's Up, "Married Life"; Hot Fuzz).** Years collapse into minutes with music standing in for dialogue; emotion is carried by rhythm. Secondary student sources only. Example: Up, with no spoken word.

7. **Aftermath instead of the event.** Cut away before the big moment and show its effect. This builds speculation and mystery. Same explainer as technique 2.

## Why audiences accept a big jump (synthesis)
- They need ONE continuity thread across the cut: a matching shape, motion, sound, light, prop or face. The cut then reads as authored rather than missing.
- They need the new state to be legible in one image (these people are now walking, calm, following), so the question "why?" turns into "what?", which is mystery rather than confusion.
- The preceding beat must leave the emotional question open (relief, awe) so the next image answers it.

## Confidence
Moderate. Enter-late and Lawrence of Arabia are well sourced. Kubrick and Godfather are sourced from general reference pages. Bresson, Pixar and Edgar Wright searches returned only student and secondary material; nothing verified on Bresson. Searches found no audience-testing evidence, only critical consensus. Treat the "why it works" lines as craft opinion.

## Three ideas for the door bridge
1. **Match-held cut.** Hold on the last kill bot's red eye going dark (or the retreating line's silhouette), then hard-cut to the same shape: the long line of humans' lamps or heads cresting the hill at dawn, with Maddie small at the front. A graphic match plus a time jump (dusk to dawn) tells the audience time has passed and a choice was made.
2. **Aftermath-only door.** Show the door opening and Maddie's small wheels rolling in, then cut to the door from outside, empty, with hundreds of footprints leading away. The decision is never shown; the evidence of it is.
3. **One unexplained gesture.** One beat: the old lady looks at Maddie, Maddie holds out her little arm, the old lady takes it. Cut. No words. The audience reads trust and an unspoken pact, and the deeper mystery (what the humans have agreed to) stays closed, which sets up the later cost reveal.
