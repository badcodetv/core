BadCode government · the rework · 23 Nov 2026

Not a better Westminster. A different machine.

You set the test. Anyone writes the answer. The calendar marks it. A jury settles what the numbers can't.

The government stops being a cabinet arguing with an opposition. It becomes a working picture of Future Proof: people choose outcomes, not people or parties; every answer competes in the open and is tried small; and the date proves it right or wrong. The ministers stay on top as the face of it, with the powers taken out.

A thought experiment, not a real government. It only has to be the most convincing wrong answer on the internet: concrete enough that people argue about what would actually work.

    Who holds the power

    The machine reads the meter. It never sets the thermostat.

    Humans

    • Set the tests and limits (the Ask)
    • Settle clashes between fields, and move money (the Jury)
    • Sign each trial, or refuse once in public (ministers)
    • Merge everything into the record (Kai or Jack)

    Machines

    • Pick today's three issues by a public rule anyone can read
    • Draft rival answers, blind, on different AI models
    • Cost them, grade the evidence, check every limit
    • Read the number on the date, against a rule fixed in advance

    Expertise counts in one place only: forecasting what an answer will do. Your weight there is your marked record, never your title. Money never follows popularity: each field has a floor, and only the jury moves cash, in small steps with their own tests.

    The surface

    The cabinet: politicians with the powers taken out

    Ministers may

    • Read out what the engine found, and grumble
    • Sign a trial, or refuse once in public
    • Pull their own failing policy, and keep the job
    • Lose to another minister's limit, in a table

    Ministers may not

    • Set a goal or choose the agenda
    • Pick the winner among rival answers
    • Mark their own prediction
    • Announce anything without its test

    The point of it

    What we hand political scientists to go and test

    1. Tests, policies or parties. Ask matched groups to choose each way. Which answers hold a month later, and which are shared across camps?

    2. The stand-in rule. Count those who turn up as themselves, and fill the empty seats by lot. Does that look more like the whole country than volunteers alone?

    3. Safe to stop. Fix a kill number before a pilot, and protect the official who stops it on that number. Do more failing pilots actually get stopped?
