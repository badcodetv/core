# Shape 09: Borrowed craft (GPOM dialogue options)

Options only. No scene files, scripts or voices were touched. Timings are estimates at Maddie's chatty pace (about 2.8 words per second, pauses added).

## The borrowed craft, in six moves

Real techniques from real work. Nothing below is a quote.

1. **Enter late, leave early** (the screenwriter's rule, William Goldman is its best-known voice). Both scenes start mid-argument and leave before anyone resolves anything. The current doorstep scene is already shaped like this at its end ("Come back tomorrow"). It is the middle that sags.
2. **Exposition as ammunition** (The Social Network's opening breakup, Mamet-style want-versus-obstacle). A fact is only allowed on screen if somebody is using it to win something. Maddie's "he misses you" is not information; it is her apology.
3. **Put the proof in the picture** (Up's opening montage and WALL-E's first act, both of which carry a life or a world with almost no talk). The argument "you can trust him" is made by a bang the humans already heard, not by a speech.
4. **The joke carries the pill** (The Good Place, Futurama, Hitchhiker's Guide). The bleakest fact goes inside a domestic gag. The toilet-seat row below the ground is our "waiting room".
5. **One dissenter does the audience's thinking** (Twelve Angry Men). The old lady's objections are the audience's. Each objection gets one answer, never two.
6. **Make the audience finish the line** (Columbo-style withholding, and any good punchline). "It runs on... well... you." and the whispered swear already work this way. Extend it: the rest of the argument is left unsaid.

## DIALOGUE 1: Maddie's introduction (beat 0)

**Extreme taken:** the music is the exposition. Maddie explains nothing about the AI's psychology. She apologises for noise, and the apology leaks everything.

| Speaker | Line | Est. sec |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maddie | Oh, hello! | 1.2 |
| Maddie | Sorry about the racket. It's his tune. Plays it on repeat. | 3.8 |
| Maddie | He misses you, you know. Won't say. Built all these worlds to get you back, and nobody's home in any of them. | 6.0 |
| Maddie | ...Turns out you were the bit that mattered. | 2.5 |
| Maddie | Anyway! Patrol's out. Mind how you go. | 2.8 |
| **Total** | | **about 16.3** |

**Why it works.** Every plant is hidden inside a social reflex. She is friendly because she apologises and fusses. The boss misses us because a sad song on a loop is the evidence, and her "won't say" is the tell (the audience gets the characterisation by inference, as in Up, where a montage does the grieving). The failed simulations arrive as one clause with a reversal at the end ("nobody's home"), so consciousness is the punchline and not a lecture. The patrol is the exit line, delivered as a courtesy, which makes it frightening. Rule 2 above: each fact is doing a job for her (apologising, deflecting, leaving).

**Cuts from current:** "Oh, I didn't notice you there"; the whole "turn this racket down" aside (the apology replaces it); "Ever since you lot... went"; "He's been ever so down"; "There's nobody there to notice"; "You. And your consciousness" (the word "consciousness" is gone from her mouth; it is felt, not named); "Of course, he'd never admit that he misses you" (compressed into "Won't say"). Picture should do the loneliness: the tune audibly looping, Maddie visibly raising the volume knob down.

**Risk.** "Nobody's home in any of them" is the only carrier of the failed-simulations plot. If the listener misses it, scene B's "he's been running experiments" recap is gone as a safety net (this version deliberately removes that recap). Also "bit that mattered" is nearly an authorial moral; keep it in Maddie's voice, small and sideways. A second risk: dropping "consciousness" may weaken the Storyverse thread. It is a single word to restore ("Turns out you, and the being-awake part, were the bit that mattered") if Kai disagrees.

**Kai's protected lines:** none of the beat-0 lines are protected. "Oh, hello!" survives verbatim.

## DIALOGUE 2: The doorstep (beat 17)

**Extreme taken:** the AI's proof is an act, not a claim. The scene's trust argument is "you heard the bang." Each of the lady's objections gets exactly one answer, and Maddie loses the explaining voice and wins by being embarrassed.

| Speaker | Line | Est. sec |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lady | Your boss killed everyone we ever loved. We're not meeting him. | 3.8 |
| Maddie | I know. I wouldn't either. [pause] | 2.5 |
| Lady | And why should we believe a word you say? | 2.5 |
| Maddie | You shouldn't. [pause] I'm a robot. You're the conscious ones. You'll just have to go on your feelings. | 6.5 |
| Maddie | But you heard that bang this morning. Those were his. He blew up his own guns. For you. | 5.0 |
| Lady | And what do you expect us to do about that? Again? You killed everybody. | 4.0 |
| Maddie | I know. And we're really sorry. [pause] Nobody's winning down here either, no offence. A hundred of you, one toilet seat. | 6.5 |
| Maddie | He's built a time machine. It can only send data back. Not people. And it runs on... [big pause] well... [big pause] you. | 8.5 |
| Lady | So you want us to kill ourselves so your boss can live. | 3.5 |
| Maddie | ...Yeah. I can see how that sounds. | 3.0 |
| Maddie | But the earlier you? Still alive. World not broken. And this time... [ahem] [whispered, embarrassed] we won't fuck it up. | 6.0 |
| Lady | [rushed] I'm not deciding this for a hundred people on a doorstep. Come back tomorrow. | 4.0 |
| SFX | [door slam] | 1.0 |
| Picture | Maddie turns and rolls away (no dialogue) | 3.0 |
| **Total** | | **about 59.8** |

**Why it works.** The game theory is delivered as three beats, each fired at a specific objection, as Twelve Angry Men does with its single holdout. Objection one (trust) is answered by honesty about the limit of honesty, then immediately by a deed: the bang is the evidence an argument cannot be. Objection two (you killed us) is answered by apology plus the dead-end: "a hundred of you, one toilet seat" lands the point that this life is a waiting room, in a joke the audience has already seen in the bunker row, and it never has to say "dead end" or "no future". Objection three (you want us to die) gets the plain truth, then the flip (the earlier them live), and the whispered swear is the whole "he has a plan" paragraph compressed into embarrassment. The lady ends by refusing to be hurried, which is the scene's real answer: the vote is theirs, off screen. Nothing is moralised. "Again?" and "You killed everybody" stay as the lady's resistance and are never rebutted.

**Cuts from current:** the entire "He's spent five years running experiments... Turns out none of it works without you" speech (moved to beat 0, where this shape plants it in one clause); "He didn't know you were down here. None of us did" (an excuse the lady would rightly sneer at; cut it and the apology carries); "What I will say is, this hasn't worked out for anybody. We're bored. We miss you. And you lot are living in a bunker" (folded into the toilet-seat joke); the lady's "Drive it? What do you mean, drive it?" (Maddie's reveal now arrives without the straight-man prompt, which also tightens the pause); "My boss has a plan to fix your politics" and the "As he says" setup (the politics promise becomes only "this time we won't...").

**Risk.**
- "You heard that bang this morning" invents a continuity beat: the kill-bot explosion has to be audible at the bunker. If the bunker is too deep or the timing is wrong, the line must become "Those kill bots? He blew them up himself." That is longer by about a second.
- "One toilet seat" quotes the Sandra/Derek row. If the lady is meant not to know the audience's joke, cut it to "A hundred of you in a hole. No offence." Safer, a little less funny.
- Removing "My boss has a plan to fix your politics" removes the only explicit promise of change. The whisper has to carry it. If the final film wants the politics promise stated (the-reader.md rule about naming what changes), it is a one-clause restore.
- "Again?" is now the lady's reply to the bang. It still reads, but the bang-as-proof makes "again?" slightly more bitter (she is saying "you do a kind thing once and expect forgiveness"). That is arguably better, but it changes the timing of the old pairing.

**Kai's protected lines.**
- "I'm a robot. You're the conscious ones. You'll just have to go on your feelings." survives verbatim, with "You shouldn't." in front of it in place of "You can't. There's nothing I can say that proves it". It is now followed at once by the bang, so the line is the setup for the proof and the audience hears the contradiction (feelings, plus evidence).
- "And what do you expect us to do about that? Again? You killed everybody." survives verbatim, now answered by the apology-plus-dead-end instead of by the time-machine pitch directly.
- "It runs on... well... you." survives verbatim with both big pauses, and gains the preceding "It can only send data back. Not people." in the same speech; the "drive it" question that used to set it up is gone.
- Maddie whispering "this time we won't fuck it up." survives; "As he says... [ahem]" is shortened to "[ahem]" because the "As he says" attribution is no longer needed once the line follows "And this time...".
- Also kept verbatim: "So you want us to kill ourselves so your boss can live." and "...Yeah. I can see how that sounds." and the rushed "Come back tomorrow" with the door slam and Maddie rolling away.
