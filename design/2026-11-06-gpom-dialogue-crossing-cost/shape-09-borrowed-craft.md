# Shape 09: Borrowed craft: the leaflet, the bomb under the table, the kettle

Three techniques from real screen work, pushed past comfort. Hitchcock's bomb under the table (from his talks with Truffaut): tell the audience the dreadful fact first and let the scene run on dread, not surprise. Chernobyl episode 1 and the NHS consent form share a register: procedure and plain facts carry the horror, so it is never described. Maddie reads out the terms like a nurse before a minor operation. The opening of Pixar's Up and Alan Bennett's Talking Heads show a whole marriage compressed into small domestic objects and understatement; here the toilet seat carries thirty years.

## Dialogue 1: The wafer: what it costs

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DEREK | What is it? | 1.0 |
| MADDIE | It's the time machine. It runs on people. On you. All hundred, all at once. | 4.5 |
| SANDRA | Runs on us how? | 1.5 |
| MADDIE | Your minds. Bit like nodding off. Bit like dying, if I'm honest. | 4.5 |
| SANDRA | Will it hurt? | 1.2 |
| MADDIE | Less than the last five years. | 2.2 |
| DEREK | And after? | 1.0 |
| MADDIE | There isn't one, love. You don't wake up. Only he goes. He's just data, see. Data can travel. | 5.5 |
| DEREK | [flat, to Sandra] Is there tea? | 1.5 |
| MADDIE | Kettle's on. | 1.2 |
| **Total** | | **24.1** |

**Why it works.** The consent-leaflet method: Maddie covers the standard items in order (what it is, how it works, what it feels like, what happens after), and the humans ask only a patient's questions. The bomb is on the table by the fourth line. The tea closes the beat in the Chernobyl register, where the routine after the verdict is the horror.

**Cuts from current.** "It'll send all of you back..." (implies survival) and "it'll send him too, so he can help you avoid it" (states the moral).

**Risk.** The most explicit option; "bit like dying" could tip into cruelty. "Is there tea?" could read as a gag; needs a held mug shot and a beat before it. At 24 s it sits at the top of the target; if it runs over, cut "Is there tea?" and "Kettle's on." together (saves 2.7 s).

**Protected lines.** "What is it?" survives verbatim.

## Dialogue 2: The goodbye: two hands

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SANDRA | [eyes front, hand finding his] Seat down? | 1.5 |
| DEREK | Seat down. | 1.0 |
| SANDRA | Liar. | 0.8 |
| DEREK | Thirty years, love. Never once. [pause] Always up. | 3.0 |
| SANDRA | [a squeeze] I know. | 1.2 |
| **Total** | | **7.5** |

**Why it works.** A whole marriage carried by one domestic object and an understated lie. "Seat down?" asks "are you here?" and "Seat down." answers "I'm here". Sandra's "I know" shows she has always known. Nothing mentions after. It ends on a squeeze and a jab, so the crossing arrives straight after the joke.

**Cuts from current.** "Does this mean I get to hang out with non-grumpy Derek?" and "Yes, love. Are you ready for this?".

**Risk.** "Liar" then "Always up" is dense for TTS, and the reversal can read as a mistake. Fallback: "Seat down?" / "Seat down." / "Liar." / "Aye." (4.1 s). Depends on the seat row having landed earlier.

**Protected lines.** None.
