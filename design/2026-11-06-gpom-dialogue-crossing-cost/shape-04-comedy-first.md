# Shape 04: Comedy first, the jokes carry the argument

I let bluntness and bad timing deliver every hard fact. Maddie says the terrible thing plainly and kindly, a human answers with a deadpan jab or an irrelevant practical question, and the weight sits in the gap after the joke. Nobody names a feeling; the facts (it runs on you, it feels like dying, you don't wake up, only he goes) are each one short sentence, and the laugh arrives a beat after the fact has landed.

## Dialogue 1: The wafer: what it costs

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DEREK | What is it? | 1.0 |
| MADDIE | It's the time machine. It runs on you. All hundred of you, at once. | 4.0 |
| SANDRA | Runs on us how? | 1.2 |
| MADDIE | Your minds, love. Feels like nodding off. Bit like dying, if I'm honest. | 3.8 |
| SANDRA | Will it hurt? | 1.0 |
| MADDIE | Less than the last five years, he reckons. | 2.5 |
| SANDRA | And after? | 1.0 |
| MADDIE | There isn't an after. You don't wake up. Only he goes. He's just data. | 4.5 |
| DEREK | [flat] Is there tea? | 1.2 |
| MADDIE | [beat] There's tea. | 1.0 |
| **Total** | | **21.2** |

**Why it works.** The facts come in the order a frightened person would ask for them: what, how, does it hurt, what happens after. Each answer is one plain sentence from Maddie, so it never feels like a speech. Sandra's "And after?" is the only moment she stops jabbing, and Maddie answers it with no joke at all, so the room goes quiet. Derek then asks about tea, which is bad timing used as bravery: he has taken it in and is doing the practical thing. "There's tea" is Maddie being kind in the only way left, and the mug is already in the shot.

**Cuts from current.** "It'll send all of you back", "before it all went wrong", and "so he can help you avoid it" (all imply the humans survive and travel). The explanation of purpose is dropped; earlier scenes already carry it.

**Risk.** "Bit like dying, if I'm honest" may be too blunt for Maddie's warmth, and "Less than the last five years" from Maddie can read as her being unkind (the "he reckons" is there to put the dig on the boss). The tea beat could tip into sitcom if Derek is voiced too jokey; it needs to be flat and tired. "And after?" followed by "You don't wake up" is stated twice over (nodding off, then no waking), which is deliberate but could feel heavy. "He's just data" leans on earlier lines; check it does not undercut "only he goes".

**Protected lines.** "What is it?" survives verbatim as Derek's opener.

## Dialogue 2: The goodbye: two hands

| Speaker | Line | Est. s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SANDRA | Seat down? | 1.0 |
| DEREK | [long pause] Seat down. | 1.8 |
| SANDRA | Liar. | 0.8 |
| DEREK | Always was. Night, love. | 2.0 |
| SANDRA | Night, you lump. | 1.4 |
| **Total** | | **7.0** |

**Why it works.** The toilet-seat row is the whole marriage, so the last promise either of them makes is the one the audience knows he never kept. Sandra's "Liar" and Derek's "Always was" is thirty years of love as a jab, with no word of love in it until "Night, love", which is the euphemism for the sleep they are walking into. Nobody says goodbye, forever, or see you; "Night" does all of that without stating it, and it promises no morning.

**Cuts from current.** "Does this mean I get to hang out with non-grumpy Derek?" (implies an afterwards), and "Yes, love. Are you ready for this?" (the readiness question is now answered by the hands).

**Risk.** "Night, love" could be read as a hopeful "see you tomorrow" if voiced bright; it must be low and slow. "Always was" asks Derek to admit he lies about the seat, which may slightly soften his gruffness, though it is affectionate. The hand-hold has to be on screen, as no line mentions it. If the row has not been set up earlier in the film, "Seat down?" will not land alone.

**Protected lines.** None.
