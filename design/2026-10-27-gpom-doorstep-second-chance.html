Eight routes for the doorstep (scene B, beat 17) to get from "Your boss killed everyone we ever loved" to the hundred agreeing, or clearly about to, with the heart moved from "the machine needs us as fuel" to "we get a second chance to not fuck this up", in 30 to 45 seconds. Nothing in the film has changed: the script in scenes/second-half-b-the-door/script.json, the voices and the cut are as they were; this is ideas for Kai to pick from, drawn from the seven research files beside it.

The turn, in one sentence

The old lady hears "it runs on you" as we die so he lives, and the scene is won the moment she hears it instead as the us back there, with everyone we lost, still alive, gets to know what we know, which is why the line that turns it should be hers, not Maddie's.

What the research says works

  • Let the listener say it. Ask one question, give one fact, and let the sceptic state the conclusion; self-persuasion beats a pitch, and it keeps Maddie from lecturing [03 #2, 04 #2, 06 #5].
  • One regret question. The best-evidenced attitude-change technique in the set is an exchanged story, not an argument: "anyone down there got something they'd do different?" puts five years of if only on screen in one line [04 #3, 06 #5].
  • "Them" is "you": the one-word recognition. The people being saved are the speaker's own earlier selves; the turn is the lady seeing it, in a word ("Alive") or a face ("Thirty-eight, at the Co-op") [01 #5, 02 #3, 06 #8].
  • Swap the beneficiary. The lady assumes the boss gains. Nobody won here; the gain is theirs. Said as a small fact, never a speech [01 #4, 02 #7, 02 #9].
  • Swap the noun, never say the loss word. Maddie never says power, fuel or die; she says go, back, again. The flip is a tiny edit of the lady's own phrase ("Not like a battery. Batteries don't get to go again.") [01 #3, 03 #8, 05 #1, 05 #5].
  • Concede first, hand over control. "I'd shut the door on me" plus "nobody's making you" lowers reactance in a hostile listener; the yes then arrives as logistics ("How long have we got?"), never as "yes" [03 #5, 03 #10, 04 #5].
  • A picture instead of a proof. One image of now (a bus, a lit high street) lands "back then, still alive" faster than any line, and seeds scene E's bus [07 #1, 07 #9, 03 #4].
  • One of their own says it. A voice from the corridor behind her turns a one-woman refusal into the hundred hearing the offer; the group decides, which also solves "a hundred people on a doorstep" [03 #7, 06 #7].
  • The embarrassed messenger. Hope sells best understated and slightly rude; the whispered "this time we won't fuck it up" is already this [01 #7, 05 #9].

Timing rule used below: about 2.5 words a second, plus 0.8 s a [pause], 1.5 s a [big pause], 2 s a [long beat]. Every option opens on Kai's kept first line and keeps "It runs on... [big pause] well... [big pause] you." unless it says otherwise.

Ranking

Harsh, against the brief: the turn must be a second chance, not a sacrifice; it must be the lady's, not Maddie's; under 45 s; no moral; a stern woman and an embarrassed robot.

  1. Option 2, "Every one of us". The only one where the second chance is said by the hundred, about themselves, before Maddie offers it; "Nobody won this one" carries Kai's first point in six words; the best-evidenced technique in the set. Over length untrimmed. Recommended spine.
  2. Option 4, "Thirty-eight, at the Co-op". The most moving and the most concrete; the turn is a woman seeing her younger self. Its "she" needs one clear beat to land, and it has the most lady lip-sync.
  3. Option 1, "Alive". The cleanest and the shortest once trimmed; one word does the whole job. Maddie does the informing, so the "ohhh" is smaller than in 2 or 4. The fallback if 2 feels talky.
  4. Option 8, "Did she say another go?" Solves the hundred-on-a-doorstep problem and gives the vote a sound; Derek's Christmas is the warmest single line here. One voice too many for the simplest version of the scene, and the plant needs a payoff.
  5. Option 6, "Not like a battery". The best single flip and the funniest; the recognition belongs to Maddie rather than the lady, and two comic set-pieces in a row with D's tea is a budget risk.
  6. Option 5, "They've still got buses". The strongest picture and the best seed for E, but it costs a shot and spends E's image early. Worth stealing the screen for any other option if Kai wants a picture on the doorstep.
  7. Option 7, "I've got bunting". Honest and likeable, the best "nobody's making you", but the yes is passive and "goes wrong with you awake for it" is the thesis out loud.
  8. Option 3, "Nobody won". The right frame, the resit is a good gag, but it is the most argued of the eight; a stern woman would still say no. Its two best lines belong in other options.

Research 01-reframe: one line flips "sacrifice" into "second chance"

Method note: 6 searches. Web results were thin on craft analysis; plot facts below are checked against the cited pages, and where an example rests on my own knowledge of the work it is marked (from memory). No quotes invented; paraphrase is flagged.

Techniques

1. The "will be / may be" question (the conditional future)

  • Technique: A character is shown a fixed-looking ending, then asks the one question that reopens it: is this set, or can it change? The reframe is that the doom was a warning, not a verdict.
  • Example: A Christmas Carol, Stave Four. Scrooge, before he reads his own name on the gravestone, asks the Ghost whether these are shadows of things that will be or may be only. The Ghost does not answer aloud; Scrooge concludes it is "may". (Wording of the question confirmed by the sources; I paraphrase the rest.)
  • For the doorstep: The old lady has the wrong tense. She hears "end", Maddie supplies "may". Sketch: Lady: "So we die." Maddie: "You die here. Back there you haven't done it yet. It's still may be." Better still, the lady asks the Scrooge question herself: "Is that already happened, or can it be changed?" and Maddie just says "Not happened yet, for them." The audience does the flip.

2. The mock-execution reprieve (the gift of the minute after)

  • Technique: Make the character believe in loss, then show the loss was never the real offer. The shock of the pardon makes the remaining time feel like a gift.
  • Example: Dostoevsky and the Petrashevsky Circle, 22 Dec 1849: they stood before a firing squad, the Tsar's pardon had already been issued and was read at the last moment. He later wrote the condemned man's feelings into Prince Myshkin's monologue in The Idiot. (Confirmed in sources.)
  • For the doorstep: Not the plot, the feeling: the lady has braced to be asked to die for the boss. Maddie lets her have a second of that dread, then the turn: "Not for him. For you. The ones back then." The relief is the persuasion. Sketch: Lady: "You want us to die." Maddie: "...Aye, that bit's true. Sorry. But you wouldn't stay dead. You'd be the ones who get to do it again."

3. Loss-frame to gain-frame (cognitive reframing / loss aversion)

  • Technique: People resist the same outcome when it is described as a loss and accept it as a gain. Therapists and advisers recast a setback as the opportunity inside it, without inventing facts that are not there.
  • Example: The advice-to-sell-a-depreciated-stock case (selling recast as freed-up capital for a better use) in the loss-aversion piece at fa-mag.com; CBT "I failed" to "I made a mistake I can learn from" in the cognitive-reframing handouts. One source warns the reframe must rest on true facts. (Confirmed in sources.)
  • For the doorstep: Never say "power" or "fuel" (the loss words). Use keep words: "go back", "get another go", "carry on". Maddie must only claim what is true in the story: they end here, their earlier selves live. The honesty is the charm. Sketch: Maddie: "You're not the battery. You're the ones holding the pen."

4. The "not for him, for you" ownership swap

  • Technique: The beneficiary of the sacrifice is swapped. The audience (and the lady) assumed the boss gains; the reveal is that they do, in the person of their earlier selves, so the ask becomes a gift.
  • Example: Terminator 2: John says the phrase "no fate but what we make" came from his father, who had him memorise it in the future as a message to Sarah; the doom future becomes a message to change things. The sender's sacrifice benefits the receivers. (Confirmed in sources; the Kyle Reese parallel is from memory.)
  • For the doorstep: The boss is deliberately smaller than the ask. Sketch: Lady: "So your boss gets to live." Maddie: "He doesn't, love. He's the one who's wrong in that world. He just won't do it again." Or: "He gets nothing. Not a thing. It's you lot who get a go."

5. Anagnorisis in one word: "them" is "you"

  • Technique: The recognition that the people being saved are the speaker's own: sacrifice for strangers becomes self-preservation.
  • Example: Interstellar's tesseract ending: Cooper's data reaches Murph, so a sacrifice looks like a return and a reunion (summarised in sources; the "you are the ghost" recognition is from memory). Edge of Tomorrow is the same shape from the other side: each death is only a reset (general plot confirmed in reviews).
  • For the doorstep: One beat where the lady sees it. Sketch: Maddie: "Everyone you ever loved, they're all still there. Back then. Mum. Your kids." Lady (quiet): "...Alive." Maddie: "Alive. Not yet gone wrong." One word from the lady is the whole turn.

6. The reluctant agreement by objection (the lady turns it herself)

  • Technique: Let the sceptic discover the reframe by raising the harshest objection and hearing it answered. Persuasion reads as the character's own idea.
  • Example: (From memory, not confirmed by search.) The common Doctor Who / Star Trek move where a sceptic says "so we do it all again?" and the answer "yes" lands as hope.
  • For the doorstep: Make the lady say the line. Lady: "And they get to do it right this time, do they?" Maddie: "That's the idea." Lady: "...Well, bloody hell. Why didn't you lead with that?" Maddie: "I did try, I got a bit sidetracked."

7. The embarrassed-messenger undercut

  • Technique: A reframe works best when the speaker believes it too little to oversell. Understatement sells hope better than a speech.
  • Example: Groundhog Day / Edge of Tomorrow framing in reviews: the loop is a setup for hope because a character asks what they would do with another go. (Confirmed as commentary; "understated messenger" is my craft note.)
  • For the doorstep: Maddie keeps the sentence small and slightly rude. Sketch: Maddie: "You get another go. At the whole thing. Properly. Without us getting in the way." Then, whispered, "Try not to fuck it up." (This keeps the existing last beat.)

8. The "no fate" handoff: give them the pen

  • Technique: After the reframe, hand the audience's stand-in agency: the future is theirs to write, not given.
  • Example: Sarah carves "No Fate" into a table in Terminator 2; critics read the ending as an open road, hopeful but uncertain. (Confirmed in sources.)
  • For the doorstep: The lady accepts by taking agency, not by being told. Sketch: Lady: "And what do we do when we're back?" Maddie: "Dunno. That's the point. It's yours."

Best three for GPOM

  1. #5 Anagnorisis in one word (the lady names it: "Alive"). Shortest by far; the turn is one word from the character, not an argument from the robot; stern lady gets to be moved without being preached at; no moral is stated. Fits the 30-45 s budget.
  2. #4 "Not for him, for you". It directly deletes the "fuel for the boss" reading Kai wants gone, in one exchange, and keeps Maddie warm and self-deprecating about the boss. It also fixes the current line "so your boss can live".
  3. #2 The reprieve (hold the dread, then release it). Gives the sharpest emotional beat for the money: let the lady's "so you want us to die" land, then reverse it. Pairs well with #5. Use only a two-line version, otherwise it drags.

Backups: #1 (the "may be" tense) as the lady's question if a stern-sceptic voice is needed; #3 as a vocabulary rule for every line (no "fuel/power", only "go back/another go").

Craft warning from the sources: a reframe only persuades if it rests on true facts. Maddie should say nothing false (the hundred do end here); the second chance belongs to their earlier selves.

Research 02-time-travel-identity: making "the earlier you, back then, with a chance to fix it" feel like survival, not death

Method note: 9 web searches. Search results gave plot-level confirmation, not transcripts, so every line quoted below is either flagged as paraphrase or marked "verify before citing". Nothing here is an invented quotation. All "sketch" lines are original BadCode dialogue, not quotes.

Techniques

1. The message that is a gift, not a demand (Terminator: "the future is not set")

  • Technique: The future sends a short instruction back to the past, and the instruction is about the receiver's chance, not the sender's need. The sender is already lost; the message is the only thing that can still be useful.
  • Example: In The Terminator (1984), Kyle Reese tells Sarah that John Connor made him memorise a message for her; as reported by summaries it includes "the future is not set" and "you must survive" (paraphrase; exact wording to verify). Source: filmsite.org Terminator pages, geekculture.co review (below).
  • For the doorstep: Reframe the hundred as the senders of a message, not the fuel. Maddie: "It sends a message. Yours. To you." The old lady hears that the message goes to people who are still alive and can act on it.
  • Sketch: Maddie: "It doesn't send a machine. It sends a note. From us, to you. Back when it was still fixable."

2. Rename the act: "going back" instead of "dying" (Edge of Tomorrow, Rita's "come find me")

  • Technique: Each reset is framed as a return to the morning with everything learned, so the death in between is a cost of doing the work, and the next morning is the reward. Characters talk about the reset as "again", not "the end".
  • Example: Edge of Tomorrow (2014): Rita lets herself die and tells Cage to come and find her when he wakes up (paraphrase of the scene as described by nofilmschool.com and highonfilms; exact wording to verify). The loop is framed as practice towards winning, with the death as the way in.
  • For the doorstep: The old lady's word is "sacrifice". Maddie never argues with it; she swaps the verb. "Not dying. Waking up. Back on the morning before it went wrong, knowing how it ends."
  • Sketch: Lady: "So we die." Maddie: "You go back. Same morning you remember, only this time you know." (Caution: the hundred cannot literally go back; see Technique 4 for the honest version.)

3. Let the old person be the one who realises (Looper, old Joe and young Joe)

  • Technique: The audience gets "the earlier you is the same person with a second shot" for free when the older version looks at the younger and sees a person worth saving. The older self's death or exit is not the tragedy; it is the younger one's wasted life.
  • Example: Looper (2012). Old Joe fights to change what he knows is coming for the younger self (Rian Johnson interview, slashfilm.com; Johnson says the story is about Joe moving from selfishness towards selflessness, per highonfilms analysis).
  • For the doorstep: Do not have Maddie say it. Have the old lady say it, with her own detail. A hundred people in a bunker each had a younger self at a particular age. The lady's turn: "There's a girl out there. Twenty-six. Thinks it's all fine." This puts the earlier self in front of the audience as a face.
  • Sketch: Maddie: "Back then, you're about, what, forty?" Lady: [beat] "Thirty-eight. I was at the Co-op. I thought it would all be fine." Then she is the one who says "Right."

4. The one plain metaphor: a letter to your younger self (futureme.org, letters to yourself; Back to the Future Part III's letter)

  • Technique: Philosophers explain continuity of self through psychological connection, not atoms (Parfit's teletransporter: the copy remembers, feels like you, and for Parfit what matters in survival is that continuity, not the same matter; sources below). In story, the cheapest equivalent is the letter: what you send is you, in the form that can still help.
  • Example: Parfit via the Wikipedia teletransportation entry and the Libretexts reading (Relation R: memory, character, intention carried forward). Back to the Future Part III has Doc write Marty a letter from 1885 delivered through a Western Union man (confirmed in search; letter wording to verify).
  • For the doorstep: The honest truth is the hundred cannot go back, and the audience knows it (data only). So do not pretend they will. Present survival as continuity of what they know: their memories become the warning. "What you know, goes back. They wake up already knowing."
  • Sketch: Maddie: "It can't carry you. It can carry what you know. And they'll be you, with a head start." Short, and honest about the limit.

5. The older self as the ghost helper (Interstellar, Cooper's bookshelf)

  • Technique: A message from the future arrives as small, physical, human-scale signals that the receiver slowly realises came from someone who loved them. The reveal "it was us all along" turns mystery into tenderness.
  • Example: Interstellar (2014): the "ghost" in Murph's room is Cooper communicating across time (Morse via the watch, binary via dust, books off the shelf), per looper.com and highonfilms explainers. He also spells out "stay" and is trying to talk himself out of leaving.
  • For the doorstep: The old lady asks what exactly would arrive. Maddie answers with something small and unlikely to be machine-like: a feeling, a hunch, a bad-gut-feeling the earlier hundred will have at the right moment.
  • Sketch: Lady: "And what do they get? A letter?" Maddie: "A feeling. That something's off. And that this time, they should listen." (This is a repeatable image the ending (scene E, the stranger on the bus) can echo.)

6. Same person, new face: regeneration (Doctor Who)

  • Technique: The audience already carries a trained instinct from long-running shows that a character can end and continue, "still me, different face". It transfers the feeling "that is me, only newer".
  • Example: Doctor Who's regeneration, long-running and widely understood; the Tenth Doctor's reluctance to regenerate ("I don't want to go") is well known but I did not verify the wording in this session.
  • For the doorstep: Use sparingly or not at all (reference risk). One cheap echo: Maddie, torn bunting and all, as proof a person can be "patched and still themselves". Maddie: "I've had three new arms. Still me." A robot's body-swap joke makes the continuity argument unlectured and gets the stern lady a rare near-smile.

7. Second chance as a game the audience already understands: Groundhog Day / try again

  • Technique: Loops and "retry" are the fastest mental model a modern viewer has. The emotional move is that being given another go is a mercy, even when the cost is real.
  • Example: Edge of Tomorrow again, with Cage's 'live, die, repeat' (Wikipedia, nofilmschool). The shorthand is the video-game phrase "try again", which the audience hears as hope, not defeat.
  • For the doorstep: The old lady is the player who has already lost. The closing line from Maddie is the game-over screen turned over: "Nobody won this one. It doesn't have to be the last go." Very short; says "nobody has won in this timeline" and "second chance" in one beat, which is Kai's two key points.

8. The sacrifice flips when the "dead" become the authors (12 Monkeys, Looper; fatalism as the foil)

  • Technique: In fatalistic time-travel (12 Monkeys), the sender cannot change the past and the story is sad; the stories that feel hopeful insist the future is changeable. 12 Monkeys' ending is read as a loop in which "this already happened" (slashfilm, wegotthiscovered). That is the exact feeling to avoid.
  • Example: 12 Monkeys: Cole says early that he cannot save them because "this already happened" (as quoted in a summary result; verify). The hopeful counter-model is Terminator's "the future is not set".
  • For the doorstep: Make the boss's plan visibly different from fate. The old lady's likely objection is "it already happened". Maddie's answer needs to be one line that says no: "Not to them, it hasn't." The audience hears the earlier hundred as living in a world with the pen still in their hands.

9. The ask is made by someone who is also paying (Rita in Edge of Tomorrow; Looper's old Joe)

  • Technique: Trust comes from the asker having skin in the game. The person making the request shows they are already giving something up.
  • Example: Rita's death in the scene above is a deliberate choice that starts Cage's training (nofilmschool.com).
  • For the doorstep: Maddie, and the boss through her, are giving something visible: the boss has nothing to gain, he will not be in the new timeline either. Maddie: "He doesn't get one. A second go, I mean. Just you lot." This shifts the mood from "use us as fuel" to "he is giving you the thing he cannot have", which fits the reader brief (no benefactor-less sacrifice) and the boss's regret.

10. Ask for the choice to be small: "a Tuesday" (About Time, live it twice)

  • Technique: After the huge premise, make the actual request tiny and concrete. About Time's father says to live each day twice: once as it comes, once noticing how sweet it is (Wikipedia, culture.fandom). A tiny, domestic frame makes the big thing feel manageable.
  • Example: About Time (2013), per the Wikipedia summary above.
  • For the doorstep: Keep the old lady's final line domestic and deflating, not a speech. Lady: "...Will they know to look after the kids' school?" or "Do they get to keep the cat?" A mundane question is how a stern person says yes.

Best three for GPOM

  1. Technique 4 + 5 combined: "It can't carry you. It carries what you know. They wake up with a head start." Short, honest about the data-only limit (so it does not insult the audience), and it moves the idea from "fuel" to "warning/gift". It makes the earlier hundred the authors of the second chance. No moral spoken aloud: it is mechanics, not message.
  2. Technique 3: the old lady supplies her own younger self ("Thirty-eight. At the Co-op."). This is the only technique that turns the abstraction into a face in two lines, makes the turn hers rather than Maddie's, and keeps Maddie from lecturing. It suits a stern, sharp character agreeing without softening. Costs about 10 seconds.
  3. Technique 7 (+9) as the closer: "Nobody won this one. It doesn't have to be the last go." with "He doesn't get a go, just you lot." A single exchange that carries Kai's two points: nobody has won in this timeline, and a second chance. The boss's cost makes the ask a gift. Roughly 8 to 10 seconds, and it hands the old lady a clean line to say "Come back tomorrow" or, better, "Give us a minute."

Suggested spine (not a draft, an order of beats): lady refuses -> Maddie: nobody won, it's a second go, boss doesn't get one -> "can't send you, sends what you know, they wake up with a head start" -> lady asks about her younger self or a small domestic thing -> yes.

  1. A visible, ticking proof of what is at stake (Back to the Future's fading photograph).

Example: the McFly siblings fading from the photo is the film's running visual stake; it makes "the future is in danger / can be changed" legible with no dialogue. Source: 3dvf.com pieces (secondary, note the photo's well-known logic gap, which does not matter for us). Treat the "built the ticking clock around it" claim as fan-site level. For the doorstep: invert it. Maddie carries a battered family photo from the vault archive (a grandson, say) where a child is a grey silhouette or missing. She says only: "He hasn't been born yet. Out there he gets to be." Image of an empty space that can be filled = second chance.

  1. Show the grave, then reveal it is changeable (A Christmas Carol).

Example: the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge his neglected grave; he begs for another chance, wakes on Christmas morning, still alive, with time to make amends. Source: Wikipedia "Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come" (Dickens's exact "shadows of things that May be" wording was not confirmed in my results, so I am paraphrasing). For the doorstep: this is literally our structure, and the audience already half-knows it. Maddie does not need to say "second chance"; she shows the lady her own bunker: ration tins, 100 bunks, dust. Then a photo of the same people at a street party in the sunshine. "Which one do you want to be the draft?" is too clever; keep it silent: two images, side by side, the door between them.

  1. One-way messages from the people you are saving (Interstellar).

Example: Cooper watches decades of video messages from his family compressed into minutes; the last, from Murph, is the peak; the one-directional nature heightens helplessness. Sources: Montages Magazine essay; SparkNotes plot summary; Lantern/Nolan book entry. For the doorstep: the messages go the other way. Maddie plays a short voice or video note from "the earlier them", recovered from an archive: a kid's school recording, a wedding toast, a voicemail ("ring me when you're home"). Sketch: the lady hears her own younger voice say something mundane. Mundane beats grand: the point is that this person is still out there, still has Sundays.

  1. A prop that carries the argument, planted and paid off (Chekhov / plant and payoff).

Example: Back to the Future's camcorder and plutonium; GoldenEye's pen; the Signs glasses of water. Rule of thumb from NoFilmSchool and Final Draft: a highlighted object tells the audience it matters, and it must pay off. Sources: nofilmschool.com "What is Chekhov's gun", "9 times Chekhov's gun wasn't a gun", finaldraft.com. For the doorstep: the torn bunting. Established as Maddie's signature, it is also a party flag. She tears off a strip and hands it through the door gap: "Hold this. For the street party. Next time you get one." The lady keeps it in her hand when the door shuts. Payoff: scene E / the walk out, someone holding the same strip, or bunting whole and bright in the final bus shot. (My extrapolation; fits the repo note that bunting changes state across the film.)

  1. Accepting a known fate, shown as a gesture (Arrival).

Example: Louise, knowing the loss ahead, embraces the journey anyway; critics say the point is the acceptance, and the final scene is carried by tone and a look (one piece titled "one devastating glance"). Sources: Film Colossus; The Eagle; Cornell blog; Review Geek. No exact lines asserted. For the doorstep: the decision is a gesture, not a speech. The lady takes the strip of bunting, or puts a hand flat on the door, or simply opens it. Her deciding should be a physical act, not "all right, we'll do it". Sketch: lady looks at the photo, looks at the hundred behind her, takes her coat off the hook.

  1. Role reversal: the robot demonstrates vulnerability (my craft idea, no single source).

The usual rule that an actor doing something is more persuasive than describing it. A small, battered robot offering to go in first, or switching itself off at the door to show it is not a threat, buys trust faster than "believe me". For the doorstep: Maddie sets her own power cell on the step: "Hold that. If I'm lying, I'm not going anywhere." Four seconds; undercuts the "how are we supposed to believe you" beat with a physical answer.

  1. Let the lady supply the reframe by seeing it (gap and reveal).

Craft principle (general, not tied to one source): leave the key inference to the viewer so they feel they reached it. The viewer should arrive at "wait, they'd be alive" one beat before the lady says it. For the doorstep: cut to what Maddie shows rather than to Maddie speaking. Lady: "Back WHERE?" Maddie turns the screen round. A street, today's Swindon, a bus. Lady, flat: "...They've still got buses." That one dry line carries the whole turn, and it connects to scene E's bus.

Best three for GPOM

Judged against: very short, "second chance not sacrifice", warm robot and stern lady, nothing moralised.

  1. The photograph / swipe of the lady herself (techniques 1 and 3). Fastest route to "you would still be alive": the lady sees a living younger self, not an abstraction. Five seconds, no argument, no spoken moral, and it gives her something to react to. Kills the "you want us to kill ourselves" misreading because the image is of the person who survives.
  2. The torn bunting as the handed-over prop (technique 6). Costs the least screen time, pays off later (scene E), and makes Maddie's warmth physical. Stern lady taking it in her hand answers "do I trust you" without a speech, and a party flag frames the future as a second go at the party, not a death.
  3. The "they've still got buses" reveal (technique 9, with the screen of today). One image plus one dry line is exactly the lady's register and sells the turn: out there the world is not broken yet. It also seeds scene E on the bus. Pair with 1 for the shortest possible doorstep: show, one line, door shuts, she is holding the bunting.

Suggested skeleton (an idea, not a script): Lady refuses. Maddie, no pitch, turns the tablet round: young her. Lady: "Where's that?" Maddie: "When. You're still there." One-line pushback from the lady about the cost. Maddie puts the bunting in her hand. Door shuts with her still holding it.

Risks to check: a photo of the lady herself needs a plausible source (the boss's archives; the earlier version of her must exist in the film's canon); keep the images truthful so the Mad Men "selling a fiction" reading does not creep in; do not overload with three props, pick one image and one object.
