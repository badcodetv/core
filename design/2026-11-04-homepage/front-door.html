Eight ways in to the BadCode homepage

One page has to say what BadCode is in five seconds: it tells stories, it reads the news, and it is running for government.

  • Stories: four films and four songs
  • News bulletin: working name Money For Something
  • The BadCode Government: promises Future Proof

Ten researchers each searched the web a different way (joke parties, fake broadcasters, teletext, street posters, live counters, and so on). One merge turned their 30 ideas into these eight. Every picture on the right is a rough working toy: tap it, drag it, change the channel. Pick Build this, Borrow bits or Not this on each, leave a note, and tick any small ideas at the bottom you want whichever concept wins to steal. Figures shown are examples, not real numbers.

Year Zero / Year Now

The screen is split down the middle: the future that went wrong on the left, the building site where you fix it on the right. You drag the seam.

“Left is what happened. Right is what you do instead. Drag.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Saira Stencil on the ruined side, Barlow on the clean side, Permanent Marker for the taped notes
Strands
Three objects that exist in both timelines: a cinema screen (Stories), a TV (News), a podium (Government). Burnt out on the left, working on the right.
Motion
One rip of daylight on load. A Revert button snaps everything clean, the GPOM git revert as a gesture.
Steals
Nine Inch Nails, Year Zero, Gource, the Sator square
Risk
The clean side can tip into start-up optimism; it must stay handmade (tape, tea rings, marker). Keep the ruin small; GPOM's dark register is already overused.

The Wedge

The logo is already a 1919 poster: a red triangle against a ring. On load the triangle drives through the ring and breaks it into the three strands.

“We tried subtle. You were busy.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Big Shoulders Stencil for the slabs, Archivo Black for the ticker, plain sans on the Government slab
Strands
Stories and News are tilted slabs that clatter in like machine parts. The Government slab is the only flat, upright, official-looking one. That's the joke.
Motion
One violent move on load, then the slabs slide mechanically. Nothing soft.
Steals
El Lissitzky's Red Wedge, Rodchenko, Channel 4 idents, the GOV.UK form style
Risk
Red wedge on black reads as communist to a reader drifting right. Postbox red, no Soviet type, and the very British Government slab sets the tone.

X Marks the Spot

A ballot paper with three candidates. All of them are BadCode. Already phone-shaped, already British.

“Choose carefully. You didn't last time.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Libre Caslon small caps for candidates, Courier Prime for the descriptions, Caveat for pencil
Strands
Identical rows, told apart only by name, description and the emblem box. Future Proof opens as a lit ballot box.
Motion
The cursor is the pencil on a string. Somebody very fast fills in all three, a stamp says COUNTED, and the pencil is handed to you. After you vote: “You voted BadCode. So did everyone.”
Steals
Monster Raving Loony Party, Jón Gnarr's Best Party, The Juice Media
Risk
Electoral law: no real constituencies, no Electoral Commission wording, a printed “not a real election” line. Equal rows suit three strands today, less so if one grows.

The Plug

A scuffed extension lead on a prepayment meter. Plug a strand in and the room powers up as it.

“The future is on a meter. Plug something in.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Overpass heavy for moulded labels, a seven-segment face for the meter, Instrument Serif italics for deadpan captions
Strands
Black flex to a projector (Stories). Red flex to a studio monitor (News). Hi-vis flex to a building-site floodlight (Government): the only warm daylight.
Motion
A mains thunk and a bulb flicker. Pull the plug and the room goes dark. The meter ticks down as a joke and never blocks anything.
Steals
Bruno Simon's one-verb site, the usable workshop desk
Risk
The joke must aim at whoever set the tariff, never at the person on the meter. Lives or dies on art direction; a stock 3D model kills it.

The Hoarding

A boarded-up high-street shop. Someone else's promises are pasted on it; BadCode's three posters go up on top.

“Previously: someone else's promises.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Anton for the tabloid, Bodoni italic for the cinema poster, Rubik Mono One for the leaflet, typewriter for the small print
Strands
Each strand is a different kind of poster on different paper: a cinema one-sheet, a tabloid front page, a council-style party leaflet. Peel a corner to see the slogan it answers.
Motion
A paste roller, posters unrolling wet, the third one crooked, corners that peel.
Steals
Die PARTEI, the 1939 Ministry of Information trio, Barnbrook's Dismaland
Risk
Street-art grunge is the most copied look here; it needs real photographed paste and staples. Under-posters must be invented slogans, no real faces or party layouts.

Departures

A station departures board. Every service to the future is delayed, and BadCode knows why.

“All services to the future are delayed. We've seen the cause. It's not the leaves.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
A dot-matrix or flap face for the board, Barlow Condensed for platform labels, Public Sans inside
Strands
Platform 1 Stories, platform 2 News tallies, platform 3 the manifesto, struck through when the real cabinet contradicts it.
Motion
Hundreds of flaps clatter and settle. Stories clack slowly, News never stops, Government lands with one thunk. The station clock is the logo, with the triangle as the second hand.
Steals
splitflap-js, LAb[au] Signal to Noise, the debt clock
Risk
Small text, no room for the films' pictures. Rows must open into rich pages or it's a one-visit novelty.

P100

BadCode is the last teletext service still running. You get around by page number.

“P100. The only service still running. Ask yourself why.”

Colour, type, strands, sources, risk
Colour
Type
Bedstead (a free teletext font) for the real thing, VT323 here as a stand-in; Atkinson Hyperlegible for long reads
Strands
P200 Stories as a TV guide, P300 News headlines, P400 the manifesto with “Cabinet said / BadCode says”. P888, Future Proof, is the only page that breaks into daylight.
Motion
The page draws line by line. Subpages cycle. Flash is kept for the tally.
Steals
NMS Ceefax, Tim Potter's Ceefax, Craig Oldham on teletext
Risk
Skews over 40 and can read as nostalgia, not a warning. The films get no picture until a page opens. The research suggests it as a hidden page inside another concept.
