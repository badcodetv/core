Building site · open to the public

You're allowed to question the system you inherited.

The basic machine is older than the light bulb: pick someone, wait years, repeat. You get one choice between a few pre-packed bundles, and the whole government gets replaced in one go.

It feels fixed because it's the only system anyone alive remembers. But we build other complicated things differently now. Software had the same problems: changing safely, testing before shipping, never letting one piece carry everything. It found patterns that work.

Here are three of them, borrowed on purpose. They're probably wrong. Nobody ever fixed an empty page, but post a wrong answer and ten thousand experts turn up by morning. Treat these as the wrong answer, posted on purpose.

Idea one

Stop buying the bundle.

Today you pick a party and you get its whole package: views you hold, and views you don't, welded together.

Build things from small parts that fit together, so no single part has to carry everything. Programmers call it composition. Applied to politics, your view on housing doesn't have to come chained to your view on defence.

Set your own answers on the right. The two bundles are made up for this demo.

Idea two

Small changes, tested, all the time.

Software used to ship on a disc every few years. Now big companies ship changes thousands of times a day, safely, because every change is tested before it lands and undone if it breaks.

We still ship a whole government once every few years, then wait. Speed isn't the point. Testing is what makes changing things safe, and we finally have the tools to do it.

Idea three

No promise without its test.

In a good engineering team, nobody ships a change on someone's say-so. It ships with the measurement that will show whether it worked.

Today a politician can say "trust me", everyone hopes, and the failure is never even measured. Not everything can be tested. Diplomacy and judgement stay human work. But a huge amount of policy could be, and almost none of it is.

Billionaire Coin

Hold up to about a billion in real money. Past that, you compete for coins: you still get to win the status game. The real money goes to building hospitals.

Seed

Own what can't compete

Markets need competition to work. Where there can't be any, as with the water coming out of your tap, "the market" is just rent with extra steps. Natural monopolies go into public hands.

Where the work starts

Who should decide how the gains from automation are shared?

One rule to test, not a policy we've adopted: before an employer replaces jobs with automation, the people whose jobs are affected should have a binding say in how the gains are shared.

How would the gains be counted? Who does this rule still leave out? Shorter hours with no pay cut, profit sharing and public services are all options to compare. None of them is a promise.
