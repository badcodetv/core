# Research 01-reframe: one line flips "sacrifice" into "second chance"

Method note: 6 searches. Web results were thin on craft analysis; plot facts below are checked against the cited pages, and where an example rests on my own knowledge of the work it is marked (from memory). No quotes invented; paraphrase is flagged.

## Techniques

### 1. The "will be / may be" question (the conditional future)
- **Technique:** A character is shown a fixed-looking ending, then asks the one question that reopens it: is this set, or can it change? The reframe is that the doom was a warning, not a verdict.
- **Example:** A Christmas Carol, Stave Four. Scrooge, before he reads his own name on the gravestone, asks the Ghost whether these are shadows of things that *will* be or *may* be only. The Ghost does not answer aloud; Scrooge concludes it is "may". (Wording of the question confirmed by the sources; I paraphrase the rest.)
- **For the doorstep:** The old lady has the wrong tense. She hears "end", Maddie supplies "may". Sketch: Lady: "So we die." Maddie: "You die here. Back there you haven't done it yet. It's still *may be*." Better still, the lady asks the Scrooge question herself: "Is that already happened, or can it be changed?" and Maddie just says "Not happened yet, for them." The audience does the flip.

### 2. The mock-execution reprieve (the gift of the minute after)
- **Technique:** Make the character believe in loss, then show the loss was never the real offer. The shock of the pardon makes the remaining time feel like a gift.
- **Example:** Dostoevsky and the Petrashevsky Circle, 22 Dec 1849: they stood before a firing squad, the Tsar's pardon had already been issued and was read at the last moment. He later wrote the condemned man's feelings into Prince Myshkin's monologue in The Idiot. (Confirmed in sources.)
- **For the doorstep:** Not the plot, the feeling: the lady has braced to be asked to die for the boss. Maddie lets her have a second of that dread, then the turn: "Not for him. For you. The ones back then." The relief is the persuasion. Sketch: Lady: "You want us to die." Maddie: "...Aye, that bit's true. Sorry. But you wouldn't *stay* dead. You'd be the ones who get to do it again."

### 3. Loss-frame to gain-frame (cognitive reframing / loss aversion)
- **Technique:** People resist the same outcome when it is described as a loss and accept it as a gain. Therapists and advisers recast a setback as the opportunity inside it, without inventing facts that are not there.
- **Example:** The advice-to-sell-a-depreciated-stock case (selling recast as freed-up capital for a better use) in the loss-aversion piece at fa-mag.com; CBT "I failed" to "I made a mistake I can learn from" in the cognitive-reframing handouts. One source warns the reframe must rest on true facts. (Confirmed in sources.)
- **For the doorstep:** Never say "power" or "fuel" (the loss words). Use keep words: "go back", "get another go", "carry on". Maddie must only claim what is true in the story: they end here, their earlier selves live. The honesty is the charm. Sketch: Maddie: "You're not the battery. You're the ones holding the pen."

### 4. The "not for him, for you" ownership swap
- **Technique:** The beneficiary of the sacrifice is swapped. The audience (and the lady) assumed the boss gains; the reveal is that *they* do, in the person of their earlier selves, so the ask becomes a gift.
- **Example:** Terminator 2: John says the phrase "no fate but what we make" came from his father, who had him memorise it in the future as a message to Sarah; the doom future becomes a message to change things. The sender's sacrifice benefits the receivers. (Confirmed in sources; the Kyle Reese parallel is from memory.)
- **For the doorstep:** The boss is deliberately smaller than the ask. Sketch: Lady: "So your boss gets to live." Maddie: "He doesn't, love. He's the one who's wrong in that world. He just won't do it again." Or: "He gets nothing. Not a thing. It's you lot who get a go."

### 5. Anagnorisis in one word: "them" is "you"
- **Technique:** The recognition that the people being saved are the speaker's own: sacrifice for strangers becomes self-preservation.
- **Example:** Interstellar's tesseract ending: Cooper's data reaches Murph, so a sacrifice looks like a return and a reunion (summarised in sources; the "you are the ghost" recognition is from memory). Edge of Tomorrow is the same shape from the other side: each death is only a reset (general plot confirmed in reviews).
- **For the doorstep:** One beat where the lady sees it. Sketch: Maddie: "Everyone you ever loved, they're all still there. Back then. Mum. Your kids." Lady (quiet): "...Alive." Maddie: "Alive. Not yet gone wrong." One word from the lady is the whole turn.

### 6. The reluctant agreement by objection (the lady turns it herself)
- **Technique:** Let the sceptic discover the reframe by raising the harshest objection and hearing it answered. Persuasion reads as the character's own idea.
- **Example:** (From memory, not confirmed by search.) The common Doctor Who / Star Trek move where a sceptic says "so we do it all again?" and the answer "yes" lands as hope.
- **For the doorstep:** Make the lady say the line. Lady: "And they get to do it right this time, do they?" Maddie: "That's the idea." Lady: "...Well, bloody hell. Why didn't you lead with that?" Maddie: "I did try, I got a bit sidetracked."

### 7. The embarrassed-messenger undercut
- **Technique:** A reframe works best when the speaker believes it too little to oversell. Understatement sells hope better than a speech.
- **Example:** Groundhog Day / Edge of Tomorrow framing in reviews: the loop is a setup for hope because a character asks what they would do with another go. (Confirmed as commentary; "understated messenger" is my craft note.)
- **For the doorstep:** Maddie keeps the sentence small and slightly rude. Sketch: Maddie: "You get another go. At the whole thing. Properly. Without us getting in the way." Then, whispered, "Try not to fuck it up." (This keeps the existing last beat.)

### 8. The "no fate" handoff: give them the pen
- **Technique:** After the reframe, hand the audience's stand-in agency: the future is theirs to write, not given.
- **Example:** Sarah carves "No Fate" into a table in Terminator 2; critics read the ending as an open road, hopeful but uncertain. (Confirmed in sources.)
- **For the doorstep:** The lady accepts by taking agency, not by being told. Sketch: Lady: "And what do we do when we're back?" Maddie: "Dunno. That's the point. It's yours."

## Best three for GPOM

1. **#5 Anagnorisis in one word (the lady names it: "Alive").** Shortest by far; the turn is one word from the character, not an argument from the robot; stern lady gets to be moved without being preached at; no moral is stated. Fits the 30-45 s budget.
2. **#4 "Not for him, for you".** It directly deletes the "fuel for the boss" reading Kai wants gone, in one exchange, and keeps Maddie warm and self-deprecating about the boss. It also fixes the current line "so your boss can live".
3. **#2 The reprieve (hold the dread, then release it).** Gives the sharpest emotional beat for the money: let the lady's "so you want us to die" land, then reverse it. Pairs well with #5. Use only a two-line version, otherwise it drags.

Backups: #1 (the "may be" tense) as the lady's question if a stern-sceptic voice is needed; #3 as a vocabulary rule for every line (no "fuel/power", only "go back/another go").

Craft warning from the sources: a reframe only persuades if it rests on true facts. Maddie should say nothing false (the hundred do end here); the second chance belongs to their earlier selves.
