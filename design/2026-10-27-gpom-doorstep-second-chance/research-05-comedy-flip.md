# Research 05-comedy-flip: comedy as the delivery (how a joke lands a reframe faster than an argument)

Method note: 7 web searches. Sources confirmed premises and structure of the examples; none gave exact transcripts, so all quotes below are PARAPHRASED, not verbatim. Sketch lines are our own inventions.

## Techniques

1. **The literal-minded machine (wrong word, instant correction).** A robot or alien uses the wrong word for the thing, a human corrects it, and the correction IS the reframe. The audience gets the new meaning from the gap between the two words.
   - Example: Kryten in Red Dwarf ("Kryten", S2, 1988) keeps serving crew who have been dead for centuries, with no idea. His literal devotion carries the tragedy and the joke at once. Sources describe it as denial dressed up as programming.
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie keeps using the boss's word and the old lady keeps flinching at it.
     - Maddie: "He needs you to... power it."
     - Lady: "Power it. Like a battery."
     - Maddie: "Oh no, not like a battery. Batteries don't get to go again." (The correction swaps "fuel" for "second go" in one beat.)

2. **The bureaucratic alibi (disaster delivered as paperwork).** A catastrophe is announced in the flat language of admin. The mismatch between scale and tone is the joke, and it carries blame without a lecture.
   - Example: Hitchhiker's Guide, the Vogons demolish Earth and say the plans were on display for decades in a distant planning office. Arthur's house gets the same treatment first, with the plans in a locked cabinet in a disused lavatory (paraphrased from the novel, per Wikipedia and commentary).
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie reads the apology off a laminated card. The comedy is that it's inadequate, and it lets her admit "we killed everyone" without grovelling.
     - Maddie: "I have been told to say: 'We regret the extinction.' [flips card] There's a bit on the back about 'opportunities'. I'm skipping that."

3. **Bathos: the huge thing, then the small thing.** Build to the big word, then drop to something mundane. The drop is where the audience relaxes enough to accept the idea.
   - Example: Ghosts (BBC) is built on dead people being petty about rooms and housemates. Julian, the dead Tory MP, died with his trousers down (per Chortle/Wikipedia). The show's warmth comes from death being an inconvenience that people still love each other through. Farnaby has said the appeal is learning to value life.
   - **For the doorstep:** The lady asks the existential question and Maddie answers with logistics.
     - Lady: "And when we're gone?"
     - Maddie: "You'd wake up on a Tuesday. In your own bed. Bit confused. [beat] Nobody ever tells them about the Tuesday."

4. **The deadpan blunt answer to the question nobody dares ask.** The character says the awkward true thing plainly, so the other character has nothing to push against. Candour defuses suspicion faster than persuasion.
   - Example: Baldrick's outburst in Blackadder Goes Forth, "Goodbyeee", is the sitcom's rare moment where Blackadder has no quip. The series is mostly jokes, so the silence is the point (Wikipedia; Kicker newsletter).
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie has already used the blunt honesty ("you can't prove it, I'm a robot"). Push it one line further, where the lady asks the real question and Maddie answers it in four words.
     - Lady: "Is it going to hurt?"
     - Maddie: "I'd be lying if I said I knew. [beat] But you'll be someone else's problem by teatime. Sorry. Someone else's *miracle*."

5. **Reversal by tiny edit (change one word, change the deal).** Take the lady's own phrase back and alter one word. The audience hears both versions and feels the flip without it being explained.
   - Example: the incongruity-resolution account of humour (Koestler's frame clash, summarised by Big Think and others) says a joke works when a second frame collides with the first and the listener resolves it. A one-word edit is the cheapest way to cause the collision.
   - **For the doorstep:**
     - Lady: "You want us to die for him."
     - Maddie: "I want you to *clock off*. [beat] And clock on again. Back when it was still fixable."
   - Or the lady's "sacrifice" returns as "a do-over, love."

6. **Rule of three with the third item breaking the pattern.** Two items set the line, the third cuts across it (Vorhaus, via No Film School; TV Tropes "Rule of Three").
   - Example: the standard comic list gag.
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie lists what the old lady loses, then the real ask.
     - Maddie: "You'd lose the bunker. The tinned peaches. [beat] The slightly damp feeling of being the last of everything."
   - The third item is the lady's actual burden, so the joke names her grief gently. Keep it to three beats.

7. **Death as a polite colleague (take the awe out of it).** Treat the unspeakable thing as a routine professional matter, so fear has nowhere to stand.
   - Example: Pratchett's Death (all-capitals speech, dry and bureaucratic). Commentary argues his persona keeps mortality from turning bleak (secondary sources, some weak; treat as supported but not authoritative).
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie treats the thing as ordinary admin.
     - Maddie: "It's a quick form. Name, next of kin, and 'would you like to go again.' Most people say yes."

8. **The bit of business that does the explaining (visual gag, no words).** Wallace & Gromit and Pixar's WALL-E lean on silent business. Stanton has said he sees WALL-E's silence as showing rather than telling, and he talks about "2+2 not 4": give the audience two pieces and let them add.
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie never says "second chance". She holds up something that is the second chance.
     - Maddie pulls a wilted birthday card from her bunting: a card for a grandchild who is, in the other timeline, still alive.
   - The lady reads the name and goes quiet. Props can carry the turn that a speech would labour.

9. **Embarrassment as honesty (the robot is bad at this job).** The messenger is visibly out of her depth and says so. Awkwardness reads as sincerity and shortens everything, because nobody argues with someone who is clearly not selling.
   - Example: The Ghosts and Blackadder warmth points above. The same mechanism: characters fumble their way to love.
   - **For the doorstep:** Maddie tries the grand speech, abandons it, says the plain version.
     - Maddie: "I had a whole thing. It was good. [beat] Right: you get to go again. With the good bit of the map this time."

## Best three for GPOM

1. **Technique 1 (the literal-minded word swap: "fuel" becomes "go again").** It does the exact job Kai asked for: the lady's word "kill ourselves / fuel" is gently replaced by Maddie's, and the audience feels the narrative reset in a single exchange. It is short (two lines), keeps Maddie warm and slightly daft, lets the lady stay stern, and states no moral.
2. **Technique 3 (bathos, the Tuesday).** One mundane image ("you'd wake up on a Tuesday") makes the second chance concrete and survivable faster than any explanation of time travel. It plays Maddie's embarrassed charm and gives the lady something small and human to react to, with the reframe done by image, not argument.
3. **Technique 8 (the prop that does the explaining).** The shortest of all, because the turn happens without dialogue. It suits a film, and the "no moral spoken" rule fits perfectly. Risk: it needs a shot to be built (a card or object), so check with scene-images. Runner-up: technique 9 for tone only, as the wrapper around whichever of the above is chosen.

Caution common to all: a joke that mocks the lady's grief breaks the reader rule (never talk down to the viewer). Aim the jokes at Maddie's incompetence and the boss's bureaucracy, never at the dead.
