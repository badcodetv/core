# Research 04-persuasion-psychology: persuading a hostile listener who distrusts the messenger

Evidence notes: effect sizes here are modest and several classic compliance effects replicate shakily; they are craft levers for a 40-second scene, not guarantees. Anything I could not verify is marked.

## Techniques

1. **Reframe the loss as a gain (gain/loss framing).** The same outcome is judged differently depending on whether it is described as what you lose or what you keep. Example: Tversky and Kahneman 1981 "Asian disease" problem (from memory; not re-verified this session). Also Aronson-style evidence is not needed: the point is that "we need you as fuel" is a loss frame, "you get to go back and do it again" is a gain frame.
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie never says "power", "fuel", "die". She leads with what they get. Sketch: Maddie: "It sends you back. Not the bodies, but the you. The one who still has a go at it." Lady: "...Back to when?" (the lady asks for the detail herself).

2. **Let the listener say it (elicitation / self-persuasion; motivational interviewing "evoke").** MI practitioners draw out the person's own reasons ("change talk") and avoid the "righting reflex" of telling people what to do; elicit-provide-elicit: ask, give one piece of information, ask what they make of it. Source: Miller and Rollnick, summarised in MI pocket guides (secondary sources only; I did not see the primary text).
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie gives one fact, then asks, and the old lady states the conclusion. Sketch: Maddie: "The earlier you's still walking about. Hasn't done any of it yet." Lady: "...So she could stop it." Maddie: "I didn't say that." (beat). The lady's line IS the pitch; the turn is hers.

3. **Deep canvassing: story swap instead of argument.** Broockman and Kalla (Science 2016, transgender prejudice, Miami) and Kalla and Broockman (APSR 2020, three field experiments, 6,869 voters): a non-judgmental exchange of personal narratives produced lasting change (at least 3-4 months, small effects, d around 0.08 in 2020); the same conversation with arguments alone did not. Canvassers ask the person to recall a time they were treated unfairly or needed a second chance, then share their own story. Sources below.
   **For the doorstep:** Replace argument with one human question. Sketch: Maddie: "Anyone down there got something they'd do different?" Lady (after a long beat, looking back into the bunker): "...Every one of us." Their regret does the persuading, not Maddie.

4. **Admit self-interest and limits (pratfall effect; the "I can't prove it" move).** Aronson 1966: a competent person who makes a small blunder becomes MORE likable (the effect reverses for mediocre people). Maddie is already battered, with torn bunting, and the current draft already uses "you can't believe me". Use it harder: confess the boss's selfishness out loud.
   **For the doorstep:** Sketch: Maddie: "Course he wants something. He wants you back. Bit late, I know." This pre-empts "what's in it for him" and answers it with the one want the audience already knows is sad and true.

5. **"But you are free" (autonomy affirmation).** Adding "but you are free to refuse" after a request raises compliance. Carpenter 2013 meta-analysis of 42 studies found it effective; a 2020 pre-registered re-analysis (Fillon et al., Meta-Psychology; 52 experiments) found g = 0.44 overall but about 0.11 (non-significant) in the seven lowest-bias studies. So: weak, cheap, use as texture, not as the engine.
   **For the doorstep:** Sketch: Maddie: "Nobody's making you. I couldn't if I tried. I've got bunting." It also fits the existing "come back tomorrow" and lowers reactance (hostile listeners push back on pressure).

6. **Hypocrisy induction / making the listener advocate (Aronson, Fried and Stone 1991).** Prompting people to argue for a value and recalling where they fell short produced stronger intent to change than lectures. The old lady is a leader of survivors who raised them on "never again" or "somebody should have done something".
   **For the doorstep:** Sketch: Maddie: "You lot have been down here five years going 'if only somebody had stopped it'." Lady: "..." Maddie: "Well." Her own refrain becomes the offer. One line, no lecture.

7. **Disrupt-then-reframe (DTR).** Davis and Knowles 1999: a slightly odd phrase in the pitch ("300 pennies") followed at once by a reframe ("that's a bargain") roughly doubled sales in the original card-selling study (reported about 40% to 80%; another account gives lower baselines; 14 studies by 2009). It works by disrupting the listener's automatic refusal script.
   **For the doorstep:** The old lady's refusal script is "you want us to kill ourselves". Disrupt it with an unexpected small, funny line, then reframe. Sketch: Lady: "So you want us to kill ourselves." Maddie: "Ooh, no, nothing like. [beat] Want you to turn up for work. Early. In the past." Reads as a laugh, then the reframe.

8. **Small ask first: foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face.** Freedman and Fraser 1966 (small sign, then big sign); Cialdini et al. 1975 (refused big ask, then agreed to small). Replication of FITD is patchy; DITF has meta-analytic support but needs the same person making both requests.
   **For the doorstep:** The film already has this: Maddie asks for tomorrow, not forever. Sharper: Maddie opens with the big ask ("the lot of you, all in") and instantly retreats to "just come and look at the thing". The old lady agreeing to walk out (scene C) is the small yes. Use it as structure, not as dialogue.

9. **Sincere messenger doubt turned into a test the listener sets.** Distrust is lowered when the source hands over control of verification. (No single named study found this session; this is a synthesis from MI and autonomy research, so treat as craft, not evidence.)
   **For the doorstep:** Sketch: Lady: "Why should we believe you?" Maddie: "Don't. Ask me something only a Swindon bin lorry would know." One quick, charming proof of character instead of claims; or Maddie hands over the one thing she has (her bunting, her route) as a hostage.

## Best three for GPOM

1. **Technique 2, let the lady say it (with 1, the gain frame as the single fact).** It makes the turn "a second chance, not a sacrifice" belong to the old lady, so the film states no moral and Maddie never lectures. It is two exchanges long. Strongest fit for "no moral spoken aloud".
2. **Technique 3, one regret question (deep canvassing in miniature).** The one question "has anyone down there got something they'd do different?" gets the hundred's regret on screen in a few seconds and is the best-evidenced technique here (replicated field experiments). It also fits a stern leader speaking for a group.
3. **Technique 4, the pratfall confession ("course he wants something; he wants you back").** Costs one line, uses Maddie's battered warmth as the credibility engine, and pre-empts the audience's own "what's in it for him" without a long reassurance. Technique 5 ("nobody's making you") can ride along for free as a closing half-line.

Note on the audience (the-reader.md): for an impatient viewer the lady stating the stakes herself is also less preachy than any speech from the robot.
