# Research 03-compressed-persuasion: how short scenes turn a distrustful "no" into an enormous "yes"

## Techniques

1. **Offer the thing they secretly want, not the thing you need.** The pitch is about the listener's lost life, not the pitcher's project.
   Example: Inception (2010), Saito's pitch to Cobb: do this job and he can make a phone call that lets Cobb go home to his children. The job is almost an afterthought. [memory, paraphrase]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie never says "power the machine". She says what they get back: the street, a kettle, a neighbour who is still alive. The machine arrives last. Sketch: Maddie: "Margaret, how long since you had a cup of tea with someone who's still alive?" ... Lady: "..." Maddie: "That's what I'm offering. Sorry about the paperwork."

2. **Let the listener say the thing.** The persuader leaves the gap; the persuaded fills it, which makes the conclusion theirs and saves a speech.
   Example: Chris Voss's "calibrated questions" ("how am I supposed to do that?") hand the problem back so the other side supplies the answer. [checked: secondary summaries only, e.g. MasterClass; the principle is negotiation craft, not film]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie asks one question and stops. Maddie: "If you'd known, back then, what you know now. What would you have done?" Lady (slow): "Everything. Differently." Maddie: "Yeah." Beat. That line does the whole "second chance" job and the old lady said it.

3. **Reframe the cost as a trade the audience can see at a glance (two futures, one choice).**
   Example: Inception again: Saito frames the choice as leaping or growing old waiting to die alone (close paraphrase, [memory]). Shawshank's "get busy living or get busy dying" is the same shape. [memory]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie: "Down here you've got forty years in a bunker and then nobody. Up there you've got the whole of it again. Twice, if you count the bit where it works out." Keep it to one line; the lady's flinch is the acceptance.

4. **Show, don't argue: let the proof be a thing in the listener's hand.**
   Example: Terminator 2 (1991): the T-800 gets Miles Dyson to stop not by arguing but by showing him the arm from the first Terminator and what the chip leads to; Dyson's reaction is horror and "what have we done", then agreement. Sources: Wikipedia/Wikiquote summaries confirm the sequence (T-800 shows consequences, Dyson agrees to destroy the research). [checked for the beat, wording paraphrased]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie has one object: a photo, a child's drawing, the lady's own old wedding ring from the surface, or a wristwatch showing the date it is *back then*. She doesn't explain it. She holds it out. Lady takes it. That replaces the "you can't prove it, I'm a robot" exchange with something tangible. Sketch: Maddie: "He kept this." Lady turns it over. It is her own street, dated.

5. **Concede the objection before they voice it (disarm by agreeing).** The persuader says the lady's worst line for her, so there is nothing to fight.
   Example: Casablanca (1942), the airport: Rick takes Ilsa's objection and names the regret she will feel, then turns it into a reason to go ("maybe not today... but soon and for the rest of your life" is the commonly quoted shape; paraphrase). Short, no counterargument, the leaving *is* the persuasion. [memory]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie: "You're right. We killed everyone. I'd shut the door on me." Then, no pause for rebuttal: "So don't do it for us." Lady: "Then who for?" Maddie: "The ones up there. They haven't done it yet." The lady's question is the turn.

6. **Ask for something small first; the enormous thing is already implied.**
   Example: the classic foot-in-the-door shape; film version: Obi-Wan only asks Luke to come to Alderaan, not to join a rebellion (Star Wars, 1977) [memory]. 
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie only asks for a look, not a yes. "Walk with me to the building. See it. Then say no." That is scene C's walk-out, and it makes the doorstep shorter because it no longer needs to win the whole argument. The lady's "come back tomorrow" becomes "I'll look. Just look."

7. **The unexpected speaker: the persuasion comes from one of their own, not the outsider.**
   Example: Darkest Hour (2017) tube scene (invented for the film, a famous example of fiction beating fact): Churchill asks ordinary passengers and their answer persuades him, not the other way round. [memory]
   **For the doorstep:** Someone inside the bunker (a child, a very old man) says the line, not Maddie. The old lady turns round and sees the hundred faces already nodding. Sketch: Boy (off): "Is it true we'd get to do it again?" Silence. The lady looks at Maddie. Maddie: "...Yeah." Zero extra words from the robot.

8. **Make the accepted reframing a single re-spelled word.** The audience hears "fuel/sacrifice" and the persuader swaps one noun: "not fuel, a message", "not dying, going first."
   Example: Arrival (2016) turns on whether you would take a life you know the end of; the film's engine is a re-seen choice, not a re-explained one. [memory, loose]
   **For the doorstep:** Lady: "So you want us to die." Maddie: "No. I want you to *go back*. All of you. Younger." The word "back" does the work. Keep "die" for the audience to notice, never for Maddie to say.

9. **Warm humour as the pressure release right before the yes.** A joke from the persuader lowers the temperature so the persuaded can change their mind without losing face.
   Example: Doctor Who, "The Doctor Dances" (2005), "Everybody lives, Rose!" works because the whole episode earned a joyful reversal; the Doctor's delight is the persuasion for the viewer. Related: the Ninth Doctor in "The Parting of the Ways" retrospectives are noted for plain, jargon-free emotional beats. [checked at a summary level, wording of the first line from memory]
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie, embarrassed: "He wrote a bit on a Post-it. 'This time we won't mess it up.' Bit stronger than that, actually." The lady's mouth twitches. That twitch is the yes.

10. **The persuaded character turns the argument into logistics.** Once someone asks "how many, when, who goes first", they have already decided.
    Example: Ocean's Eleven (2001) recruitment: after Danny's pitch, each recruit's first question is practical (what's the split, what's the job), reviews describe the sequence as a set of quick introductions rather than debates. [checked at review level]
    **For the doorstep:** Lady (rushed, annoyed): "How long do we get?" Maddie: "Till tea." Lady: "...Fetch my coat." Cut. She has agreed without ever saying yes.

## Best three for GPOM

Judged against: very short; the turn is "a second chance, not a sacrifice"; warm robot / stern old lady; no stated moral.

1. **Technique 2 (let the listener say it) with technique 5 (concede first).** Maddie takes the hit ("I'd shut the door on me"), then asks one question: what would you have done differently, knowing what you know? The old lady answers it herself and the second chance is *hers*, not Maddie's pitch. It is about 15 seconds, spoken morals are replaced by a question, and it matches the brief that Maddie never lectures.
2. **Technique 4 (an object in the hand).** Replaces "you can't prove it, I'm a robot" with a tangible: a dated photo, a watch showing the earlier date. One image does what the simulation speech did, and the audience understands "back then, still alive" without a word. Pair with a single line from Maddie.
3. **Technique 7 (one of the hundred says it).** A child or the oldest man asks "do we get to do it again?" and the lady looks at the hundred. The decision for the group is made by the group, solving the "I can't decide for a hundred on a doorstep" beat, and Maddie's whole contribution is one word. Strongest on warmth and shortest on screen time.

Combined shape (about 35 s): lady's refusal -> Maddie concedes -> Maddie asks the one question -> lady answers it -> an object or a voice from behind her -> lady: "Fetch my coat." The reframe (technique 8: "back", not "die") sits in one Maddie line.
