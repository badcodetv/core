# Research 07-show-dont-argue: let an image do the persuading on the doorstep

Angle: visual and non-verbal devices that land "you'd be back then, alive, with a chance" in seconds, so the dialogue can stay at a handful of lines. Web evidence is secondary (criticism, Wikipedia, essays); no exact quotes are asserted beyond what is marked. Where an idea is my own craft extrapolation rather than from a source, it says so.

## Techniques

1. **Compress a life into a wordless sequence (the Up montage).**
   Example: the opening of Pixar's *Up* (2009) covers a whole marriage in roughly four minutes with almost no dialogue; Docter has said every element sets something up or pays something off, and the first cue Giacchino worked on was "Married Life". Sources: Wikipedia "Up opening sequence"; Far Out and Pixar Planet pieces.
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie holds up a tablet/old phone and swipes through a few fast photos of the lady herself, young, at a kitchen table, at a school gate, on a beach. No explanation. "That's you. Before. She's still there." Sketch: three swipes, a beat on the last one, the lady's hand comes up to the screen. Five seconds, replaces a whole speech.

2. **The pitch that sells a feeling via projected images (Mad Men, "The Wheel").**
   Example: Don Draper dims the lights and clicks through family slides on the Kodak carousel, calling it a time machine and nostalgia, "the pain from an old wound" (the show's own gloss; paraphrased). The persuasion is the slides, not the argument; a close-up on his face against the images on the wall does the work. Sources: Wikipedia "The Wheel (Mad Men)"; Lawyers, Guns & Money essay; Medium analysis. Note critics read the slides as partly fictional; here the images must be true to avoid that trap.
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie has a small chest projector (delivery robots carry stuff; give her a lamp) and throws the images on the bunker's concrete wall. The old lady's own face is lit by them. Sketch: Maddie: "Found these in the archive." Lady: (silent). Maddie: "That's the one that gets to go back."

3. **A visible, ticking proof of what is at stake (Back to the Future's fading photograph).**
   Example: the McFly siblings fading from the photo is the film's running visual stake; it makes "the future is in danger / can be changed" legible with no dialogue. Source: 3dvf.com pieces (secondary, note the photo's well-known logic gap, which does not matter for us). Treat the "built the ticking clock around it" claim as fan-site level.
   **For the doorstep:** invert it. Maddie carries a battered family photo from the vault archive (a grandson, say) where a child is a grey silhouette or missing. She says only: "He hasn't been born yet. Out there he gets to be." Image of an empty space that can be filled = second chance.

4. **Show the grave, then reveal it is changeable (A Christmas Carol).**
   Example: the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge his neglected grave; he begs for another chance, wakes on Christmas morning, still alive, with time to make amends. Source: Wikipedia "Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come" (Dickens's exact "shadows of things that May be" wording was not confirmed in my results, so I am paraphrasing).
   **For the doorstep:** this is literally our structure, and the audience already half-knows it. Maddie does not need to say "second chance"; she shows the lady her own bunker: ration tins, 100 bunks, dust. Then a photo of the same people at a street party in the sunshine. "Which one do you want to be the draft?" is too clever; keep it silent: two images, side by side, the door between them.

5. **One-way messages from the people you are saving (Interstellar).**
   Example: Cooper watches decades of video messages from his family compressed into minutes; the last, from Murph, is the peak; the one-directional nature heightens helplessness. Sources: Montages Magazine essay; SparkNotes plot summary; Lantern/Nolan book entry.
   **For the doorstep:** the messages go the other way. Maddie plays a short voice or video note from "the earlier them", recovered from an archive: a kid's school recording, a wedding toast, a voicemail ("ring me when you're home"). Sketch: the lady hears her own younger voice say something mundane. Mundane beats grand: the point is that this person is still out there, still has Sundays.

6. **A prop that carries the argument, planted and paid off (Chekhov / plant and payoff).**
   Example: Back to the Future's camcorder and plutonium; GoldenEye's pen; the Signs glasses of water. Rule of thumb from NoFilmSchool and Final Draft: a highlighted object tells the audience it matters, and it must pay off. Sources: nofilmschool.com "What is Chekhov's gun", "9 times Chekhov's gun wasn't a gun", finaldraft.com.
   **For the doorstep:** the torn bunting. Established as Maddie's signature, it is also a party flag. She tears off a strip and hands it through the door gap: "Hold this. For the street party. Next time you get one." The lady keeps it in her hand when the door shuts. Payoff: scene E / the walk out, someone holding the same strip, or bunting whole and bright in the final bus shot. (My extrapolation; fits the repo note that bunting changes state across the film.)

7. **Accepting a known fate, shown as a gesture (Arrival).**
   Example: Louise, knowing the loss ahead, embraces the journey anyway; critics say the point is the acceptance, and the final scene is carried by tone and a look (one piece titled "one devastating glance"). Sources: Film Colossus; The Eagle; Cornell blog; Review Geek. No exact lines asserted.
   **For the doorstep:** the decision is a gesture, not a speech. The lady takes the strip of bunting, or puts a hand flat on the door, or simply opens it. Her deciding should be a physical act, not "all right, we'll do it". Sketch: lady looks at the photo, looks at the hundred behind her, takes her coat off the hook.

8. **Role reversal: the robot demonstrates vulnerability (my craft idea, no single source).**
   The usual rule that an actor doing something is more persuasive than describing it. A small, battered robot offering to go in first, or switching itself off at the door to show it is not a threat, buys trust faster than "believe me".
   **For the doorstep:** Maddie sets her own power cell on the step: "Hold that. If I'm lying, I'm not going anywhere." Four seconds; undercuts the "how are we supposed to believe you" beat with a physical answer.

9. **Let the lady supply the reframe by seeing it (gap and reveal).**
   Craft principle (general, not tied to one source): leave the key inference to the viewer so they feel they reached it. The viewer should arrive at "wait, they'd be alive" one beat before the lady says it.
   **For the doorstep:** cut to what Maddie shows rather than to Maddie speaking. Lady: "Back WHERE?" Maddie turns the screen round. A street, today's Swindon, a bus. Lady, flat: "...They've still got buses." That one dry line carries the whole turn, and it connects to scene E's bus.

## Best three for GPOM

Judged against: very short, "second chance not sacrifice", warm robot and stern lady, nothing moralised.

1. **The photograph / swipe of the lady herself (techniques 1 and 3).** Fastest route to "you would still be alive": the lady sees a living younger self, not an abstraction. Five seconds, no argument, no spoken moral, and it gives her something to react to. Kills the "you want us to kill ourselves" misreading because the image is of the person who survives.
2. **The torn bunting as the handed-over prop (technique 6).** Costs the least screen time, pays off later (scene E), and makes Maddie's warmth physical. Stern lady taking it in her hand answers "do I trust you" without a speech, and a party flag frames the future as a second go at the party, not a death.
3. **The "they've still got buses" reveal (technique 9, with the screen of today).** One image plus one dry line is exactly the lady's register and sells the turn: out there the world is not broken yet. It also seeds scene E on the bus. Pair with 1 for the shortest possible doorstep: show, one line, door shuts, she is holding the bunting.

Suggested skeleton (an idea, not a script): Lady refuses. Maddie, no pitch, turns the tablet round: young her. Lady: "Where's that?" Maddie: "When. You're still there." One-line pushback from the lady about the cost. Maddie puts the bunting in her hand. Door shuts with her still holding it.

Risks to check: a photo of the lady herself needs a plausible source (the boss's archives; the earlier version of her must exist in the film's canon); keep the images truthful so the Mad Men "selling a fiction" reading does not creep in; do not overload with three props, pick one image and one object.
