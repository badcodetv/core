# Chancellor: two judgement calls (AI profits, AI copyright)

2026-11-05. Draft for ruling 4 in `../../README.md` §8 (RULED: option A, so the Chancellor gets
labelled judgement calls on where AI profits earned here are taxed and on AI and copyright). Strategy
and draft copy, not a ruling. Nothing in `docs/government/` is touched; this lands through the
section 9 route like the other pages.

**Form** (standing amendment 2): each call is labelled *Judgement call* and publishes its
principle, its review date and the condition that would change our mind, before anything happens.
It is not trialled, because a tax treaty or a copyright rule can't be handed out by lot. It still
carries a number where one exists, a Ledger row, and "Parliament votes" in place of "the data says".

**Grades:** [H] primary or several agreeing reputable sources; [M] one reputable secondary source or
secondaries that agree without the primary seen; [L] single weak source; [K] background knowledge, not
re-checked this session; **[K, check]** fairly sure, must be sourced before public use. Ten web
searches were run; the sources are listed at the end. **The fact gate holds:** nothing marked [M], [L]
or [K] goes on a public page until the primary is found.

## 1. Judgement call A: where AI profits earned here are taxed

### The call (contract form)

**Judgement call.** Labelled, because a tax treaty is negotiated, not trialled.

- **Principle.** Profit is taxed where the customers are and the work is done, not where the lawyer
  files it. We keep the 2% Digital Services Tax and we don't trade it for tariff relief. We back the
  15% global minimum and keep our domestic top-up tax applying to every group, US-owned included.
- **The rule, with the existing law named.** The DST exists (Finance Act 2020) [K, check]; we keep its
  rate and scope this parliament. The domestic top-up tax exists (Finance (No. 2) Act 2023) [K, check];
  we keep it on US groups under the side-by-side deal.
- **The Ledger row (the Chancellor's own instrument).** Every Budget publishes, in one line: what AI
  and big-tech groups take from UK users, what they pay here in DST and corporation tax, and the gap.
  From HMRC totals and published UK accounts; no firm's tax return is disclosed.
- **Review date.** Every Budget, in public. First full review at the first fiscal event, and again at
  the OECD's 2029 stocktake of the side-by-side deal.
- **What would change our mind.**
  1. *Drop the 2%:* a world deal in force that taxes those profits here (Pillar One Amount A or its
     successor). The DST was always the stopgap for that deal; we keep the promise to drop it.
  2. *Widen the 2% to AI services:* if the first review shows AI services' UK revenue is large and
     mostly booked abroad, we bring a widening to Parliament, scored by the OBR, with its own stop
     date. Not promised now. (The threshold is set at the first review and published in advance;
     we don't invent one here.)
  3. *Defend it less hard:* if the US carries out a tariff on British goods tied to the DST, we publish
     the jobs and pounds that tariff costs next to what the 2% raises, and Parliament votes on the
     trade-off in the open. We don't swap it away in a room.
- **Who it leaves out.** AI profit booked abroad on services the 2% doesn't cover. Counted on its own
  line in the Ledger, so the gap has an address even before it has a tax.
- **Who signs.** The Chancellor.

### The reasoning

- **Why not a new AI tax now.** No precedent to compare against, a live tariff threat, and the
  Chancellor already refuses a robot tax. A new tax with no test breaks the brand; publishing the gap
  first is cheap (Small band), and makes the later argument with a number instead of a slogan.
- **Why keep the DST against the threat.** It raised under a billion pounds last year [M], which is
  real money and also small; the honest case is not the yield but the principle that revenue earned
  here is taxable here. Dropping it for a tariff carve-out would be the bad branch's move in
  miniature: the bill for a shock abroad posted to people at home, the saving to the platform.
- **Why condition 3 is on the page.** The opposition's best attack is "they'll start a trade war that
  costs jobs at Jaguar Land Rover for £944m". The answer is not bravado; it is the comparison,
  published, and a vote. That is "Tested. Undoable." applied to a judgement call.
- **Why the Ledger row is the heart of it.** "Every pound has an address" is the Chancellor's line. The
  2% and the 15% are existing law; the new thing BadCode adds is printing the address of the money
  that escapes both.
- **Interfaces.** Foreign owns the trade negotiation and uses this wording unchanged. The Public Slice
  stays the answer where public money builds the machine; this call answers the profit the slice
  can't reach (wonk finding 6). It fills the Chancellor page's last line ("we say where those
  profits are taxed").

### The reader test

- *The reader already believes* big tech pays less tax than the chippy. Half true. The page must not
  confirm the false half: the line is "pays 2% on what it takes from you, and tax on profit it books
  here", not "pays nothing".
- *The chooser is named:* the US administration threatening tariffs and the Treasury that negotiates.
  Never "globalisation" or "AI".
- *The fear is aimed* (guardrail 6): tariffs on British jobs are named as a cost, and the answer is a
  published sum and a vote, not "don't worry".
- *Risk:* "a tax gap table" is wonk furniture. Keep it to one sentence on the page; the table lives in
  the Ledger.

### Page-ready text (Chancellor's voice, renderer-safe plain paragraph)

> Where the profits go. A tech giant pays 2% on what it takes from British users, and tax on the
> profit it books here, and we keep both: not traded away for a tariff threat, not dropped until a
> world deal taxes that profit here properly. Every Budget prints what these firms take from you and
> what they pay, gap included, and if a tariff ever costs British jobs more than the tax raises, we
> print that sum too and Parliament decides. That's a judgement call, looked at again every Budget.

Replacement for the current last line of the Chancellor page ("We count it anyway, and we say where
those profits are taxed"): *Who it leaves out: AI profit booked abroad, with nothing here to slice.
It gets its own line in every Budget, so the gap has an address before it has a tax.*

## 2. Judgement call B: AI and copyright

### The call (contract form)

**Judgement call.** Labelled, because a copyright rule binds the whole market at once and can't be
trialled in one town.

- **Principle.** Whose work taught the machine gets asked and gets paid. No opt-out exception: the
  law that says "ask first" stays. And anything sold here has to say what it learned from, because a
  right you can't check is a right you don't have.
- **The rule, with the existing law named.** The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 already
  requires a licence to copy work for commercial training here; the s29A exception is for
  non-commercial research only [M]. We keep that, and we rule out the opt-out the last government
  preferred and then dropped. **New:** a duty on any firm offering a general AI model in the UK to
  publish a summary of what it was trained on (finishing the transparency work the Data (Use and
  Access) Act 2025 debates left undone). **Agreed with the government's March 2026 report:** no
  copyright for a work with no human author (repeal s9(3) CDPA).
- **Review date.** One year after taking office, and within three months of the Court of Appeal's
  judgment in Getty v Stability AI, whichever comes first.
- **What would change our mind.**
  1. *Go further:* if, two years in, licensing income reaching UK creators (as reported by the
     collecting societies: PRS, ALCS, DACS, PPL) has not risen by at least a quarter (provisional),
     we bring in a payment every firm selling AI models here makes into collective licensing, the
     way a pub pays for the radio. Statutory remuneration, scored by the OBR, with a stop date.
  2. *Pull back:* if the transparency duty, on the published review, leaves British users and firms
     unable to buy the leading models, we publish that finding and the change it implies. Stated in
     advance so nobody can claim we hid it.
- **Who it leaves out.** Freelancers and session players paid nothing even when their label or
  publisher signs a licence. Counted on their own line: the review reports what reached individual
  creators, not just rights-holding companies.
- **Who signs.** The Chancellor, with the Culture Secretary's countersignature (copyright is DCMS and
  the IPO in practice; it sits with the Chancellor because it is a fight over who collects the rent).
- **The disclosure, on the page itself.** BadCode makes its work with these models (Google's Nano
  Banana and Veo, Suno). The page says so, bare, in its own sentence (`docs/using-ai.md` §7: the
  confession and the reason never share a sentence).

### The reasoning

- **Why this is the "who gets the gains" fight in its purest form** (wonk §7.1): the value in a
  model is, in part, everybody's work, and the gain goes to whoever owns the model. That is arc row 2
  (the saving goes up) with the receipts attached.
- **Why transparency, not a new right.** The law already says ask first. What fails is seeing who
  didn't. 81% of consultation responses wanted licensing in every case and over 90% wanted disclosure
  [M]; the opt-out had 3% [M]. We stand with the consensus, and the one thing we add is the check.
- **Why the s9(3) repeal is in, though it costs us.** Our renders would get no copyright of their own.
  We already say the rendering isn't ours (`docs/using-ai.md` §1, §2 Q3: we win authorship of the
  writing and the direction, never the drawing). Backing the repeal is the position made true, not
  clever. It is also the strongest answer to "hypocrites": we propose the rule that costs us.
- **Why not "AI training is theft".** It isn't settled in law: the UK High Court found the finished
  model is not a copy, and US courts have split [H for the UK judgment; `docs/using-ai.md` §4 for the
  US]. The honest sentence is *legally unresolved, ethically indefensible on consent*. The page says
  "nobody was asked", which is true, and leaves "theft" to the comic.
- **The reach problem, admitted.** Most training happens abroad, where UK copyright doesn't bite. The
  transparency duty attaches to *selling here*, which is the only lever a mid-sized market has. It is
  the weakest link and the review is built round it (condition 2).
- **What we never write here:** "ethically sourced" (none of our tools supports it), "AI democratises
  art", "we can't afford to commission", "AI is inevitable" (`docs/using-ai.md` §5.5, §7).

### The reader test

- *The reader's first AI question* is "who's coming for my job", not "who painted this"
  (`docs/using-ai.md` §9). So the page leads with the person: the session musician, the wedding
  photographer, the 23-year-old who never gets the first illustration job. Not "the creative
  industries", which sounds like London.
- *The chooser is named:* the firms that trained without asking, and the government that preferred
  an opt-out 3% of people backed. Not "the technology".
- *The honesty line will be read twice:* by the reader (who mostly won't care, and will respect the
  plainness) and by the critics (who will check it against what we do). It must survive the second
  read; it does only if `docs/using-ai.md` §5 is real. **Pay-a-human-on-every-release is the
  precondition for publishing this paragraph.** If it hasn't happened yet, say "and we're starting to
  pay people on every release" only once a name can be printed beside it.
- *Risk:* the confession is clipped alone ("AI party admits stealing art"). Mitigation: it is one
  plain sentence in a paragraph that proposes the rule that costs us; never a headline, never the
  lead.

### Page-ready text (Chancellor's voice, renderer-safe plain paragraph)

> Whose work taught the machine. The law here already says a firm that copies your songs, your
> photos or your words to train a model for profit has to ask you first, and we keep it that way,
> with no opt-out, and make every firm selling AI here publish what it trained on, because a right
> you can't check is a right you don't have. We make our own pictures and music with these machines,
> and nobody was paid for the work they learned from, including us. That's a judgement call, looked
> at again in a year: if the people whose work it was aren't being paid more by then, every firm
> selling AI here pays into the pot that pays them.

(Four sentences; if it must be three, cut the "with no opt-out" clause and keep the confession
sentence intact. The confession sentence is never cut alone.)
