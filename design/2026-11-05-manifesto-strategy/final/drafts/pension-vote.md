## Where the UK actually is (the research, eight searches)

- **Default funds are being made huge, by law.** The Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April
  2026, c. 22) requires multi-employer auto-enrolment schemes (master trusts and group personal
  pensions) to run at least one "main scale default arrangement" of £25bn or more by 2030, with a
  transition route for £10bn schemes to reach £25bn by 2035 [M]. Most of it commences by later
  regulations [M].
- **Ministers took a power to direct where the default fund invests.** The same Act holds a reserve
  power to set mandatory allocation targets in default funds, capped at 10% of the fund with 5% in UK
  assets, usable no earlier than 2028, lapsing in 2032 if unused and repealed in 2035 [M]. The Lords
  rejected it several times as an intrusion on trustees' fiduciary duty before the caps were added [M].
- **The Mansion House Accord (May 2025)** is the voluntary version: 17 providers, about £252bn of
  default-fund money in scope, aiming at 10% in private markets and 5% in UK private markets by 2030
  [M]. It builds on the 2023 Mansion House Compact (5% unlisted equity) [M]. First progress report:
  £11.1bn in UK private markets at end-2025, mostly property [M]. **Neither the Act nor the Accord
  says anything about how the shares already held are voted.**
- **Votes are reported once a year, by trustees, on their own pick of "significant".** Trust-based
  schemes publish an implementation statement describing votes cast on their behalf, "including the
  most significant votes" (the 2019 amendment to the investment-and-disclosure regulations; DWP
  statutory guidance from October 2022 says what counts as significant) [M]. The industry template
  suggests about 10 significant votes per fund [M]. Contract-based group personal pensions are
  overseen instead by the provider's Independent Governance Committee under FCA rules [K, check].
- **The UK Stewardship Code 2026** (FRC, in force 1 January 2026) is voluntary, "apply and explain",
  and says nothing about member choice [M].
- **Nest** is the state-founded auto-enrolment scheme: about 14 million members and £62.9bn at March
  2026, of which £13bn in the UK economy [M]. Its voting policy is set centrally by its trustee, Nest
  Corporation [M, inferred from the policy document; check]. Nest is a Mansion House signatory [M].
  Its chair is appointed by the DWP Secretary of State [K, check].
- **Pass-through voting exists here, at small scale.** Legal & General's asset manager (LGIM) has
  offered pass-through voting through the fintech Tumelo since 2023, the first UK manager to do so
  [M]. Takers: Camden's £2bn LGPS fund first, then Greater Manchester Pension Fund, and SAUL (the
  universities' scheme) for both its DC and DB sections [M]. These are **scheme-level** choices: the
  fund chooses the policy, not the member.
- **Member-level choice exists in the US, and the take-up is the warning.** BlackRock's Voting Choice:
  over $630bn of client assets enrolled by September 2024 [M]. Vanguard's Investor Choice: 12 index
  funds, over $1tn eligible in 2025, participation "on track to surpass 10%" of eligible clients, plus
  plan sponsors covering 100,000+ workplace-plan members [M, self-reported]. Trade press headline on
  the result: "Vanguard investor program fails to rock proxy votes" [M]: many who choose pick a
  board-aligned policy.
- **Fiduciary duty allows it.** The Law Commission's 2014 report: trustees may weigh non-financial
  factors where they have good reason to think members share the concern and there is no risk of
  significant financial detriment [K, check].

**What this means for the flagship.** The money is already pooled, already huge, and the state has
just taken a power to tell it *where to invest*. The members have no say in *how it votes*. That gap
is the policy. The honest limit: shareholders almost never vote on "automation" as such; they vote on
boards, pay, mergers and resolutions. The flagship works through those.

## The contract (twelve fields)

**1. The line.** *Your pension already owns the firms that automate. It should vote like it's yours.* (70 characters)

**2. The wrong.** A care worker, auto-enrolled at 22, has a few thousand pounds in a default fund she
never chose. That fund holds shares in the logistics firm that has just replaced her brother's
warehouse shift with a sorting robot. At the AGM the fund voted for the chief executive's pay
package, which was tied to "cost efficiencies". She finds out, if at all, a year later, in a PDF
called an implementation statement. She owns a slice of the machine. Somebody else holds her vote.

**3. Who decided.** Parliament and the Treasury, by the Pensions Act 2008 (auto-enrolment puts the
money in a default most people never leave) [K]; the Pension Schemes Act 2026, which made default
funds bigger and gave ministers a power over where they invest, and left the votes with trustees and
fund managers [M]; and the trustees and asset managers who vote those shares today, usually on a
house policy, often guided by proxy advisers.

**4. The rule.** *The reporting already exists; we make it fast, plain and the member's.* For every
main scale default arrangement (the Pension Schemes Act 2026 category):
(a) before each voting season, the trustees (or the IGC, for contract-based schemes) publish a
**flagged list**: votes at the fund's 100 largest holdings on mergers and restructurings that announce
large job cuts, pay packages tied to headcount or cost-cutting targets, and any resolution on AI and
the workforce;
(b) every flagged vote is published within 30 days, in one plain sentence each, and appears in the
member's annual statement, replacing "most significant votes, once a year";
(c) members may pick a **voting policy** from a short list the trustees certify as consistent with
fiduciary duty (for example: the trustee's own; a workforce-first policy; a board-aligned policy),
or stay on the default, which remains the trustees'. Members choose a policy, never a ballot per
company.
Drafted as an amendment to the 2019 implementation-statement rules (trust-based) and a matching FCA
rule (contract-based) [K, check: exact instruments]. No new body. No direction of where money is
invested, and on the mandation power we say plainly: *we would rather members directed the vote than
ministers directed the investment.*

**5. The ledger.**
- *Who pays:* in the trial, the government, from DWP's budget, so no member pays more than today. If
  it goes national, the funds, within the existing auto-enrolment charge cap (0.75% [K, check]), with
  its own cost ceiling (see fails-if).
- *Who gains:* members, who get a say and a receipt; boards that rely on index funds as a quiet yes
  lose some of that.
- *Direction of money:* almost none moves. This pledge moves votes, not pounds.
- *Band:* **Small** (under £100m a year). The trial is single-digit millions. National: at the cost
  ceiling of 0.01% a year, about £25m on the Accord's £252bn [M for the base; the ceiling is ours].

**6. The test 🚦.** **One default fund first: Nest**, the state's own scheme, because the state set it
up and its members are the reader (low and middle earners, auto-enrolled, mostly never chose a fund).
- *Assigned by lot:* every Nest member gets (b), the fast published votes (cheap, harms nobody). A
  random half of members get the offer in (c), the voting-policy choice; the other half stay on
  today's arrangement. The control group never gets less than today.
- *Measured how:* the share of offered members who make a choice; the cost per member; as a
  secondary, whether offered members stop or cut their contributions less often than the control
  half (does a say make the pension feel like theirs?).
- *Interim measure:* after the first voting season, the share of offered members who have opened the
  choice at all.
- *Runs:* two voting seasons, then a published review.

**7. Fails if 🚦 (provisional).** After two voting seasons, **fewer than 1 in 20** members offered the
choice have made one (Vanguard's US programme claims about 1 in 10; we set the bar at half that), **or**
the running cost exceeds **0.01% of the fund a year**. Interim: if under **1 in 50** have even opened
the choice after the first season, the review is brought forward. We publish the write-up either way.

**8. The undo 🚦.** It stops on its date unless Parliament votes to keep it, on the published review.
**Passes, and it goes national:** every main scale default arrangement under the 2026 Act offers the
flagged list, the 30-day publication and the policy choice.

**9. Who it leaves out** (counted on their own line).
- People never auto-enrolled: earning under the £10,000 trigger, often several part-time jobs added
  together, and mostly women [K, check]; anyone under 22 [K].
- The self-employed, gig and platform workers, with no workplace pension at all (roughly four million
  self-employed [K, check]).
- People who opted out, often because money was tight.
- Members of single-employer and public-sector defined-benefit schemes in the first trial (the LGPS
  already has its own pass-through precedents; they may join by volunteering).
- Pensioners already in an annuity: no shares, no vote.
- And the honest limit: most of a default fund is global shares, so the slice of any one UK firm is
  small. The vote is real; the leverage on one boardroom is modest.

**10. Who signs.** The Chancellor owns the flagship and answers for it. The DWP pensions minister runs
the Nest trial. Nest's trustee signs the flagged list and every vote; the review is signed by the
Office for Tests and Postmortems. AI may sort the flagged list; a named trustee signs it.

**11. Status.** Draft.

**12. Needs.** Nothing to start. It gets better with W&P's automation count (a cleaner flag for "big
automation decision") and the Ledger (which prints the votes beside the money). It does not need the
Public Slice, and the Slice does not need it: they are the two halves of *Yours*.

## Strongest objection, and the answer

**Objection (the wonk, the industry and the right at once):** "A gimmick. Pass-through take-up is
tiny, the members who do choose mostly pick the board's policy, it costs money that comes out of
pots, and it hands fiduciary decisions to people with no expertise. Labour's reserve power already
shows what happens when politicians get near pension money. And the fund owns a sliver of any firm
that matters."

**Answer:** Every one of those is a prediction, so we wrote it into the fails-if. If fewer than 1 in
20 take it up, or it costs more than 0.01% a year, it stops and we publish why. The default stays
with the trustees; members choose only among policies the trustees certify, so fiduciary duty is
untouched (and the Law Commission says trustees may weigh what members care about, short of
financial harm). We take no power over where the money goes; we are the party asking for less
ministerial control of pensions than the current law gives. If members choose the board-aligned
policy, that is a result, not a failure: the point is that they chose. And the half that needs no
take-up at all, every flagged vote in plain words within a month, is the receipt nobody gets today.
