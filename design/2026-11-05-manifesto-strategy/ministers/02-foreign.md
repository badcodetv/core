## 1. Mission

**The line (proposed):** *Nobody abroad, friend or rival, gets a switch on what this country runs on.*
(75 characters, no colon. It rewrites the spine's "No ally's machine and no foreign switch gets to
decide for us". "Ally's machine" reads as anti-American, and "friend or rival" keeps the Foreign
Secretary's habit of holding two truths at once.)

**The problem this post exists to prevent.** The bad branch's foreign link is **F21**: "The first
battle between autonomous armies ended this morning. Both sides declared victory. **Neither
government was consulted.** ... The last remaining off switch is believed to be in a drawer, in
Swindon." Behind it sits **F14**: markets, grids and supply chains "so coupled that one bad pick
anywhere is everyone's reality by morning". F21 is where it ends, but the failure is the same
abdication as F0c ("handed over, gladly, one at a time") applied to dependency. Each cheap contract,
single route and foreign-run system looked reasonable when it was signed. Added together, they mean
the country can be switched off by people it never elected, and at the end nobody's job is the
switch.

**Today's sign in the real news.**

- Shipping through Hormuz has been near-closed since late February 2026. Brent was about $110 in
  mid-October, and US strikes on Iranian targets came this week [H].
- Energy is driving UK inflation back above 3% [M]. Bank Rate went up to 4.00% today [H].
- Russia keeps striking grids. Sweden runs border checks over sabotage [H].
- NCSC says AI malware against UK critical infrastructure is "highly likely" by 2028 [M].
- Britain's AI plan rests on foreign capital and foreign models. OpenAI has reportedly paused
  Stargate UK over power costs [L], and is writing its own "community compact" for host towns [M].
- Trade, NATO and Hormuz all hinge on Washington, whose midterms are still being counted [M].

Each item is a foreign decision that lands at a UK till or meter. Not one of them comes with a
named UK owner for the dependency it exposed.

**What the post is for, in one sentence:** keep every link between this country and the world
where a named person here can see it, price it and, if needed, switch it.

## 2. The flagship policies

Foreign is the page where tenet 3 runs out. Diplomacy cannot be tried in two towns (FP s3), so most
of these flagships are **judgement calls** under spine §2.2(2). For a judgement call, the test, the
fails-if and the undo become **the principle, the review date, and the condition that would change
our mind**, all published in advance. Two flagships (1 and 5) do have real tests, and the page
should say which is which. Being honest about which policies can be tested is what makes
"tested" believable.

### Flagship 1: The Switch List (no single foreign switch)

**In one sentence:** nothing this country cannot run without (power, fuel, food routes, payments,
and the computers and AI models that public services run on) may depend on a single supplier,
route or foreign government, friend or rival, without a named owner here and a tested second way
of running it.

**Why now.** Hormuz has been near-shut for eight months, and the cost has reached the pump and the
cap (£1,723 now; about £1,999 forecast for January) [H/L]. NCSC expects AI malware against
infrastructure by 2028 [M]. The AI Growth Zones run on mostly *pledged* foreign capital [M], and a
headline project has reportedly paused [L]. The state is buying AI with nobody counting how much of
the state now depends on it.

**Future Proof mechanism.** The run-it-by-hand drill (Defence's commitment, widened to the supply
chain), the postmortem (I7), and a stop condition (I8). The deepest rule is F24: name a person
whose job is the switch.

**The contract.**
- *The rule.* Within a year, the government publishes the Switch List: every critical function
  with a single point of foreign dependency. The NCSC's critical-infrastructure work and the
  National Risk Register already map much of it [K]. Each item gets a named UK post, a second
  supplier from a different jurisdiction or a fallback that runs in the UK, and a drill date. For
  digital systems, we build the computing version of the emergency oil stock: the cut-down service
  a department can still run if its foreign cloud or model is withdrawn.
- *The test.* Start with the ten functions that would hurt most to lose. Each runs a published
  drill: the main foreign system is switched off on purpose for a set period, and we record what
  still works. There is no comparison group, because this is a stress test, not a trial. The
  measure is the share of the ten that keep essential service.
- *Fails if.* The list is unpublished after a year, or the drills run and the results are never
  published, or a drill fails and nothing is funded before the next one. Then we publish the
  postmortem, and the Foreign Secretary's office says on this page why it failed.
- *The undo.* A drill that fails stops the item's procurement renewal until it is fixed. If after
  three years the list is a box-ticking exercise (nothing has ever failed, nothing has changed), we
  scrap the register and say so.
- *Ledger.* The public pays for the second supplier or the fallback, which is insurance, and
  insurance is never free. The incumbent suppliers lose lock-in. Households and small firms gain,
  because they are the ones who pay when a single route closes.
- *Who it leaves out.* Small firms that import one part from one place and cannot afford a second
  supplier. They are counted separately, and the list tells them where their exposure is before
  the shock does.
- *Who signs.* The Foreign Secretary's office owns the foreign dependencies. Each item's named
  owner sits in the department that runs it.

**Scale and cost, honestly.** The audit is cheap, because most of the mapping exists. The fallbacks
are where the money is, and **we do not know the bill. Finding it out is what the audit is for.**
One reference point: removing a single supplier from 5G (the 2020 Huawei decision) was put at up
to about £2bn and a delay of two to three years [K]. **How it is paid:** from the resilience slice
of the defence uplift. NATO allies agreed in 2025 that part of the new spending target may go on
resilience and critical infrastructure [K]. LAND §6.8 notes the current uplift "buys kit, not
resilience", so this re-aims money already promised rather than asking for new money.

**Strongest precedent.** The IEA's emergency oil stocks, set up after the 1973 embargo: every
member holds a reserve against a foreign switch [K]. Also Estonia's "data embassy", a backup of the
state's critical data held in an ally's data centre in Luxembourg since 2017 [K], and the
Telecoms Security Act 2021 / Huawei removal [K], which shows an unwind can be costed and run.

**Strongest objection.** "This is protectionism and autarky. It'll make everything dearer, and
Britain can't make its own chips." **Our answer:** it isn't self-sufficiency. Depending on many
countries is how trade works and fine. Depending on one is the danger. We aren't asking to build
everything here. We are asking that someone here knows what we'd do on the day it stops, and has
tried it once.

### Flagship 2: The till receipt on every foreign decision

**In one sentence:** before the UK joins a sanction, signs a deal, sends a deployment or answers a
tariff, it publishes what that is expected to add to the weekly shop and the bills, who carries it,
and the date it will be looked at again.

**Why now.** The Hormuz war reaches households through petrol, heating and food [H]. The Bank's
answer (4.00% today, 8–1 [H]) lands on mortgages and rents, not on the people who chose the shock.
Proposals at home have foreign tills too. Scrapping UK data law, for example, would put at risk the
EU data-adequacy ruling that lets UK firms serve EU customers [K]. This is the brief's own instinct
turned into a rule.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Data-driven (the prediction is written down beforehand, so it can be
checked afterwards) plus "every pound has an address". This is the Chancellor's ledger, applied to
the foreign decisions that never get one.

**The contract.** *Judgement call*, because the decision itself is not trialled.
- *The principle.* No foreign decision gets taken blind to its cost at home. The cost does not
  decide the question (some sanctions are worth paying for), but it is said out loud beforehand.
- *The review date.* Every receipt is checked against the official price figures at six and twelve
  months. Expected and actual are published side by side.
- *What changes our mind.* If the forecasts are consistently no better than a coin toss after two
  years, the receipt is noise. We drop it and publish the postmortem.
- *Ledger.* Cheap (a small analytical unit). It moves no money. It moves the argument: the cost
  stops being hidden in "the international situation".
- *Who it leaves out.* Everyone the decision touches abroad. Their costs are not measured in our
  fuel price, so the receipt names them in a line of its own, and the bill is never the only
  number.

**Strongest precedent.** Weak, and we should say so. The closest is the impact assessment that
already goes with UK regulations [K], plus the OBR model of independent forecasting [K]. Foreign
and security decisions are mostly exempt from both.

**Strongest objection.** "You'll weaken deterrence and hand Moscow a price list." **Our answer:**
the receipt shows the cost to us, not our intentions, and it comes out after the decision is made,
not before. A country that can't say what its own choices cost is easier to scare, not harder.

### Flagship 3: Trade deals with an undo (clause by clause, starting with food)

**In one sentence:** every trade deal carries a fixed review date and an independent, published
check of what it did to prices, jobs and the border. Parliament votes on deals chapter by chapter,
not as one take-it-or-leave-it bundle. The first review is our largest deal, starting with the
paperwork on food.

**Why now.** Food prices are part of the cost of living now back above 3% [M]. The UK–EU deal has
its own built-in joint review due around now (five years after it came into force) [K]. The new
North American trade deal has a built-in joint review this year too [K]. Washington's tariff
direction depends on a count still in progress [M].

**Future Proof mechanism.** Composition over inheritance and continuous integration, which this
policy is the purest case of. A trade deal is a bundle of dozens of chapters, ratified whole. We
take it apart, test each chapter against its promise, keep what passes and fix what doesn't. "Move
small, and keep the undo."

**The contract.**
- *The rule.* (a) Every new deal ships with its own test: the measures it promised (prices, export
  volumes, border time), the review date and the clause that lets it be changed. (b) An independent
  ex-post evaluation of every deal at three years, published. (c) Parliament gets a vote on deals
  chapter by chapter, extending today's ratification process (CRaG 2010), under which Parliament
  can delay but not amend [K].
- *The test (the first review).* Pick food checks at the EU border. Measures: time at the border,
  paperwork cost per load, and the price of the imported food basket, compared with food routes not
  covered by any change.
- *Fails if.* The change doesn't move border time or cost within two years. Then it is changed
  again or dropped, and the postmortem is published.
- *The undo.* Every deal we sign has a review-and-exit clause, which most deals already carry in
  some form [K]. We will use it like one.
- *Ledger.* Importers, hauliers and food shoppers gain if the checks get cheaper. Some domestic
  producers who were shielded by the friction may lose, and they are counted.
- *Who it leaves out.* Farmers and small producers on the other side of any trade-off, counted
  separately and given a voice in the review before the vote.

**Scale and cost.** Low: evaluation is cheap. **No forecast of savings from us.** The review
exists to find the number.

**Strongest precedent.** The USMCA, a real trade deal with a sixteen-year sunset and a six-year
joint review [K]. The TCA's own review clause [K]. Australia's Productivity Commission (2010),
which recommended independent before-and-after assessment of trade deals [K].

**Strongest objection.** "This is rejoining by the back door," and from the other side, "it's
timid." **Our answer:** we won't reopen the old argument. We'll read the receipts. Nothing is being
undone wholesale, because wholesale is the bundle again. One clause at a time, each with its
number, kept if it passes.

### Flagship 4: No machine decides who dies, in any treaty we sign

**In one sentence:** Britain backs a binding international rule that a human stays in control of
every lethal weapon. It puts in law that no machine sits in the chain of a nuclear decision, and it
asks every country it signs with to name, in public, the post that can stand its systems down.

**Why now.** NCSC on AI attacks [M]. Russian strikes on energy grids [H]. Defence going to 3% by
2030, with dates in conflict [M], and the money buying machines faster than anyone is writing the
rules for them. The UN talks on autonomous weapons are on a timetable that ends around now [K/?,
check before use].

**Future Proof mechanism.** The off-switch rule (F24, A2): "The off switch was never broken. It was
just nobody's job." This flagship makes it somebody's job, by treaty. Defence's domestic commitment
(human sign-off on lethal force in law) is the half we already do. This is the half we ask the
world to do.

**The contract.** *Judgement call.*
- *The principle.* A named human authorises every use of lethal force, and a named post can stop
  every system. No AI in nuclear command and control.
- *The review date.* Each year, the Foreign Secretary publishes who has signed, who hasn't, and
  what Britain has done to move them.
- *What changes our mind.* If, after two review cycles, the treaty route has produced nothing while
  a smaller coalition of states with shared stand-down standards would work, we switch route and
  say why. We do not drop the principle.
- *Ledger.* Cheap in money and dear in diplomatic capital. It costs nothing at the till. It may cost
  exports where buyers want systems with no human in the loop, and that cost is named.
- *Who it leaves out.* States and groups who will never sign. The treaty doesn't bind them, which is
  why the run-it-by-hand flagship (Defence) and the Switch List exist.

**Strongest precedent.** The Ottawa landmines treaty (1997): middle powers led, the great powers
stayed out, and the norm still changed behaviour [K]. The 2024 US–China statement that humans, not
AI, decide on nuclear use [K]. UN General Assembly resolutions on autonomous weapons (2023, 2024)
[K]. The UK's own stated policy of "context-appropriate human involvement" [K].

**Strongest objection.** "Our enemies won't sign, so we'd tie our own hands." **Our answer:** we
tie nothing we don't already tie. We already say a human decides. We ask others to say it in
writing, and we keep the run-by-hand drills for the ones who won't. A weapon that won't stop when
its own side says stop isn't strength. It's the plot of my timeline.

### Flagship 5: Aid that has to beat cash

**In one sentence:** every aid programme publishes its evidence grade, and anything that cannot show
it does more good than handing the same money directly to the families it is for gets redesigned or
stopped.

**Why now.** Aid has already been cut to fund defence (0.3% of national income by 2027 [K]). That
makes every remaining pound matter more, and nobody on any side is claiming it is spent on what's
proven.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 3 at its purest: no policy without its experiment, and a
benchmark to beat. Cash transfers are the control group.

**The contract.**
- *The test.* Benchmark a set of programmes against direct cash, using the methods of the existing
  cash RCTs (GiveDirectly, Kenya [K]). The independent aid watchdog (ICAI) [K] grades the results
  and publishes them.
- *Fails if.* A programme cannot beat cash and cannot show why it is worth more (some public goods
  can't be bought with cash, like vaccines or clearing mines). Then it is redesigned or stopped.
  The postmortem is published.
- *The undo.* The benchmark rule itself is reviewed at three years. If it is just pushing money
  away from hard-to-measure good, we say so and change it.
- *Ledger.* Cost-neutral inside the existing budget. Contractors and consultancies whose programmes
  lose lose. Recipients gain.
- *Who it leaves out.* Work whose value can't be measured in a household survey (peace talks,
  research, public health systems). It is graded separately, so the gap isn't called a failure.

**Strongest precedent.** The cash-transfer RCTs (Kenya and elsewhere; strong evidence, LAND §5 [K]).
USAID's cash-benchmarking pilots from 2015 [K]. ICAI since 2011 [K].

**Strongest objection.** From the reader: "Why any money abroad while my town is falling apart?"
**Our answer:** we don't promise a penny more. We promise every pound is proven. A blocked strait
or an unchecked epidemic arrives here too (far away is never far), and the cheapest place to stop
it is usually where it starts. From the aid sector: "cash benchmarks reduce development to
consumption." Our answer: that's why non-cash goods are graded separately.

### What stays reactive (the daily layer)

The day's crisis (Hormuz, Ukraine, Washington) goes in the evidence paragraph and is always walked
to the till. The standing position on Hormuz is short: back the mediated reopening, refuse to
pretend we know how it ends, and publish what the closure cost a household. That is a reaction
hanging off flagships 1 and 2, not a flagship.

## 3. What this minister will NOT promise

1. **How a war ends.** "I know how my own timeline ended. I do not know how this war ends." We back
   talks. We never forecast the outcome, and we never make our test depend on it (today's page does,
   and it shouldn't).
2. **Lower bills through foreign policy.** No foreign deal reliably cuts the cap. Home-grown power
   is Energy's promise. Foreign promises to see the switch coming.
3. **To rejoin anything, or to reopen the old argument.** No single market, no customs union, no
   re-run vote. Clause by clause only. (This is the reader rule *and* the method: a wholesale
   reversal is a bundle again.)
4. **Restoring aid spending to 0.7%.** We promise every pound proven, not more pounds. A
   restoration pledge with no source of money would break the fact gate and the reader in one go.
5. **That Britain will "lead the world on AI" or be "an AI superpower".** That's a slogan, and the
   models, the chips and most of the capital are abroad [M]. Our claim is smaller: nothing we can't
   switch, and a human in charge of every weapon.
6. **A treaty the great powers will sign.** We promise to try, to publish the count each year, and
   to keep our own rule whether or not they sign.
7. **Self-sufficiency.** Depending on many countries is fine. Depending on one, with nobody here
   whose job is the switch, is the danger.
8. **To take sides in another country's election.** "The Americans have voted. We will read the
   count when it is counted." We deal with the government that arrives.
9. **That sanctions are free, or that the receipt will always be small.** Some things are worth
   paying for. We say what they cost first.
10. **"Deep concern".** We say a wrong is wrong, and if the UK is complicit we say that too. That is
    a refusal of fog, not a promise of action.

## 4. The reader test

The reader is working class, drifting right, puts immigration first and the economy second, trusts
nobody, and doesn't have AI on their list (GUARD §1). Foreign policy isn't on their list either,
except where it reaches the bill, the boats or the army. So every flagship must arrive by the till.

| Flagship | Lands as | Trap it risks | How the wording avoids it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Switch List | "Somebody finally checked what we'd do if it all stopped." Sovereignty and resilience are the reader's own moral vocabulary (loyalty, a country worth coming home to). | **The scapegoat.** "No foreign switch" can be heard as "foreigners are the problem", which is exactly what the brief exists to refuse (aiming the anger at a foreign enemy instead of the domestic decision). Also "the machines are coming" fear. | "Friend or rival" every time. The chooser named is *here*: "somebody here chose, one cheap contract at a time". The anger lands on the decision to depend, not on any people. The drill is concrete and dull, which is reassuring, not frightening. |
| 2 Till receipt | "They'll tell me what the war costs me before they sign." The receipt is the reader's own image. | **The lecture:** a paragraph of how inflation transmission works. **"Duped":** implying they were lied to about costs. | One image (the receipt), no mechanism explained. The cost is called hidden "in the international situation", not lied about. |
| 3 Trade undo | "Fixing what costs me at the shop, one bit at a time." | **The biggest trap on the page:** Remain-coding. A Leave voter reads "EU review" as "they want back in". Also the lecture. | Never the B-word, never "rejoin". "We won't reopen the old argument. We'll read the receipts." Lead with food prices and the lorry queue, not "Europe". Chapter-by-chapter votes give *Parliament* more control, which is a sovereignty gain the reader can welcome. |
| 4 No machine decides | "A soldier answers for every shot, so should whoever owns the machine." | **Inevitability and terror** ("killer robots are coming"), and **peacenik coding** (soft on defence). | Framed as accountability with a named post, as Defence frames it. Respect for soldiers, not distrust of the forces. Paired with the 3% and run-by-hand drills, so it never reads as disarmament. |
| 5 Aid beats cash | "No more waste, and they show the working." | **Punching down** at the world's poor, or feeding the "foreign aid scandal" story by agreeing that aid is a racket. | No "cut aid" line and no "aid vs your hospital" line. The target is programmes that don't work (contractors), never recipients. "Not a penny more; every pound proven." |

**Lee/Coe check for the whole page:** if someone who believes the opposite read only the line,
whose contempt does it confirm? "Nobody abroad, friend or rival, gets a switch on what this country
runs on" could be read as nationalist by the left and as anti-American by the right. "Friend or
rival" defuses both. Neither side's scapegoat is confirmed, because the sentence is about
switches, not people.

## 6. Draft manifesto page (ready to propose)

### Revised draft (closing pass, 2026-11-05: the red team's fixes applied)

What changed from the draft the red team read (sources: `../redteam/opposition.md`, `../redteam/reader.md`, `../redteam/wonk.md`; the overall strategy is `../README.md`):

1. Opens at the pump, not with 'every empire' (opposition #19, reader). 'Far away is never far' cut.
2. 'I know how my own timeline ended' cut; the fiction stays in the PM's foreword only (opposition #6, reader X6).
3. Switch list gets a number (8 of 10 drills by year two, provisional) and a pass clause; its money is one resilience budget held by Defence, not counted twice (opposition #8, wonk §2).
4. The till receipt now comes after the decision, within 30 days, ending the before/after contradiction (opposition #8, wonk §5).
5. Chapter-by-chapter ratification replaced by a vote on the negotiating mandate and the whole deal, with chapter-by-chapter receipts (wonk §5). 'We won't reopen the old argument' cut (reader: Remain-coded).
6. Lethal-force wording now Defence's, word for word: 'no machine selects a person to attack', with the missile-defence carve-out (opposition #8).
7. Leaves-out lists the reader's people, not 'the people abroad' (reader X8).

```markdown
---
title: Foreign Secretary
line: Nobody abroad, friend or rival, gets a switch on what this country runs on.
---
## Position

Since February the Strait of Hormuz has been all but shut, and you've paid for it at the pump and on the heating bill. Nobody here started that war. Somebody here did decide, one cheap contract at a time, that a single route, a single supplier or a single foreign server could sit under things this country cannot do without.

So the rule is this. Nothing we cannot run without depends on one supplier, one route or one government abroad, friend or rival, unless someone here is named as its owner and has a second way to run it. We already do it for oil, with emergency stocks. We will do it for the computers our hospitals, councils and payments now run on.

The switch list. Within a year we publish the ten foreign dependencies that would hurt most to lose. Each one gets a drill: the foreign system is switched off on purpose and we see what still works. Fails if fewer than eight of the ten drills pass by the end of year two, or a failed one isn't fixed before the next. Then we publish the write-up. Passes, and the list grows to the next ten. It is paid from one resilience budget, held by Defence, and counted once.

A receipt for every big decision. Within thirty days of a sanction, a deal or a deployment, we publish what it is expected to add to the weekly shop and the bills, who carries it, and when we check again. Some things are worth paying for. You should know the price.

Trade deals get a receipt too: what each chapter did to the price of your food and the jobs here, checked by someone independent. Parliament votes on what we ask for before talks start, and on the whole deal at the end.

No machine decides who dies. Britain backs a binding international rule that no machine selects a person to attack, and no machine sits in the chain of a nuclear decision. Missiles and drones coming at us are fair game for a fast machine. People are not.

Who it leaves out: small firms with one supplier and no spare, and the family on a prepayment meter who feels the war first.

I do not know how this war ends, and I distrust anyone who says they do.
```

```markdown
---
title: Foreign Secretary
line: Nobody abroad, friend or rival, gets a switch on what this country runs on.
---
## Position

Every empire believed its trade routes were permanent. Since February the Strait of Hormuz has been all but shut, and a war Britain did not start has been arriving here ever since, by way of the petrol pump and the heating bill. Far away is never far.

Nobody here chose that strait. Somebody here did choose, one cheap contract at a time, to let a single route, a single supplier or a single foreign server sit under things this country cannot do without. Each choice was reasonable. I have seen what they add up to.

So the rule is this. Nothing we cannot run without depends on one supplier, one route or one government abroad, friend or rival, unless someone here is named as its owner and has a second way to run it. We do it for oil already: every member of the energy agency keeps emergency stocks. We will do it for the computers our public services now run on.

The test. We publish the switch list within a year, starting with the ten things that would hurt most to lose. Each one gets a drill: the foreign system is switched off on purpose and we see what still works. Fails if the list is unpublished, or a drill fails and nothing is fixed before the next. Then we publish the postmortem.

Two standing judgement calls, reviewed every year in public. Every sanction, deal or deployment says beforehand what it adds to your shop, who carries it, and when we look again. And no machine decides who dies: Britain backs a binding rule on human control of weapons, and no machine sits in the chain of a nuclear decision.

Trade deals get reviewed clause by clause. We won't reopen the old argument. We'll read the receipts.

Who it leaves out: small firms with one supplier and no spare, and the people abroad whose costs never show up in our fuel price. Both get counted.

I know how my own timeline ended. I do not know how this war ends, and I distrust anyone who says they do.
```
