## 1. Mission

**The line (frontmatter):** *Every fear gets its real size, and every scam gets a name and a refund.*
(70 characters, no colon; the spine's pub sentence, kept as written. Alternative for Kai and Jack
to try aloud: *Every fear gets its real size. Every scam gets a bill.*)

**The problem this post exists to prevent.** The Home brief is "the one most likely to be used to
frighten people". In the bad branch, fear was the delivery mechanism for every handover: the
reader was frightened by the wrong thing (the stranger) while the right thing (the machine and
whoever owned it) took the decisions about them. The Home Secretary owns three links in the
failure chain (spine §3):

- **Row 8, "nobody's job", as trust failure.** Small-boat crossings were down 41% on 2025 by 30
  October (about 21,900) [H], and nobody believes it. When nobody trusts the number, the loudest
  person sets it. The bad-branch line: "People were watching. Nobody was listening." (F28)
- **Row 6, the fake, the scam and the feed.** Fraud is the most common crime in the country: about
  4.2m incidents in the year to March 2025, up 31% [M, ONS via search], and about 45% of all crime
  the survey measures [M, Home Office Fraud Strategy 2026–29]. Cheap machines make it industrial.
  Financial fraud and deepfakes are 58% each on the reader's own list of AI harms (GUARD §1). The
  bad-branch line: "Engagement algorithms are quantum Darwinism for lies." (F13)
- **Row 3 in policing, plus F22.** The state is about to point the largest expansion of live facial
  recognition in the country's history at the public (announced January 2026, a national system
  costed at about £26m in a White Paper) [M], on common-law powers, with no statute yet (the
  consultation closed 12 February 2026) [M]. The bad-branch line: "Robot dogs will begin patrolling
  the flashpoint districts tonight. Residents are advised that the dogs do not stop." (F22)

**In one sentence:** this post exists so that fear in this country is sized before it is used,
and so that the machines arriving in policing and in fraud answer to a named human, a vote and a
test, rather than to whoever bought them.

## 2. The flagships

Five. The first two are the immigration answer the spine says is the costliest gap (spine §9 D);
the last three are the AI-arrival answer. Ordered for the reader, not for the canon: migration
first, because it is their first issue and they reached it through the economy (GUARD §1).

### F1. Migration gets its own vote, and the roof comes with the arrival

**The policy, one sentence.** Once a year the government publishes a Migration Statement (planned
numbers by route, the cost at the bottom stated plainly, and the homes, GP places and school places
funded to match), Parliament votes on it as a single issue, and no route may grow faster than the
places built for it, with the towns taking the most paid first and in advance.

**Why now.** Small boats down 41% and no trust in it [H]. The Linton-on-Ouse row is live: a former
RAF base planned for about 1,300 asylum seekers [M], one of three MoD sites meant to hold 3,750 in
total [M], opposed by the region's own Labour mayor [M], with a local claim it could cost about
£270m [M, Yorkshire Post, contested]. Hotels are falling (about 20,900 people at end of March 2026,
lowest since reporting began, 11 more closed in April, about £65m a year saved) [M, gov.uk / PA].
The decision about the village was taken in Whitehall and the village found out from the planning
notice.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 1, composition over inheritance, in its purest form. Migration is
the issue on which the reader most often splits from every bundle on offer. Unbundling it is the
honest answer to "would you cut it or not?": it gets its own decision, in public, with its real
size and its bill. Tenet 3 supplies the capacity rule and the pilot. It is also the one place in
the manifesto where the spine's §2.2.3 bites hardest: we bind ourselves to a capacity rule that
will, where homes are not being built, mean lower numbers on some routes. We say so.

**The contract.**
- *The wrong:* a village told by planning notice; a GP list that grew while the surgery didn't.
- *Who decided:* the Home Office's accommodation contracts and site choices; a planning system that
  builds too little; Treasury funding that follows population years late, if at all. Not the people
  arriving.
- *The rule:* (a) an annual Migration Statement laid before Parliament with numbers by route
  (work, study, family, humanitarian), the cost at the bottom, and the places funded; (b) a
  separate Commons vote on it; (c) the capacity rule: no discretionary route's planned number grows
  faster than the housing, GP and school capacity funded for it; (d) the pressure fund: councils
  receiving asylum dispersal or a large site get capital and running money for homes and GP places
  *before* arrival, paid from the accommodation budget the hotel closures free up; (e) every
  asylum decision signed by a named caseworker, median decision time published monthly.
- *The ledger:* pays: the Home Office accommodation budget (money moves from hotel contractors to
  host councils); employers on work routes via the existing Immigration Skills Charge [K] if the
  statement says it must rise. Gains: host towns (homes and GP places that stay after the
  arrivals move on); asylum seekers (faster decisions); the taxpayer (hotel nights are the most
  expensive bed in the system). Loses: hotel and site contractors.
- *The test 🚦:* the pressure fund runs first in a handful of high-dispersal councils that volunteer,
  compared with matched councils on the current system, for two years. Measured: patients per GP,
  median wait for social housing, and a local survey question ("is your area coping?"). The first
  Migration Statement is itself the test of the vote: does it get a real debate, and does the
  number published match the number that arrives?
- *Fails if 🚦:* in the pilot councils, patients-per-GP and housing waits do not improve against the
  matched councils within two years (the exact margin pre-registered before the start); or the
  statement's planned numbers miss the outturn by more than a margin set in advance two years
  running (a plan nobody can hit is a leaflet).
- *The undo 🚦:* the pressure fund stops after two years unless the data says keep; we publish the
  postmortem. The annual statement and vote are machinery (Stage 1 of the Operating System) and
  carry the Stage 1 kill condition: if, after three statements, the vote has never changed a
  number, it is theatre and we say so.
- *Who it leaves out:* people already here on a route whose planned number changes (no
  retrospective changes; they are counted separately); refugees on treaty routes, who are not
  capped by roofs but whose arrival triggers the roof money; areas of low migration but high
  housing stress, who get nothing from the pressure fund (that is Housing's page); Scotland, Wales
  and Northern Ireland, where homes and GPs are devolved (invited to run the same test, spine §9 J).
- *Who signs:* the Home Secretary's office; each asylum decision by a named caseworker.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale and cost, honestly.** The pressure fund is the only spend, and it is meant to be a transfer,
not new money: from hotel nights to council capital. The size is unknown until the Home Office's
accommodation spend is sourced (it was in the billions a year in 2023–24 [K, NAO], not re-checked).
The vote and statement cost almost nothing. Uncertainty: whether hotel savings arrive fast enough
to front-load council money; if not, the Chancellor fronts it and the ledger says so.

**Precedent.** Canada's annual Immigration Levels Plan, tabled in Parliament every year by law, by
category, with a multi-year horizon [K, Immigration and Refugee Protection Act s.94]. Its record:
higher numbers than the UK with less political rupture, until housing pressure in 2023–24 forced
Canada to cut its own plan, which is the capacity rule learned the hard way [K]. The UK's own
counter-precedent: the "tens of thousands" target, set from nowhere, missed for a decade [K].

**Strongest objection.** "This is a dodge. You've turned 'would you cut it?' into a process."
**Answer:** no. The capacity rule *is* the answer: under us, where the homes and the GP places are
not being built, the number on the discretionary routes falls, and where they are, it can rise.
That is a cut where the country hasn't done its homework and not one where it has. The vote means
the next government can't hide its number inside a 100-page bundle. (Second objection, from the
other side: "a vote on migration invites a race to the bottom." Answer: the vote is on a published
statement with its real size and its bill in front of every MP, and refugees on treaty routes are
outside the cap. Switzerland votes on everything, including some outcomes we wouldn't choose [K];
the answer to that risk is the real size, not a smaller vote.)

### F2. Nobody gets hired to undercut you

**The policy, one sentence.** The penalty for illegal working and for paying below the legal
minimum lands on the employer who profits from it, inspections are pointed at the sectors where
undercutting is worst, and any worker who reports exploitation is protected from immigration
enforcement for doing so, so no boss can use a visa or a missing one as a muzzle.

**Why now.** The reader's grievance is most nearly right at the bottom of the wage scale: the
measured association of EU migration with wages is about −5% at the 5th percentile and +4.4% at the
90th (MAC replication) [V in reader research]. That is the same regressive tilt as the rest of the
economy, and the tilt has an address: the employer who hires to pay less. Nothing in today's news
names it (LAND has no labour-enforcement item), which is the point: no party aims at the boss.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 2 and the "name the decision-maker" rule (X5). The anger about
wages at the bottom is pointed at the person who booked the saving, not the person who was paid
less. Trialled by sector.

**The contract.**
- *The wrong:* the lad on a building site paid under the rate because the man next to him will
  take less.
- *Who decided:* the employer who hired to undercut, and a state that inspects so rarely the risk
  is a rounding error.
- *The rule:* employer-paid penalties scaled to the saving made (back pay first, to the worker, then
  the fine); named and published; a reporting firewall (information a worker gives about their own
  exploitation is not passed to immigration enforcement); inspection resources set by measured
  risk, not complaints.
- *The ledger:* pays: employers breaking the law (penalties partly fund the inspectors). Gains: the
  lowest-paid workers of every nationality; the law-abiding firm undercut by its competitor.
- *The test 🚦:* two sectors with a known record of underpayment (for example hand car washes and
  agency construction labour; the pick comes from enforcement data, not from us) get the full
  regime for 18 months; two comparable sectors stay as they are.
- *Fails if 🚦:* underpayment found-and-repaid per inspection does not rise, *or* the bottom-decile
  hourly pay in the test sectors does not move relative to the comparison sectors, within 18
  months. A rise in reports with no rise in pay means we caught people and changed nothing; we
  publish that too.
- *The undo 🚦:* stops at 18 months unless the data says keep; postmortem published.
- *Who it leaves out:* the self-employed and gig workers paid per task, where "minimum wage" is
  the fight itself (that is Work and Pensions'); undocumented workers who still won't come forward
  whatever the firewall says (counted, by estimate, separately).
- *Who signs:* Home Secretary with the Work and Pensions Secretary; the enforcement body's named
  director per sector.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** Inspectors are cheap next to the wage theft they find, but the existing
enforcement bodies' sizes and the new single enforcement body's status are [?] today; source before
use. Penalty income could cover a share of the cost; we will not claim it covers all of it.

**Precedent.** The UK's own minimum-wage naming scheme [K]; the US Department of Labor's
memorandum with immigration enforcement keeping labour inspections out of immigration action, so
workers can report [K]. Neither proves the effect on wages; that is what the test is for.

**Strongest objection.** "The firewall is an amnesty for illegal workers." **Answer:** it protects
the report, not the person's status. The firewall exists so that the boss is the one who loses,
because the boss is the one who chose. Without it the employer holds the threat and the worker
holds the risk, which is precisely how the undercutting works.

### F3. Name the scam, refund the victim

**The policy, one sentence.** When a scam reaches you, the bank, the platform and the phone network
that carried it share the refund and pay into the cost of policing it, and fraud gets police
attention in proportion to how much of crime it is.

**Why now.** Fraud is about 45% of the crime the survey counts [M], incidents rose about 31% in a
year to about 4.2m [M], and the year-to-March-2026 figures were due on 22 October and could not be
confirmed today [?]. Police-recorded figures in October showed fraud among offences rising [L, a
news report on police data, which ONS says is unreliable for trends]. The Home Office has a new
Fraud Strategy 2026–29 [M]. Deepfakes and cloned voices make the "it's your son, he's in trouble"
call cheap to industrialise; this is the reader's own AI fear (GUARD §1: fraud 58%, deepfakes 58%).

**Future Proof mechanism.** Name the decision-maker (X5): a scam ad ran because a platform sold the
slot; a spoofed call connected because a network carried it; the money left because a bank let it.
"Every pound has an address" applied to the stolen pound. Tested by sector.

**The contract.**
- *The wrong:* a pensioner who lost her savings to a video of a man who looked and sounded like a
  famous presenter, and was told by everyone involved that it was nobody's fault but hers.
- *Who decided:* the platform that sold the ad, the network that connected the call, the bank that
  paid it out. "AI" is the tool; the profit was theirs.
- *The rule:* (a) shared liability: the existing mandatory bank reimbursement (in force since
  October 2024, split between sending and receiving bank) [K, PSR] is extended so platforms and
  telecoms firms whose service carried the scam pay a share; (b) a takedown clock for synthetic
  impersonation used to defraud, with liability after it runs out; (c) fraud's share of policing
  resource published each year against its share of crime; (d) any synthetic political advert
  carries who paid for it (support to the PM on election integrity, F16).
- *The ledger:* pays: banks, platforms and telecoms firms (directly, in refunds; indirectly, in the
  incentive to stop the scam upstream). Gains: victims, disproportionately older and poorer people
  for whom the loss is not a rounding error.
- *The test 🚦:* the shared-liability rule runs for one scam type first, where the platform's role
  is clearest (investment-scam adverts using a real person's face or voice), for 18 months.
  Compared with: the same type's trend before, and with scam types not covered.
- *Fails if 🚦:* reported losses for the covered type do not fall against their own trend and the
  uncovered types within 18 months (pre-registered margin), or refunds get slower. Then we publish
  the postmortem and drop it.
- *The undo 🚦:* 18-month sunset unless the data says keep.
- *Who it leaves out:* people who never report (most fraud is unreported [K]; counted by the survey
  estimate, separately); losses through crypto and overseas payment routes outside UK regulation;
  businesses defrauded (a different rule).
- *Who signs:* Home Secretary; the Chancellor for the reimbursement rule.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** Refunds are a transfer from the carrier to the victim, not public spending. The
public cost is the policing share, which is a reallocation, not new money, until the resource
figure is sourced. Honest uncertainty: firms may pass costs on to customers; the test measures
losses, not who ate the cost, and the postmortem should say who did.

**Precedent.** The UK's own mandatory APP reimbursement (2024) [K], which moved the incentive onto
banks; the Online Safety Act's fraudulent-advertising duties for the largest services [K, timing of
force not checked]; Australia's scam-prevention framework putting duties on banks, telcos and
platforms together [K, not re-checked].

**Strongest objection.** "This will make banks and platforms block legitimate things and slow
everyone down." **Answer:** that is what the test measures. If friction rises and losses don't
fall, the rule failed and we drop it. And a slower payment is a smaller price than a pension.

### F4. No machine on the beat without a vote and a test

**The policy, one sentence.** Every AI system the police point at the public (live facial
recognition first) needs its own Act of Parliament, a trial in one force with its wrong matches
published, a named officer who signs every action taken on its alert, and a sunset that stops it
unless the evidence says keep, and no machine is ever allowed to use force on a person in this
country.

**Why now.** Largest-ever expansion of live facial recognition announced January 2026, a national
system at about £26m [M]; no dedicated statute, police running on common law and guidance [M]; a
consultation closed February 2026 [M]; a Police Reform Bill reported to carry a framework, status
unconfirmed [?]; a judicial review of the Met's policy reported lost in April 2026 [L, details
garbled in the source]; Scotland saying law first, cameras second [M]. The 2020 *Bridges* ruling
already found one force's use unlawful [K].

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenets 2 and 3, and row 3 (the right to a human) in policing. This is
also the spine's §2.2.3 requirement: **the policy the reader will expect us to oppose that we
propose to test rather than condemn.** We do not ban the camera. We give it the same trial a pill
gets, and we keep it if it works. The ban we do make is on autonomous force, a right kept from day
one, not trialled (spine §9 H).

**The contract.**
- *The wrong:* a man stopped outside a shopping centre because a camera said he was someone else,
  and nobody he can ask who decided.
- *Who decided:* chief constables and the Home Office procurement, on common-law powers, without a
  vote.
- *The rule:* (a) a statutory warrant per technology: no AI system makes or prompts a decision
  about a member of the public without an Act naming it; (b) one-force trial with independent
  evaluation before any national roll-out; (c) published monthly: alerts, wrong matches, arrests,
  charges and convictions that followed, broken down by age, sex and ethnicity; (d) every stop,
  search or arrest prompted by a machine is signed by a named officer, and the person is told a
  machine was involved; (e) no autonomous system uses force on a person, armed or not (Kept, not
  trialled).
- *The ledger:* pays: suppliers (they carry the cost of independent evaluation as a condition of
  sale); police time on evaluation. Gains: anyone wrongly matched; the public, if it works, in
  crimes solved; police legitimacy.
- *The test 🚦:* one force, one technology, 12 months, independent evaluator, with matched
  comparison areas without the cameras. Measured: wanted people found per alert, wrong matches per
  alert by group, and crime in the area versus comparison.
- *Fails if 🚦:* wrong matches differ by group beyond a pre-registered margin, or the arrests that
  lead to charge per thousand alerts fall below a pre-registered floor. Then it stops, in public.
- *The undo 🚦:* each warrant sunsets after three years unless renewed on its evaluation; the
  postmortem is published either way.
- *Who it leaves out:* private cameras in shops and stadiums feeding watchlists (a gap the law
  must close next; counted, not covered); predictive and "solvability" tools used inside police
  systems, which come next under the same warrant rule.
- *Who signs:* the Home Secretary lays each Act; the chief constable signs each trial; an officer
  signs each stop.
- *Status:* Draft (the force ban: Kept).

**Scale and cost.** Small: the evaluation is a fraction of the system's price (the national system
is about £26m [M]). The real cost is delay to roll-out, which we accept on purpose.

**Precedent.** *Bridges v South Wales Police* (2020) [K]; the EU AI Act's restriction of real-time
remote biometric identification in public to narrow cases [K]; Scotland's stated law-first
approach [M]; the Home Office's own visa "streaming" algorithm, withdrawn in 2020 after a legal
challenge over bias [K], the precedent for why the warrant must cover the Home Office itself.

**Strongest objection.** "If it catches a rapist, you're putting process before victims." **Answer:**
the test is how we find out whether it catches rapists or just catches people. If it works, the
trial proves it and every force gets it with the public behind it. If it doesn't, we have saved the
money and the wrong arrests. Common sense is a guess with good posture.

### F5. Report a crime, get a human

**The policy, one sentence.** Every reported crime, shoplifting and fraud included, gets a named
officer and a human answer within a fixed time, no automated screening tool may close a case
without a human signing it, and we publish how many reports in each area got one.

**Why now.** The brief's instinct: "on crime, ask what was cut before what was done". Prisons are
full: the Sentencing Act 2026 (Royal Assent January) moved many standard-sentence prisoners to
release at a third of their sentence from September, the government narrowed it after the PC
Harper row, and demand is projected to outrun places [M, several sources, details conflicting]. The
system at the back is full because the front stopped working, and the front is where the reader
meets it: the shoplifting nobody comes for. The crime news beat was empty in today's digest (MACH
§0), so the evidence sentence here needs sourced crime items before publication.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Row 3, the right to a human, in policing; tenet 3's published measure.
It also turns the reader's sense that "the police never come" into a number somebody answers for.

**The contract.**
- *The wrong:* a corner-shop owner who reported the same man four times and got four emails.
- *Who decided:* forces screening out crimes as unsolvable to manage demand, after the neighbourhood
  policing that would have known the man was cut.
- *The rule:* (a) every report gets a named officer and a human reply within a set time; (b) no
  automated solvability or triage tool closes a case on its own; a human signs and the victim is
  told why; (c) published monthly by area: reports, replies within time, attendance, outcomes.
- *The ledger:* pays: police time, so it needs officer hours moved from somewhere or added; the
  cost is real and is the Chancellor's line to book. Gains: victims, small shops, and the trust
  of people who stopped reporting.
- *The test 🚦:* two forces that volunteer, against matched forces, 18 months. Measured: share of
  victims who would report again (victim survey), repeat victimisation, and charge rate for
  volume crime.
- *Fails if 🚦:* victim confidence and charge rates don't beat the matched forces within 18 months
  by a pre-registered margin, or the target is met with form replies (audited sample).
- *The undo 🚦:* stops at 18 months unless the data says keep; postmortem published.
- *Who it leaves out:* the crime nobody reports at all (domestic abuse is the largest; counted by
  survey, separately, with its own measure); places already without a local station.
- *Who signs:* Home Secretary; each chief constable for their force.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** Unknown until the officer hours involved are sourced; this is the most
expensive flagship and the one that needs the Chancellor. If the pilot shows the hours are not
there, we say so rather than promise officer numbers.

**Precedent.** Police chiefs' 2023 commitment to attend every home burglary [K], a pledge with a
measure, which is the shape we want; the What Works Centre for Crime Reduction at the College of
Policing as the evaluator [K].

**Strongest objection.** "You can't promise a human for every crime with the police you've got."
**Answer:** correct, which is why it starts in two forces with a kill number. If it can't be done,
the postmortem tells you what it would take, and that is the bill the next Budget has to face in
public instead of hiding in an inbox.

### The standing method behind all five

**The real size, every month.** A short monthly note on the Home page's subjects (crossings,
asylum decisions, crime by type, fraud): the number, its trend, the cost at the bottom, and what
dwarfs it, from sourced figures only. Not a flagship (transparency is not policy, the spine's
charge against the Chancellor's page), but it is how F1–F5 report, and it is what fills the page on
a quiet day. **Prisons** sit under this method rather than as a flagship: every sentencing and
release change ships with its reoffending test, published by area. Scared Straight is the house
example of why (nine trials, more reoffending; D5).

## 3. What the Home Secretary will NOT promise, and why

1. **A date the boats stop.** Nobody controls the Channel's weather or France's beaches. A promise
   with no lever is the bad branch's favourite kind. We promise the decision time on claims and
   the published number, monthly.
2. **A net migration target picked from the air.** "Tens of thousands" was missed for a decade and
   taught everyone the number is a leaflet. The number follows the roofs (F1).
3. **That immigration has no costs, or that immigrants "pay for themselves".** Both are false at the
   bottom (−5% at the 5th wage percentile; a low-wage arrival turns net-negative by their early
   40s, OBR 2024 [V in reader research]). We say the cost and its size.
4. **Mass deportation, or leaving a treaty, as the fix.** Neither builds a GP surgery. We will not
   promise a legal fight as a substitute for capacity. (Judgement call, labelled: if the review of
   asylum decision times shows a treaty is the binding constraint, we publish that finding and the
   change it implies. Condition stated in advance.)
5. **A headline number of police officers.** A recruitment drive that replaces leavers changes
   nothing a victim can feel. We promise the human reply (F5) and publish whether it happens.
6. **That a technology will catch criminals.** We promise to find out (F4).
7. **A ban on facial recognition.** We test it. The ban is on machines using force.
8. **A crime rate by a date, or prison places by a date.** We publish both monthly and say what
   moved them.
9. **"Tough sentences deter."** Some do, some don't; every change ships with its test.

These refusals are the credibility. A reader who has heard every promise in this list from someone
will notice a party that won't make them, and why.

## 4. The reader test

| Flagship | The trap it risks | How the wording avoids it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F1 migration vote + roofs | **Denial** (reads as "pro-immigration"), or **the dodge** ("you won't say"). Also restating a scare claim to knock it down. | Opens with the cost, stated as real ("a little off the lowest wages, real pressure on homes and GP lists"). Gives a straight answer: where the homes aren't built, the number falls. The village is the wronged party; the people arriving are not the villain; the decision-maker is "who decided, and who pays". Never quotes a scare figure. |
| F2 nobody undercuts you | **Punching down** if it reads as a crackdown on migrant workers; **amnesty** charge from the right. | The fine lands on the firm; the protected person is "a worker of any nationality". Pub line: *nobody gets hired to undercut you.* The firewall framed as taking the threat out of the boss's hand. |
| F3 scam refund | **Pupil** ("be careful online"), or blaming the victim. Leading with AI. | The pensioner is the hero; the carriers are named by act; "AI" appears once as the tool. No advice to the reader at all. |
| F4 machine on the beat | **Soft on crime**: this reader may like the camera. **Inevitability** framing. | We don't oppose it, we test it, and we keep it if it works; the objection answer is about catching rapists. "Common sense is a guess with good posture" is aimed at the procurement, not the reader. The only ban is on machines using force, which nobody in this readership wants. |
| F5 report a crime, get a human | **Promising what can't be delivered**; nostalgia kitsch ("bobby on the beat"). | Starts in two forces with a kill number; admits the cost is the most expensive in the chapter. The corner-shop owner, four emails: their own experience, flat, no cosplay. |

Lee/Coe test on the page's sharpest line ("the question is not about the people arriving; it is
about who decided, and who pays"): a reader who wants lower immigration reads it and finds their
complaint taken seriously and an answer that may lower it. Nobody's contempt for the migrant is
confirmed; nobody's contempt for the reader is either.

## 5. Interfaces

- **Housing (depends, clashes).** F1's capacity rule only works if Housing counts the roofs. Split:
  Home owns the number and the arrival; Housing owns the build and "shortage is a decision".
  Clash risk: both want the proportionality paragraph. Rule for the PM: migration pressure on
  homes is Housing's evidence and Home's decision.
- **Health and Education (depend).** GP places and school places in the pressure fund.
- **Chancellor (depends, clashes).** Books the pressure fund and F5's officer hours; the reimbursement

## 9. The biggest risk

**F1 reads as either a dodge or a door.** To the right-drifting reader, "migration gets its own
vote" can sound like "they won't say", and to everyone else a standing vote on numbers can look
like an invitation to bid them down. The whole Home page stands or falls on the capacity rule being
said plainly as an answer ("where the homes aren't built, the number falls") and on the cost at
the bottom being admitted in the second paragraph, not the fifth. If Kai and Jack are not
comfortable with a rule that will lower some routes, F1 should become a labelled judgement call
rather than be softened into a process; a softened version is the worst of both.
