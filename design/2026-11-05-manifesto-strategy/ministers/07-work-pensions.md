## 1. Mission

**The line** (92 characters, no colon):

> Before a firm automates your job, you get a say in the saving, and a human reads your claim.

It is spine §1's pub sentence for this post, shortened to fit under 100 characters. Two halves, one
idea: **a decision about you is made by a person you can find.** The first half is the employer's
decision. The second half is the state's.

**The problem this post exists to prevent.** This post owns two links in the bad-branch chain. They
are consecutive rungs of Act 1's ladder, and the post exists to stop the slide from one to the next:

- **F3 / F10, rung two (work): the saving goes up.** "Two hundred thousand office workers were
  made redundant today. The chief executive called it the hardest email he has ever asked an AI to
  write." The work is automated, the gain is booked by the owner, and the worker gets a letter.
  (Spine arc row 2.)
- **F4 / F5, rung three (the safety net): bots all the way up.** "The benefits line, the appeal, the
  human who used to be able to say *yes*. Now it's bots all the way up." Karen, thirty years in a
  job, is filed as noise by the system meant to catch her. (Spine arc row 3.)

The bad branch's lesson is that these are **one fall, not two.** The person automated out of work
lands straight in a benefits system that has also been automated, with nobody in it who can say
yes. Rung two pushes them and rung three fails to catch them. A party that fixes only one rung has
fixed nothing.

**Today's signs** (LAND):
- 16–24 unemployment about 16%; overall unemployment 5.1–5.2%, the highest since 2021; graduate
  postings at their lowest since 2020; BBC reporting graduates who mostly supervise AI agents [M].
  The cause (AI, trade or rates) is disputed (NIESR). **The government does not track AI
  displacement at all** (LAND §6.2).
- Netflix cutting about 1,000 jobs; Goldman restricting AI coding tools to engineers [M, single
  source].
- The UC health element for new claims cut to about £50 a week from April 2026. The PIP "4 points
  in one activity" rule is due from November 2026 **while the Timms review into it is still to
  report** [M, dates mixed]. This is LAND §6.4's "no undo button" gap in its plainest form.
- Claimants win about two in three PIP appeals that reach a tribunal (HMCTS, Jan–Mar 2026), and
  getting there takes about eleven months once the 79-day mandatory reconsideration is counted
  [M, checked today: Benefits and Work reporting HMCTS; the primary HMCTS table is still owed].
- Concern about the welfare algorithm rose from 44% to 59% in two years, the sharpest rise
  measured. **This is the reader's real machine fear** (GUARD §1).
- Bank Rate up to 4.00% today, CPI 3.1% in August and rising [H/M]: every shock lands on the
  bottom first.

## 2. The flagships

Five, in the order a reader feels them: count it, share it, catch them, check the cut, bridge the
gap. **Each stands alone** (spine §4 stage 0): a reader can take flagship 3 and reject flagship 2.

The design idea behind the set is **Future Proof's own order of work: measure first, test second,
roll out last.** Flagship 1 costs almost nothing and produces the data that every other flagship,
and every other party, is missing.

---

### Flagship 1: Count it (the automation box)

**The policy.** When a firm lays off twenty or more people, the redundancy form it already has to
send the government gains one question: is a machine or software now doing any of this work, and
what is the expected saving? The answers are published every quarter, by sector and region.

**Why now.** Nobody in government counts AI displacement (LAND §6.2). Youth unemployment is at a
decade high and its cause is disputed (LAND §2.4). Every party argues about AI and jobs from
anecdote: Netflix, Goldman, one BBC piece. The argument can't be settled because nobody measures
it.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 3, data-driven: no policy without its measurement. It is the
precondition for flagships 2 and 5, which cannot be tested without knowing which redundancies are
automation redundancies.

**The contract.**
- *Who decided.* The rule that employers report redundancies but not their cause. The HR1 form
  (advance notice of 20+ redundancies at one establishment within 90 days) is the existing pipe [K].
- *The rule.* Add an automation question and an expected-saving field to HR1; publish quarterly;
  share the data with Education (entry hiring) and the Chancellor (the ledger).
- *The test.* Run it for four quarters. Compare declared automation redundancies against ONS
  sector data and a sample audit of firms.
- *Fails if:* after four quarters, audit shows fewer than half of the automation redundancies were
  declared (the box is being ignored), or the form demonstrably delays redundancy processing.
  Then we publish the postmortem and either fix the question or drop it.
- *Undo.* A one-field change to a form. Sunset after three years unless the data is being used.
- *Ledger.* Pays: firms, a few minutes per form. Gains: everyone arguing about AI, who finally
  gets a number.
- *Leaves out:* layoffs under twenty; agency and gig workers (no redundancy at all); jobs that
  were never created (the young). Counted separately: the never-hired gap is Education's measure
  of entry hiring.
- *Who signs.* The minister; the Insolvency Service publishes.
- *Status.* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** Near zero: one question on an existing form plus a quarterly statistical
release. Low millions at most, including the audit sample [estimate, unsourced].

**Precedent.** The HR1 regime itself [K]; the US WARN Act notices, which researchers already mine
for layoff data [K]. Nobody we know of asks the automation question. That is the point.

**Strongest objection.** "Firms will lie or tick no." **Answer:** some will, and the audit measures
how many. A rough count beats the current count, which is zero. Flagships 2 and 5 give firms a
reason to tick the box honestly (the say and the bridge apply to declared cases, and undeclared
automation found on audit is treated as declared).

### Flagship 2: A say before the saving

**The policy.** Before an employer replaces jobs with automation, the workers affected elect their
own reps and get a binding say in how the saving is shared: shorter hours on the same pay, a share
of the profit, or paid retraining into a job that actually exists. The workers choose; the board
does not choose for them.

**Why now.** Canon's doorway question (X3), "who should decide how the gains from automation are
shared?", has no answer in any party's platform (LAND §3, §6.1). Labour promises "not leaving
workers behind" with no mechanism. Reform does not mention AI. This is spine arc row 2 and the
heart of the party.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 1 (composition: the workers pick from a menu, not take the
bundle), tenet 3 (trial before rollout), and X4. Spine tension E is settled the way the spine
leans: **"binding" lives inside the trial, and the trial decides whether it becomes law.** That
keeps the word honest.

**The contract.**
- *Who decided.* The board that books the saving. Today UK law requires only *consultation* before
  collective redundancies (45 days for 100+, 30 days for 20–99) [K]. Consultation is being told.
  It is not a say.
- *The rule.* In trial firms, an automation redundancy cannot proceed until an agreement on the
  split is signed by the elected reps, or 60 days pass and an independent arbiter (Acas-style)
  rules on the menu.
- *The test.* Volunteer employers in one region (and in AI Growth Zone firms, if the Chancellor
  makes it a condition of the public-power deal) for two years. Compared with similar firms that
  automated in the same period without it, found through flagship 1's data. Measured: the affected
  workers' earnings and employment at 12 and 24 months; firm investment and productivity.
- *Fails if:* at 24 months the affected workers are no better off in pay or work than the
  comparison group, **or** trial firms cut investment noticeably more than comparison firms
  (threshold pre-registered before the trial starts). Then we publish the postmortem and drop it.
- *Undo.* Sunset at three years. It stops unless the data says keep. Becoming law needs a second
  vote after the postmortem.
- *Ledger.* Pays: shareholders of the automating firm, who keep less of the first years' saving.
  Gains: the workers affected. Neutral to the Treasury, beyond the arbiter and the evaluation.
- *Leaves out:* agency, gig and self-employed workers, who have no table to sit at; firms that
  automate by never hiring (the young); small firms below the HR1 threshold. Counted separately,
  so nobody calls the gap a success.
- *Who signs.* The minister; the reps and the board sign each agreement.
- *Status.* Draft (it is X4's hypothesis, posted on purpose).

**Scale and cost.** Public cost small: the arbiter service and an independent evaluation, low tens
of millions over two years at most [estimate]. The real cost is a transfer from shareholders to
workers inside trial firms, so the size depends on the saving declared on flagship 1's box.

**Precedent.** Germany's works councils have legal rights over the introduction of new technology
and over a "social plan" for workers affected by major changes to the business (Works Constitution
Act) [K]. Germany has also automated more than the UK, not less (robot density is several times
the UK's, IFR) [K, needs a primary before use]. Also the 2023 Writers Guild contract, which put
terms on AI use into a bargained agreement [K].

**Strongest objection.** "It slows adoption, so firms automate abroad and Britain falls behind."
**Answer:** the strongest real-world case for co-determination is a country that automated faster
than we did. And it is a trial with a kill number on investment: if it drives investment away
relative to comparable firms, we say so and drop it. A firm that automates with its workers'
agreement keeps a workforce that doesn't sabotage the machine.

### Flagship 3: A human reads your claim

**The policy.** Every benefit decision that cuts, stops, sanctions or flags you names the human who
signed it, tells you whether a machine was used, and gets reviewed by a human within four weeks if
you ask.

**Why now.** The welfare algorithm is the machine fear the reader actually lives with (44% → 59%,
GUARD §1). Claimants win about two in three PIP appeals that reach a tribunal, after about eleven
months [M, checked today]. If a checker were wrong two times in three, a firm would sack the
checker. DWP already uses machine-learning risk models on parts of UC [K: DWP's own reporting on
the UC advances model; needs a primary].

**Future Proof mechanism.** Spine arc row 3 (the right to a human; the PM owns the cross-government
statute, W&P runs it first). X7: AI proposes, a named human decides. Per spine tension H, **the
right is Kept from day one and measured, not trialled.** What is trialled is the delivery: the
four-week human review.

**The contract.**
- *Who decided.* The department that procured scoring tools and put them in front of decisions
  about people, without a published test or a named human answerable for each stop.
- *The rule.* (a) No reduction, stop or sanction without a named human signatory. (b) Notice on
  every letter when an automated tool contributed. (c) A human review within 28 days, replacing a
  mandatory reconsideration that averages 79 days [M]. (d) Every model DWP uses on claims is
  published on the government's algorithmic transparency register, with its error rates by age,
  disability and nationality [K: the register exists].
- *The test.* The 28-day review runs first in a set of Jobcentre districts against matched
  districts on today's process. Measured: time to a correct decision, tribunal appeals lodged,
  tribunal overturn rate, wrongful stops, and fraud and error caught (so the trial proves it isn't
  soft).
- *Fails if:* after 12 months, trial districts catch measurably less fraud and error than
  comparison districts, **or** the overturn rate at tribunal does not fall. Then we publish the
  postmortem and change the delivery. The right itself stays.
- *Undo.* The delivery method sunsets at three years unless the data says keep. The right does
  not sunset.
- *Ledger.* Pays: the Treasury, for more decision-makers' time up front. Gains: claimants wrongly
  stopped, and the tribunal system, which today does DWP's checking for it, slowly. Direction: the
  appeal backlog of well over 100,000 [M] is itself a cost, so part of this pays for itself. We
  will not claim it all does until the trial says so.
- *Leaves out:* people who never claim because the system frightens them (counted through
  take-up estimates); people with no phone or internet (F26: invisible if not indexed), who get a
  paper and in-person route by right.
- *Who signs.* Every decision letter: a named DWP officer. The policy: the minister.
- *Status.* The right: Draft, to be Kept. The 28-day delivery: Draft, to be trialled.

**Scale and cost.** Hundreds of millions a year at most for extra decision-maker time if rolled out
nationally [estimate, unsourced]; offset by fewer tribunal hearings. The size of the offset is
what the trial measures.

**Precedent.** The warning cases are strong and well documented. Australia's Robodebt: automated
debt notices, a Royal Commission in 2023 that called the scheme unlawful [K, strong]. The
Netherlands' childcare benefits scandal: a risk model flagged families, and the government
resigned in 2021 [K, strong]. At home: the Post Office's Horizon system, where hundreds of people
were prosecuted on a computer's word [K; FP-A §5 says "hundreds", never a number, and never a joke
at the victims]. The positive precedent: GDPR Article 22's right not to be subject to a solely
automated decision, which the UK is loosening, not tightening [K, check the current status of the
Data (Use and Access) Act].

**Strongest objection.** "This is soft on benefit fraud, and it costs money." **Answer:** the
fraud check stays; only the stop needs a signature. The trial's own kill number is less fraud
caught, so if we're soft, the test says so in public. And the current system is already
expensive: it pays tribunals to overturn two in three of the decisions they see.

### Flagship 4: No cut before the check

**The policy.** No change that lowers anyone's benefit income starts before the review into it has
reported, and every cut and every sanction rule is tested against its own stated aim, with a
sunset.

**Why now.** The PIP four-point rule is due before the Timms review reports, and the UC health
element for new claims has already been cut to about £50 a week [M]. LAND §6.4 names this as the
plainest "no undo" gap in British politics today.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 2 (small changes, an undo, a postmortem) and tenet 3. It is also
the **neutral test** spine §2.2.3 requires: here is a policy the reader expects us to oppose, and
**we test it instead of condemning it.** If the government's cut does what it says (more people in
work without hardship), the test keeps it. This is the plank that makes "tested" believable to
someone who distrusts everyone, us included.

**The contract.**
- *Who decided.* The department and the Treasury, which booked the savings before the review was
  done. The review exists; the order of work is the decision.
- *The rule.* (a) Pause the PIP four-point rule until the Timms review reports and the department
  has published its response. (b) The UC health element change gets a pre-registered test against
  its own stated aim: employment at 12 and 24 months, against hardship measures (rent arrears, food
  bank referrals, emergency hospital admissions). (c) Every sanction rule goes through the same
  form: sanctions are neither abolished on a hunch nor kept on one.
- *The test.* For (b): compare new claimants on the lower rate with the last cohort on the old rate
  and, where possible, the devolved nations or regions with different take-up. For (c): a sample
  of districts runs a warning-first sanction regime against the current one.
- *Fails if:* the cut does not raise employment by the amount the department itself claimed, **or**
  hardship measures rise beyond a threshold set before the data comes in. Then the cut stops and
  the old rate returns, with a postmortem.
- *Undo.* Every welfare reduction carries a two-year sunset. It stops unless the data says keep.
- *Ledger.* Pays: the Treasury forgoes the saving it booked during the pause (a real cost, named).
  Gains: disabled and sick claimants during the pause; and the Treasury, if a cut that doesn't work
  is stopped before it pushes costs onto the NHS and councils.
- *Leaves out:* people already reassessed under the new rule before the pause, who get a review
  on request; carers, whose Carer's Allowance cliff edge is a separate problem (see §5, Health).
- *Who signs.* The minister. Each test is published in the PM's Test Register (spine §4 stage 1).
- *Status.* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** The cost of the pause is whatever saving the department booked for those
months; we owe the figure from the Budget documents before any public number [L, not found].
The test itself is cheap: it uses data DWP already holds.

**Precedent.** Universal Credit was "badly piloted" by the department's own auditors' account
(NAO, 2018) [K; FP-A wording: never "unpiloted"]. *Test, Learn, Adapt* (Cabinet Office, 2012)
already recommends exactly this [K]. On sanctions, the Welfare Conditionality project (2018)
found them largely ineffective at moving people into lasting work [K, needs the primary], and the
NAO (2016) found the department had not evaluated their effect [K].

**Strongest objection.** "This blocks every welfare reform forever, and welfare must be got under
control." **Answer:** it blocks nothing; it changes the order. Check first, cut second. A cut that
works passes its test and stays, with our name on the result. Only a cut that fails gets undone.

### Flagship 5: The bridge (you paid in)

**The policy.** If you lose your job to automation, after at least two years paying National
Insurance, you get up to a year of earnings-related support (a share of your old pay, capped) on
top of the flat rate, paid partly from the employer's declared saving.

**Why now.** The fall from a wage to New Style Jobseeker's Allowance (a flat rate of about £90 a
week for six months [K]) is the cliff the bad branch's office workers went over. Spine arc rows 2
and 5: the saving goes up, and the growth reaches nobody. Interest rates rose today; mortgages and
rents don't wait for a new job.

**Future Proof mechanism.** Tenet 3 (trial before rollout). It is also the plank that answers
the ownership question in the reader's moral vocabulary: **desert and contribution**, not a
handout. You paid in; you get back something that looks like what you paid for.

**The contract.**
- *Who decided.* The rule, set in the 1980s, that ended earnings-related unemployment benefit and
  left a flat rate that most working people would not recognise as insurance [K: the UK
  Earnings-Related Supplement ran 1966–1982].
- *The rule.* For redundancies declared on flagship 1's box (or found on audit): a top-up to 60%
  of previous earnings, capped near median pay, for up to 12 months, conditional on job search or
  training. Funded by an employer contribution set as a share of the saving declared, plus the NI
  Fund.
- *The test.* Two regions, for declared automation redundancies, against matched regions on
  today's flat rate, for three years.
- *Fails if:* people on the bridge take a new job markedly later than the comparison group (threshold
  pre-registered; the worry is that support delays the search) **without** getting better-paid or
  longer-lasting jobs at the end of it. Then we publish the postmortem and drop it.
- *Undo.* Sunset at three years; it stops unless the data says keep.
- *Ledger.* Pays: the automating employer (through the saving it declared) and the NI Fund.
  Gains: the worker whose job went, and their local high street, which keeps a customer.
  **Not** paid by raising income tax, employee NI or VAT (spine §5.4).
- *Leaves out:* people with under two years' contributions, most of them young; the self-employed;
  gig workers. Counted separately; the young are flagship 5's sister policy (the paid first job,
  §5).
- *Who signs.* The minister; the Chancellor books the ledger.
- *Status.* Draft.

**Scale and cost.** Unknown, and we say so: it depends on how many automation redundancies there
are, which nobody measures yet (flagship 1). As an illustration only, 10,000 workers a year with an
average top-up of £8,000 would cost about £80m a year before the employer contribution [arithmetic,
not a forecast]. The number is the reason flagship 1 comes first.

**Precedent.** Germany's unemployment insurance pays 60% of previous net pay (67% with children)
for up to a year for most workers [K]; Denmark's flexicurity pays a high replacement rate with
strong activation [K]. The UK's own Earnings-Related Supplement until 1982 [K]. The US Trade
Adjustment Assistance programme is the honest warning: mixed evaluation results, which is why
this one carries a kill number [K].

**Strongest objection.** "It pays people to stay unemployed." **Answer:** that is exactly the
fails-if. If the bridge slows the return to work without better jobs at the end, the test catches
it and it goes. Germany and Denmark run richer insurance than ours with higher employment [K,
check]. And the firm that made the saving pays part of the bill, which is the point.

### Secondary commitments (standing, short)

These sit under the flagships and give the cabinet somewhere to hang news that isn't about
automation.

- **The paid first job, tested again** (with Education). A six-month paid job for 18–24s out of work
  six months or more, in places where entry hiring has fallen. The last time this country tried
  it, the Future Jobs Fund (2009–11), the department's own 2012 analysis put the net benefit to
  society at about £7,750 per participant, and found participants more likely to be in
  unsubsidised work two years on. It had already been scrapped by the time the results came in
  [M, checked today via NIESR and secondary sources; the DWP primary is still owed; contested by
  some]. That is the bad branch in one programme: a test that passed and a decision that didn't
  wait for it. Credit the current Youth Guarantee [K] as a start, then add the pay and the test.
- **Your pension votes for you.** Workplace pension funds own shares in the firms that automate. A
  default fund should vote on automation deals in its members' interest and publish how it voted,
  with members able to instruct their vote on the big ones (pass-through voting) [K: exists at some
  US and UK providers]. This is the reader-safe ownership answer: *the workers already own a slice
  through their pensions; give them the vote that comes with it.* Not nationalisation.
- **The state pension: a judgement call, labelled.** We keep the triple lock this parliament.
  Review date: before the next state pension age rise. The condition that would change our mind is
  published in advance: if the distributional ledger shows it mostly helps the best-off pensioners.
  Before any rise in the state pension age, healthy life expectancy is published region by region
  [K: it is far below 67 in the poorest areas; needs the ONS primary].
- **No cliff edges.** Wherever £1 more in earnings can cost you the whole benefit (Carer's Allowance
  is the known case [K]), replace the cliff with a taper, tested first. Shared with Health, which
  counts the unpaid carer.

## 3. What this minister will NOT promise

Credibility comes from refusals. Each of these is one a rival would make.

1. **No universal basic income promise.** Finland and Stockton are not proof that UBI works (FP-A
   §5). Wales is already running a basic income pilot for care leavers [K, evaluation pending];
   we read its result before we promise anything. We test floors; we don't announce them.
2. **No ban on automation and no robot tax.** The evidence is thin (Korea's reduced tax credit is
   the only real case, LAND §5). Automation that shares the saving is welcome. Our quarrel is with
   who keeps the saving, not with the machine.
3. **No "no job will be lost".** Some will be. We promise a say, a count and a bridge, not a freeze.
4. **No job guaranteed to everyone straight away** (GUARD §5: skills, geography, health and
   matching are real). The paid first job is targeted and tested.
5. **No claim that AI is causing mass layoffs now.** The aggregate evidence doesn't support it
   (GUARD §4). The defensible claim is the narrow one, and our answer is to count it.
6. **No denial of benefit fraud and no fraud-savings figure.** Fraud is real and the check stays.
   We never call it a myth, and we never put a number on what our rules save until the trial does.
7. **No reversing every welfare cut on day one.** That would be inheriting a bundle the other way
   round. Each one goes through the test, and some may pass.
8. **No promise to stop firms moving abroad.** We can't, and saying so is the honest version of
   the investment kill number in flagship 2.
9. **No costing we can't source.** We pledge the test and the kill number, not a figure (spine §5).

## 4. The reader test

The reader: working class, a town that has seen better times, drifting right; immigration first,
the economy second; machine fear = being judged and defrauded (GUARD §1).

| Flagship | Trap it risks | How the wording avoids it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Count it | Lecture and red tape ("more forms"); unaimed automation fear | Frame it as a receipt, not a regulation: "the form a firm already sends the government asks one more question". The chooser is a board on a weekday. Never "AI is taking jobs"; we are counting because nobody else will. |
| 2. A say before the saving | Reads as union-boss politics or anti-business; Lee/Coe: confirms contempt for "the bosses" | Concede first: the machine may be a good idea. The quarrel is about the split. "They choose. The board doesn't choose for them" is fair-reward-for-work language, not class war. Name the board by act, never hate a person. Never say "workers' control". |
| 3. A human reads your claim | Soft on scroungers; punching down by casting claimants as victims | Lead with fraud being caught ("the check stays; only the stop needs a signature"). Its kill number is less fraud caught. The claimant is the wronged party, not a pupil, and could be the reader's mum. Robodebt and Horizon are told about the system, never as jokes at victims. |
| 4. No cut before the check | "Defends welfare" reads as the left protecting the workshy | It is the neutral test: we test the cut on its own aim and keep it if it works. That is "fair play" and "do your homework", not a defence of spending. Never "cuts" as a moral swearword; say "a change that lowers your income". |
| 5. The bridge | A handout; the left with a robot voice (spine tension C) | Contributory and desert-coded: "you paid in". Two years' NI to qualify. The firm that made the saving pays part. No tax rise on wages. The UK had it until 1982: this is getting back something taken, a country worth coming home to. |
| Secondary: first job | "Too many degrees" (Reform's frame) or "lazy youth" | Never attack degrees or the young. "The bottom rung was sawn off by a decision." The test passed once and was scrapped anyway: the broken promise is the state's. |
| All | Immigration: "the jobs went to migrants" | Not this page's argument. Don't restate it and don't deny it; Home owns proportionality. This page keeps naming the decision that moved the money. |

**The whole page passes the Lee/Coe test if** someone who thinks claimants are workshy, reading
only flagship 3, comes away thinking "fair: catch the cheats, but a person should sign it." It
fails if they come away thinking we called them heartless.

## 5. Interfaces

**Depends on:**
- **PM.** The right-to-a-human statute (spine §4 stage 1.4) is the PM's; W&P runs the first trial.
  The Test Register and sunsets-default-to-stop (stage 1.1, 1.3) are what flagship 4 hangs on.
- **Chancellor.** Books the ledger for flagships 2 and 5; sets the employer contribution; could make
  flagship 2 a condition of AI Growth Zone power deals (the public stake, spine §5.2). Later: if the
  dividend fund exists, it is a natural second funder of the bridge.
- **Education.** Owns the first rung (spine arc row 1) and the measure of entry hiring. W&P owns the
  money and Jobcentre side of the paid first job. Flagship 1's data feeds Education's measure.

**Clashes to settle (for the PM):**
- **Education vs W&P on the young.** Who leads the paid first job? Proposed split: Education owns
  the learning time and the training; W&P owns the pay, the Jobcentre and the guarantee. Same as the
  PM's 2026-11-05 settlement on graduates and AI agents.
- **Chancellor vs W&P on money.** The pause in flagship 4 forgoes a booked saving; the bridge costs
  money. The Chancellor's "every pound has an address" helps: name the payer, and say plainly that
  stopping a failing cut also saves the NHS and councils money.
- **Home vs W&P on fraud.** Benefit fraud sits between them. Proposed: Home owns organised fraud
  and scams aimed *at* claimants (spine arc row 6); W&P owns fraud and error inside the benefits
  system and keeps the "the check stays" line.
- **Health vs W&P on carers and the UC health element.** Health counts the unpaid carer; W&P pays
  them. The cliff-edge taper is joint. Hardship measures in flagship 4 (emergency admissions) need
  Health's data.
- **Housing.** The UC housing element and frozen Local Housing Allowance [K] are where rent meets the
  benefit. Housing owns rents; W&P owns the benefit. Flag for a later joint test.

## 6. Draft manifesto page

```markdown
---
title: Work and Pensions
line: Before a firm automates your job, you get a say in the saving, and a human reads your claim.
---
## Position

Nobody's job gets taken by weather. A board decides, on a weekday, and the saving goes somewhere. Nobody asks the forty people it was about.

Then the letter comes, and the next decision about you is made by a system too. Of the PIP decisions people take to a tribunal, the department loses most, and it takes the best part of a year to get there. That's a long wait to be told you were right.

Five rules.

One. When a firm lays off twenty or more people, the form it already sends the government asks whether a machine is now doing the work. It's a count, not a charge. No answer costs a firm a penny, so it stays honest. We publish it every quarter. Today nobody counts.

Two. Before an employer automates jobs away, the people whose jobs they are pick who speaks for them, and they choose how the saving is split: a shorter week on the same pay, a share of the profit, or paid training for a job that exists. It starts with employers who sign up.

Three. Every benefit decision that cuts, stops or sanctions you names the human who signed it, tells you if a machine was used, and gets a human review within four weeks. The fraud check stays. But nobody's money stops until a person has signed it.

Four. No benefit cut starts before the review into it reports. Every cut and every sanction rule is then tested on its own stated aim. If it gets people into work without hardship, it stays. If not, it stops.

Five. You paid in. Lose your job after two years of paying National Insurance, whatever the reason, and you get up to a year of support tied to what you earned, not just the flat rate. Firms making large redundancies pay into it, scaled to their size. Tested in two regions first.

The state pension. The triple lock stays this parliament. Before the pension age rises again, we publish how long people actually stay healthy, region by region, and Parliament votes on the rise alone.

The test. Rule two runs first with employers who sign up, in one region, for two years, against similar firms that automated without it. Fails if the affected workers aren't at least a twentieth better off in pay or hours, or the trial firms invest clearly less than the comparison. Then we publish the write-up and drop it. Passes, and we put it to Parliament as law.

Who it leaves out: agency, gig and self-employed workers, and the young person whose first job was never created. They're counted on their own line.

The board decides on a weekday. So can the forty people it was about.
```

```markdown
---
title: Work and Pensions
line: Before a firm automates your job, you get a say in the saving, and a human reads your claim.
---
## Position

Nobody's job gets taken by weather. A board decides, on a weekday, and the saving goes somewhere. Nobody asks the forty people it was about.

Then the letter comes, and the next decision about you is made by a system too. Two in three people who take a PIP decision to a tribunal win. It takes them about eleven months to get there. If a checker were wrong that often anywhere else, you'd sack the checker.

So, five rules. Each one stands on its own. Take the ones you like.

One. When a firm lays off twenty or more people, the form it already sends the government asks whether a machine is doing the work now, and what it saves. We publish the count every quarter. Today nobody counts.

Two. Before an employer automates jobs away, the people affected elect their own reps and get a binding say in where the saving goes. Shorter hours on the same pay, a share of the profit, or paid retraining for a job that exists. They choose.

Three. Every benefit decision that cuts, stops or sanctions you names the human who signed it, tells you if a machine was used, and gets a human review within four weeks. The fraud check stays. Only the stop needs a signature.

Four. No benefit cut starts before the review into it reports. Then every cut, and every sanction rule, is tested on its own stated aim. If it gets people into work without hardship, it stays. If not, it stops.

Five. You paid in. If your job goes to a machine, you get up to a year's bridge that looks like what you paid for, part-paid from the saving.

The test. Rule two runs first with volunteer employers in one region for two years, against similar firms that automated without it. Fails if the affected workers are no better off in pay or work. Then we publish the postmortem and drop it.

Who it leaves out: agency, gig and self-employed workers, and the young person whose first job was never created. They get counted separately, so nobody calls the gap a success.

That's a first draft, posted on purpose. Write a better rule.
```

## 8. The biggest risk

**Flagship 2 is the heart of the party and the weakest-tested idea in it.** Canon itself calls it
"a hypothesis rather than an adopted policy". To this reader "a binding say" can sound like union
bosses, and to a business reader like a tax on investment. The defence is the order of work: count
first (flagship 1), trial with volunteers, put a kill number on investment, and say out loud that it
might fail. If the cabinet ever drops that order and writes it as a promised law, the party's
central claim (tested, undoable) breaks on its own flagship. Second risk: **the fact gate.** Three
of this page's best facts (the PIP appeal rate, the Future Jobs Fund result, German robot density)
are secondary-sourced or background knowledge; each needs a primary URL before it goes public.
