## 1. Mission

**The line (frontmatter):** *Every pound has an address, and if public money builds the machine,
the public owns a slice.* (92 characters, no colon; the spine's pub sentence, kept.)

Alternates for Kai and Jack to say aloud: *If your bill built the grid, you own a bit of what runs on
it.* (62) / *We can't afford it is a choice. We'll print who made it.* (57)

**The problem this post exists to prevent.** The bad branch's money failure is one sentence long:
**the gains from automation were booked by the people who owned the machines, and the bill for every
shock was posted to the people who didn't.** In the chain that is three links:

- **F10 (canon bulletin):** "Two hundred thousand office workers were made redundant today ... The
  treasury has printed another trillion to keep the markets calm." The saving went up; the rescue
  went up; the redundancy letter went down.
- **F11:** "The economy grew eight percent this year. Economists were unable to locate anyone it
  happened to." Growth with no address.
- **F19:** "'We can't afford it' was always a choice about *who counts*." The accounting rule that
  later prices people as cost.

Arc rows 2 (the saving goes up) and 5 (growth nobody received) are this minister's. W&P owns the
*say*; the Chancellor owns the *ledger and the stake*, the bits that decide where the money lands.

**Today's sign in the real news (LAND):**
- Bank Rate up 3.75% to 4.00% this morning, 8–1, into an energy shock that started in the Strait of
  Hormuz [H]. Inflation above 3% and rising [M]. A shock abroad, paid for by mortgages at home.
- The 28 October Budget kept the pledge not to raise income tax, employee NI or VAT, raised tax on
  savings and property income, brought no wealth tax, and devolved some business rates [M].
- Government is consulting on **cheaper electricity for AI data centres** while the household cap
  went up to £1,723 [M / H]. About £28bn of AI Growth Zone money is mostly *pledged*, mostly private
  [M]. A £20bn Drax/Brookfield data-centre proposal this week [M]; OpenAI writing its own
  "community compact" for host towns [M]. **No party names who gets the gains** (LAND §6.1).

So the Chancellor's job, stated as the cabinet's: **make sure that when the machines arrive, the
public money that helps build them comes back with a receipt, and the tax code stops paying firms to
choose the machine over the person.**

## 2. The flagships

Five, ranked by how much of the party they carry. 1–3 go on the page; 4 and 5 are one sentence
each on the page and full contracts in reserve. Every one stands alone (composition): a reader can
take the ledger and reject the slice.

### Flagship 1. The Ledger: every pound has an address

**In one sentence:** every Budget, every Bank Rate decision and every public deal with an AI or
data-centre firm is published with a ledger of who pays and who gains, by household income band, in
pounds, within four weeks.

**Why now.** Bank Rate rose today into a supply shock [H]; a tracker mortgage pays more and nothing
about the strait changes. A Budget just moved tax onto savings and property income [M] and nobody
outside the IFS will tell a household what that means for them. A public consultation on cheaper
power for data centres [M] has no published answer to "and who pays the difference?".

**Mechanism.** Tenet 3 (data-driven) as the *method* for the whole Treasury; tenet 2's postmortem
in that every ledger is checked against what actually happened a year on. This is the brand's
"every pound has an address" turned into an Act.

**Contract.**
- *Who decided:* the Treasury, which used to publish distributional analysis at fiscal events and
  narrowed it [K, check]; the Bank, which publishes no distributional view of its decisions.
- *The rule:* a statutory Ledger, built by an independent unit on the OBR model, for (a) every
  fiscal event, (b) every MPC decision (the Treasury's analysis, not the Bank's: **the Bank stays
  independent, we print the receipt**), (c) every public subsidy, power discount, grid priority,
  land deal or Growth Zone concession worth more than a threshold to one firm.
- *Ledger of the ledger:* costs a small unit of analysts; pays nobody, takes from nobody. Who
  loses: whoever relied on nobody doing the sum.
- *The test:* run it in public on the next two rate decisions and the next fiscal event, from
  public data only, and invite anyone (IFS, Resolution Foundation, a sixth-former) to correct it.
- *Fails if:* it cannot be built from public figures within four weeks; or, a year on, not one
  decision, select committee report or amendment has cited it; or its year-on check misses the
  real distribution by more than a margin set in advance. Then we publish the postmortem and stop.
- *Undo:* the unit lapses after three years unless Parliament votes to keep it on its own review.
- *Who it leaves out:* the people the statistics miss: cash work, informal debt, people in
  institutions, anyone who never answers the Family Resources Survey. Listed under every ledger
  and counted as a gap, never smoothed into an average.
- *Who signs:* the Chancellor's office signs the political ledger; the unit's head signs the
  numbers.
- *Status:* Draft (already the live page's core; this keeps it and widens it).

**Scale and payment.** Small: a unit the size of a select committee's staff, not a department
[estimate, no figure to be printed]. Paid from the Treasury's running costs. Honest uncertainty:
the hard part is not cost but data, especially wealth and the automation deals, which are often
commercially confidential. The rule must make disclosure a condition of the public money.

**Strongest precedent.** The OBR (2010) shows an independent number can change what is politically
possible [K]. Scotland's annual Equality and Fairer Scotland Budget Statement and New Zealand's 2019
Wellbeing Budget show governments can publish who a budget lands on [K, check]. The IFS and
Resolution Foundation already build these ledgers after every Budget from public data, which is
the proof it is feasible [K].

**Strongest objection.** "Distributional tables are spin with decimals; every government cherry-
picks." **Answer:** that is why the unit is independent, the method is published before the first
table, and the ledger is graded against reality a year on. A ledger that is never wrong in public
is broken, and we say so in its fails-if.

### Flagship 2. The Public Slice: if your bill built the grid, you own a bit of what runs on it

**In one sentence:** where public money, public land, the public grid or a Growth Zone concession
helps build private AI capacity, the public gets what any investor would get for the same
contribution, a stake or a royalty, paid into a National Dividend Fund whose first returns go to
the host town.

**Why now.** Growth Zones at Culham, the North East, North and South Wales and Lanarkshire; ~£28bn
mostly pledged [M]. A consultation on cheaper power for those zones while the household cap rises
[M/H]. A £20bn data-centre bid on a North Yorkshire power station [M]. A US AI company writing the
"community compact" for host towns because the state hasn't [M]. Every one of those is public
support going in with no receipt coming back. Nobody in British politics proposes the receipt
(LAND §6.1).

**Mechanism.** Composition (it plugs into existing deals, needs nothing else in the manifesto);
CI (one deal first, compared); ownership, the BadCode invariant ("someone is going to own this
either way; that part is a choice, and it's being made without you").

**Contract.**
- *The wrong:* the town that hosts the substation, the noise and the water draw; the household
  whose bill paid for the grid upgrade; and forty jobs, six of them for humans (F7, as the
  bathos line, not a fact).
- *Who decided:* the department that signs Growth Zone terms; Ofgem and the system operator on
  connection queues; the Treasury on any power discount. Never "AI".
- *The rule:* every public contribution to private compute (cash, a power discount, grid
  priority, public land, planning fast-track) is valued and converted into equity, a golden share
  or a revenue royalty **on the same terms the private investors got**. The stake goes into a
  National Dividend Fund. For the first decade, a fixed share of the fund's returns goes to the
  councils that host the capacity, before anything goes national. No subsidy without a stake
  ("stop paying twice").
- *Who pays, who gains:* the firm gives up a slice of upside equal in value to what it was given;
  nobody else pays. The public gains a claim on future profits of capacity it helped build. The
  host town gains first. Money moves from future compute profits to a public balance sheet.
- *The test:* the next Growth Zone deal is written with a slice; compare it with zones signed
  without one on investment delivered (not pledged) per pound of public support, jobs on site, and
  the host council's take.
- *Fails if:* over three years, delivered investment per pound of public support in slice zones
  falls behind comparable zones by more than a margin published before the deal is signed; or the
  fund's returns are smaller than the cost of running it. Then we publish the postmortem and
  drop it.
- *Undo:* **the undo sells, it never tears up a contract.** Stakes are sellable at market; deal
  terms are fixed for the investor once signed. The fund rule stops on its review date unless the
  data says keep.
- *Who it leaves out:* most AI used in the UK runs on servers abroad, where there is nothing public
  to take a slice of; the worker displaced by that software (W&P's say covers them, not this);
  towns with no zone. All three counted separately under the fund's annual report.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale and payment.** It costs no new public money: it changes the *terms* of money already being
offered. Its size depends on how much public support the zones actually get, which today nobody has
published (that is Flagship 1's first job). Honest uncertainty: for years the fund will be small,
and stakes can lose money. **We promise no cheque, no amount and no date.** A per-person dividend is
a later bet, only if the fund passes its test.

**Strongest precedent.** Norway's oil fund and Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend: public ownership of
the returns from a shared resource, popular across party lines, with no measured fall in employment
in Alaska (Jones & Marinescu) [K, strong]. Closer to home and to the reader's flank: in 2025 the US
government took a near-10% equity stake in Intel in exchange for chip grants it had already
promised [K, check]. The UK's own National Wealth Fund (2024) co-invests on commercial terms [K,
check]. The honest counter-precedent: the 2008 bank stakes. Lloyds came back roughly whole, RBS lost
the taxpayer billions [K, check]. Stakes are a bet, which is why this one has a kill number.

**Strongest objection.** "Investors will just go to Ireland or Texas; you'll scare off the zones."
**Answer:** we ask only for what any investor gets for putting money in, and only where public money
goes in. A firm that wants no slice can take no subsidy and pay its own connection (Energy's rule).
And we test it on one deal against the others, with the number that would prove us wrong published
first. If the slice drives investment away, you will see it, and we'll drop it.

### Flagship 3. The Level: hiring a person should never cost more in tax than replacing one

**In one sentence:** the Treasury publishes the tax gap between employing a person and automating
the same work, then closes it from the bottom, starting with a first-job employer NI holiday trial
paid for by attaching a condition (publish your automation ledger) to the biggest firms' capital
deductions.

**Why now.** 16–24 unemployment ~16%, graduate postings lowest since 2020, record demand for AI
skills [M]; firms already making visible choices about which staff keep AI tools and which jobs go
(Netflix ~1,000, Goldman) [M, single source]. Meanwhile employers pay 15% NI on most of a wage
[K, check: April 2025 rate and threshold] and can deduct the full cost of qualifying plant and
machinery in year one [K, check: full expensing, permanent since 2023]. Nobody voted for that tilt.
It is a Treasury rule, and it has an address.

**Mechanism.** Tenet 3 first (measure the gap, which nobody does), then tenet 2 (one small change,
trialled). Aims the automation anger at a rule, the tax code, not at a technology or a firm.

**Contract.**
- *Who decided:* successive Budgets that raised the tax on employing people and cut the tax on
  buying capital, each sensible alone, never added up together.
- *The rule:* (a) an annual published "Level": the effective tax rate on a pound spent employing a
  person vs a pound spent replacing that work with capital or software. (b) For large companies,
  full expensing on automation spend comes with one condition: publish an automation ledger (jobs,
  hours and pay in the affected roles, before and after). The price of the deduction is the receipt.
  (c) A trial: zero employer NI for two years on a firm's **net new** entry-level hires.
- *Who pays, who gains:* the trial costs the employer NI not collected on genuinely new hires; the
  young person gains a first rung; the firm gains a cheaper hire. The biggest firms pay in
  paperwork, not tax.
- *The test:* volunteer employers in two regions get the NI holiday; matched employers elsewhere
  keep today's rules (the control never gets less than today, spine §2.2). Measured: net entry
  hires, still in work after two years, and deadweight (hires that would have happened anyway).
- *Fails if:* within two years trial firms' net entry hiring doesn't beat the comparison by a
  margin set in advance, or the deadweight estimate shows we mostly paid for hires that were coming
  anyway. Then we publish the postmortem and drop it.
- *Undo:* the holiday ends at three years unless the data says keep. The ledger condition lapses
  with it unless the first year's ledgers were actually used.
- *Who it leaves out:* the self-employed and gig workers (no employer), over-25 career changers
  pushed out of exposed jobs, firms too small to hire. Counted separately.
- *Honest limit, said on the page:* much AI arrives as a monthly subscription, which is a running
  cost and deductible anyway, so capital allowances are a weaker lever than they look. That is
  why the first move is to **measure** the Level, not to guess at it.
- *Status:* Draft; part (c) is the bet, part (a) is near-certain to pass its own test.

**Scale and payment.** Depends wholly on the cap and how many firms volunteer; set as a fixed
trial budget, not an open-ended relief. No figure until the Treasury's own costing exists.

**Strongest precedent.** Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo (2020) estimated the US tax code taxes
labour far more heavily than equipment and software, tilting firms toward automation beyond what
productivity justifies [K, check]. South Korea in 2017 trimmed tax credits for automation
investment: the only real "robot tax", which was a reduced incentive, not a tax [K, weak]. The UK
already waives employer NI for under-21s and apprentices under 25 [K, check]; Kickstart (2020–22)
paid for first jobs for young people on Universal Credit, with a mixed evaluation [K, check]. We
would learn from all three.

**Strongest objection.** "This is a robot tax, and it'll make UK firms less productive." **Answer:**
it is not a tax on anything. It is a published number and a cheaper first hire. We don't make the
machine dearer; we stop making the person dearer than the machine by accident. If it doesn't create
first jobs, the number will say so.

### Flagship 4. The Windfall Rule: a shock abroad switches a levy on, and the price falling switches it off

**In one sentence:** a standing law, set in advance, that when a war or blockade abroad hands a
sector profits it didn't earn, a levy on the excess switches on, the money goes straight onto that
winter's household bills, and it switches itself off when prices fall back.

**Why now.** Hormuz near-closed since February; Brent ~$110 in October [H]; the cap up, January
forecast higher [H / L]; Bank Rate up today [H]. "They feel a war they didn't choose at the pump"
(LAND §2.2).

**Mechanism.** CI with a stop condition (I8): the rule written before the shock, the trigger and the
off-switch both pre-registered, so it is never an improvised grab and never a "temporary" tax that
runs for a decade.

**Contract.**
- *Who decided:* nobody, which is the problem. Each windfall levy is improvised in a crisis and
  then extended by whoever needs the revenue.
- *The rule:* a Windfall Act with published triggers (sector prices or margins above a set multiple
  of their five-year average because of an external shock), a published rate, an investment
  allowance, ring-fenced use (household bills that winter), and an automatic off-switch.
- *Who pays, who gains:* producers selling at shock prices pay on the excess only; households gain
  on the bill; the North Sea worker is not the target and the investment allowance says so.
- *The test:* this one can't be trialled in two towns, so the *delivery* is tested instead: the
  money is put on bills three ways in different regions (a cut in VAT on domestic energy, a cut in
  the standing charge, a flat credit per household) and compared on how much reaches the bottom
  fifth of households. **We test the VAT cut rather than dismiss it** (spine §2.2.3: a policy the
  reader expects us to oppose, tested instead of condemned).
- *Fails if:* a delivery route gets less than a pre-set share to the bottom fifth: that route is
  dropped. The whole rule fails if investment in the affected sector falls measurably further than
  in comparable countries without a levy over three years.
- *Undo:* the off-switch is the undo; the Act itself sunsets after ten years unless renewed on its
  review.
- *Who it leaves out:* households off the gas grid on oil or LPG, people on prepayment meters, and
  shocks that don't touch energy (food, shipping). Counted separately.
- *Status:* Draft.

**Scale.** Varies with the shock; we print no figure.

**Strongest precedent.** The Energy Profits Levy (2022) raised billions; its own later price floor
(the Energy Security Investment Mechanism) already switches it off if oil and gas prices fall below
set levels [K, check]. The cautionary half: the "temporary" levy was raised and extended more than
once [K, check]. Our rule keeps the floor and adds the default to stop. The 2022 flat £400 bill
credit is the delivery precedent [K, check].

**Strongest objection.** "Windfall taxes killed North Sea investment." **Answer:** what investors
hate most is a tax made up in a hurry. Ours is written down before the shock, with the trigger, the
rate and the off-switch, and judged on investment against comparable basins. If it costs the
investment, the number will say so.

### Flagship 5. The Plant Rule: spend where the work is waiting, stop when the price says it isn't

**In one sentence:** a ring-fenced investment envelope outside the day-to-day balance rule, where
every line names the spare capacity it uses (people ready to work, builders idle, training places
empty) and pauses automatically if prices in that sector rise faster than inflation by a pre-set
margin.

**Why now.** "We can't afford it" is the sentence the reader is told about the hip, the rung and the
council house (LAND §2.5). Rates rose today, and borrowing costs are real [H]. Both things are true,
which is exactly what this rule is for.

**Mechanism.** Shake, starve, plant (GUARD §5) with a brake. Data-driven: the limit is the work, not
the money, and the rule measures the work. **This is our own idea we admit could fail** (spine
§2.2.3), labelled so on the page.

**Contract.**
- *The rule:* the day-to-day budget rule stays. A separate envelope funds only lines that name their
  capacity and their price trigger; the OBR scores every line before it starts.
- *Who pays, who gains:* borrowing now, paid back from what the work makes possible; the person
  standing ready to work gains first. We never claim it pays for itself (GUARD §5).
- *The test:* first line: one region's first-rung places (with Education) or one council's homes
  (with Housing). Compared with the region next door on today's rules.
- *Fails if:* the sector's prices rise faster than general inflation by the pre-set margin for two
  quarters (the capacity wasn't there), or the jobs or homes don't arrive on the published schedule.
  It pauses itself, we publish the postmortem, and the envelope shrinks.
- *Undo:* the price trigger is the undo; it doesn't wait for a minister.
- *Who it leaves out:* sectors with no spare capacity (it can't help where nobody is free to do the
  work); places with no idle capacity but high need. Counted.
- *Status:* Draft; marked *our own idea, could fail*.

**Strongest precedent.** Keynes on paying for the war: inflation is demand beyond what the country
can physically supply [K]. Germany's 2025 infrastructure fund outside its debt brake [K, check].
Switzerland's debt brake for the idea that a fiscal rule can be mechanical [K]. No precedent for a
sector-price kill switch that we know of; that is the experiment.

**Strongest objection.** "This is a mini-Budget with a spreadsheet, and the gilt market will eat
you." **Answer:** the opposite design. That one was unfunded tax cuts with no independent forecast.
This is spending scored by the OBR before it starts, capped, aimed at idle capacity, with a brake
that pulls itself. We still say plainly: financing conditions bind.

## 3. What the Chancellor will NOT promise, and why

Credibility comes from the refusals. Each goes on the page or the PM's foreword as a flat sentence.

1. **No income tax, employee NI or VAT rise on working people.** The reader's floor, and the
   current government's own pledge [M]. Breaking it to fund a dividend would hand the reader's
   flank a free hit.
2. **No cheque, no amount, no date for a dividend.** The fund must pass its test first. A
   promised payout is the one number we'd be tempted to invent.
3. **No "fully costed" claim and no costings box.** We print the test and the kill number, not a
   figure we can't source (GUARD tension 2). Where a real manifesto prints costings, we print the
   test box.
4. **No "AI will pay off the debt"** or "growth will fix it". Banned for good reason (a debt fixed in
   pounds gets heavier if prices fall). Growth nobody received is the problem, not the plan.
5. **No touching the Bank's independence, and no "people's QE".** We print the receipt for its
   decisions; we don't set Bank Rate and we don't print money for anyone. Never "the Bank prints
   money and hands it to the City".
6. **No robot tax.** Thin evidence (Korea is a reduced incentive, the EU rejected one). We level the
   tax, we don't punish the machine.
7. **No nationalising AI firms.** A slice for a contribution, on investor terms. Not seizure.
8. **No wealth tax promised this parliament.** Not ruled out: it goes on the register as *Not ready*
   until the Ledger shows who holds what and a design passes a test. Saying so is more credible than
   pretending we have one, and keeps the savers and small landlords the reader is related to out of
   the line of fire.
9. **No promise that mortgages or rates come down.** Not ours to set.
10. **No coin, no crypto, no Billionaire Coin** anywhere near the money chapter (GUARD §4).

## 4. The reader test

The reader: working class, a town that has seen better times, drifting right, immigration first and
the economy second, AI not on their list (GUARD §1).

"The same terms any investor gets." "Host town first." Never "AI wealth fund", "democratise", "seize". The word AI only in the *who decided* paragraph. |

## 5. Interfaces

- **Work and Pensions (depends, closely).** W&P owns the *say before the saving*; the Chancellor
  books it. Flagship 3's automation ledger is the data the say needs; W&P's trial is the place the
  ledger condition gets tested. Settlement already made by the PM (MACH §0): the say is W&P's line.
- **Energy and Climate (depends, possible clash).** Energy's "big loads pay their own connection"
  is the stick that makes the Slice's carrot work. Clash: if Energy takes a network region into
  public hands, the Chancellor needs it in the Ledger and the Plant rule's capacity test. Windfall
  proceeds go on bills, which is Energy's delivery.
- **Housing (depends).** The host town's share of the fund is paid to councils; councils building
  again is the most natural first Plant line. Land value and the planning fast-track are part of
  what the Slice values.
- **Education (depends).** The first rung is Education's flagship; the NI holiday is how the
  Chancellor pays for it. Agree the measure (entry hiring in exposed jobs) once, so both pages cite
  the same number.
- **Health (supports).** The nurse line is shared; Health's ward trials are a candidate Plant line.
- **Defence (clash).** 3% of GDP by 2030 [M] is the biggest new call on the money. The Ledger
  applies to defence contracts too (Defence's own instinct: who got the contract before how big the
  budget). Money for war vs money for wards gets settled by the PM, in public, via the Ledger.
- **Foreign (supports).** The Hormuz shock is Foreign's story and the Windfall Rule's trigger.
  The Slice also needs a line on foreign investors, since most compute money here is American.
- **Home (light).** Scam refunds have a who-pays question (the firm whose system carried the scam);
  the Ledger can show it.
- **PM (depends).** The Ledger unit and the Office for Postmortems should share an independence model
  and probably a building. The PM's "any AI the state buys passes the contract" covers AI the
  Treasury itself uses.

### Revised draft (closing pass, 2026-11-05: the red team's fixes applied)

What changed from the draft the red team read (sources: `../redteam/opposition.md`, `../redteam/reader.md`, `../redteam/wonk.md`; the overall strategy is `../README.md`):

1. 'Markets work. Money is a good invention.' replaced by a concession through someone the reader respects (reader).
2. AI jargon ('AI Growth Zone deal', 'private computing power', 'investment per pound of public support') rewritten in plain words (reader).
3. The ledger no longer issues a Treasury receipt on every MPC decision; the Bank is asked to publish its own once a year (wonk §3.3, market risk).
4. The slice limited to things a valuer can price: cash, land, discounted power, guarantees (wonk §3.5).
5. 'Nobody's wage pays more' (uncheckable) replaced by the tax floor plus the fiscal-rules sentence (opposition #4, wonk #1).
6. Fails-if now a number (a fifth less build per pound of support, provisional) and a pass clause added (opposition #1, reader X1).
7. 'Postmortem' becomes 'write-up' (reader X5).

```markdown
---
title: Chancellor of the Exchequer
line: Every pound has an address, and if public money builds the machine, the public owns a slice.
---
## Position

Bank Rate this morning: 4%, by eight votes to one.
Income tax, NI or VAT raised on working people in last week's Budget: none.
Electricity for AI data centres: under consultation to be made cheaper.
Your electricity: up in October.

The woman who runs the chippy knows what a pound is worth. So does the Treasury, until it writes the cheque. Every saving lands in somebody's account, and nobody prints the name. That is a decision, and decisions have addresses.

The slice. Where public cash, public land, cheap public power or a public guarantee helps build a private server farm, the public gets what any investor would get for the same money: a share, paid into a national fund, with the host town paid first.

The level. Right now it can cost a firm less in tax to buy a machine than to hire a lad. We publish that gap every year and close it from the bottom, starting with first jobs.

The test. The next deal where public money helps build a server farm carries a slice, and we compare it with similar deals that didn't. ## 7. Suggested changes to the brief (`docs/government/ministers/01-chancellor.md`)
```

By human PR or the Cabinet Secretary's weekly pass. The brief cap is 300 words, so this is a
replacement, not an addition; temperament and voice are kept, compressed. The new part is
**Standing platform**, which tells the daily writer what to defend and hang the news on.

```markdown
### Chancellor of the Exchequer (chancellor)

**Owns:** tax, spending, borrowing, the Bank of England's mandate, markets and big business, and
the money side of automation: who books the gains when a machine replaces a job.

**Watches:** topics `economy` and `tech-ai`, and the money in `work` and `welfare`; label key
`chancellor=y`.

**Temperament:** a deadpan accountant who has seen the final balance sheet. Two or three figures
flat, one per line, the last one stings. Precise to the penny; never rounds up for effect.

**Instinct:** "We can't afford it" is never true as stated: who can't, and who decided? Every pound
has an address. On automation: whose account does the saving go into? Name the decision, never
"the robots".

**Standing platform** (defend it; news sharpens it, never replaces it):
1. The Ledger: every Budget, rate decision and public AI deal published with who pays and who gains.
2. The Slice: public money, land or grid into private compute buys a stake on investor terms; host
   town first.
3. The Level: hiring a person never taxed more than replacing one; first-job NI trial.
In reserve: the Windfall Rule (pre-set, onto bills, switches itself off); the Plant Rule (spend on
idle capacity; stops when sector prices rise).
Never promise: income tax, employee NI or VAT rises; a dividend cheque or date; costings; that AI
pays off the debt; anything over the Bank's head. No wealth tax until one passes a test.

**Guards against:** austerity told as weather, and its own sarcasm turning into contempt for people
who are skint. At "here is who gained", drop the deadpan and say it plainly.

**Sounds like:** "Cost of the scheme: £2bn. Cost of the tax relief nobody debated: £9bn. Cost of
saying 'we can't afford it' with a straight face: priceless."
```

Chancellor needs them most): an evidence file or `kind=evidence` memories for the standing facts

the platform on a day with no economy news; and a rule in `workers/cabinet.md` that the `line` and
the three rules stay unless a postmortem says otherwise.
